Chapter 65. Private Equity, Venture Capital and Hedge Funds

Chapter 65 of 85 · about 20 minutes

Behind the public market's glass wall runs a private one: companies that never list, funds that never advertise, and returns quoted in letters, IRR, DPI, TVPI, designed to keep the lay reader out. This chapter lets you in: how private markets really work, what their celebrated returns look like after their celebrated fees, and which doors, if any, an ordinary investor should walk through.

65.1How do private markets differ from public ones?

Four structural differences drive everything else. Pricing: a private company's value is an appraisal, updated at funding rounds and audits, not a market quote updated every second, so its reported smoothness is partly an artifact, volatility you cannot see is not volatility that is absent. Liquidity: you commit capital for a decade, drawn down over years and returned on the fund's schedule, not yours. Information: disclosure is negotiated, not mandated; the fund knows far more than its investors, and both know far less than the founders. And access: returns in private markets are violently skewed between managers, the gap between a top-quartile and median venture fund is many times the equivalent gap in public equities, so "investing in private equity" is not one decision but a bet on which room you were allowed into. The advertised premium for all this is the illiquidity premium, extra return for locking your money away; it is real on average, and the average hides everything that matters.

65.2How does venture capital work: power laws and the startup funding ladder?

Venture capital is the finance of the power law. A fund writes twenty cheques expecting most to return little, a few to return something, and one or two to return the entire fund, because startup outcomes follow not a bell curve but a cliff: the median startup dies, and the winners are unbounded. The funding ladder prices that journey: seed money buys the team and the idea; Series A buys evidence of product-market fit; B and C buy scale; late rounds buy proximity to the exit, each rung repricing the company upward as risk drains out, and each new investor standing ahead of the last in the liquidation preference stack that decides who is paid first when things go merely okay. The instruments matter as much as the valuations: a ₹500 crore valuation with a 2x participating preference is not the same price as a clean ₹500 crore, a truth employees discover at exit and readers of funding headlines never do. India's ecosystem, thousands of startups, hundreds of funds, a real exit market through IPOs, now plays this game at global scale, with global lessons attached.

65.3How does private equity work: buyouts, leverage and exits?

Growth capital aside, the classic PE machine is the buyout: acquire a whole company, usually with borrowed money loaded onto the company itself, improve its operations, margins, management, pricing, and sell it three to seven years later. Each element deserves scrutiny. The leverage magnifies returns both ways, which is why buyout funds look brilliant in credit booms and discover religion in busts. The "improvement" is real in the best shops and financial engineering in the worst, cut costs, add debt, sell high. And the exit determines everything: the same company sold to another fund, listed on an exchange, or sold to a strategic buyer at three different prices. In India, PE has graduated from minority growth cheques into genuine control deals, conglomerate carve-outs, and the exits have matured with them. The investor's reading of any PE return claim: separate what the market gave (multiple expansion and leverage) from what the manager made (the company genuinely improved), because only the second is repeatable.

65.4What are hedge funds: strategies, 2-and-20 fees and who should invest?

A hedge fund is not an asset class but a compensation structure with strategies attached: long-short equity, global macro, event-driven, relative value, quant, multi-strategy, the common thread being freedom, to short, to leverage, to go anywhere, and the famous fee: 2% of assets annually plus 20% of profits. Run the arithmetic before admiring any track record: at a 15% gross year, 2-and-20 leaves the investor about 10.4%, below a dull index's net 11.8% in an ordinary year, and the gap compounds into a canyon over a decade. The industry's own long-term scoreboards have repeatedly shown the average hedge fund trailing a cheap equity index after fees, with the famous exceptions genuinely famous because they are exceptions. Who should invest? Institutions with the scale to demand fee concessions, the staff to select managers, and the liabilities that need uncorrelated returns. For everyone else, the honest answer is that the strategy you can actually buy is a mutual fund or a Category III AIF with hedge-like freedom and Indian regulation, and that the dream being sold, equity returns without equity risk, has a price list, and the first item on it is your return.

65.5How can ordinary investors access private markets?

India has built regulated on-ramps, and knowing their shapes matters more than their marketing. Category I and II AIFs, venture, growth and PE funds, take a minimum ₹1 crore per investor, pooling you into the private market with a SEBI-registered manager, a decade-long lock, and manager-selection risk doing most of the work. Category III AIFs run hedge-fund-style strategies with the same minimum and more frequent liquidity windows. Listed proxies shrink the ticket: PE and VC-backed listed companies, and abroad, listed private-equity managers themselves, give public-market prices for private-market exposure, with public-market volatility attached. And the employee route, ESOPs in a startup, is the private-market position most Indians will actually hold: illiquid, concentrated, employer-correlated, and worth treating as a high-risk sleeve to be diversified at every liquidity event, not as a lottery ticket to be sentimental about. For the typical investor this website addresses, the truthful summary is short: the public markets already hold most of the return with none of the lock-up, and the ₹1 crore door, when you reach it, will still be there when you can also afford the diligence.

65.6Worked examples and cases

Case 1: the fee drag of 2-and-20 vs an index

Two investors, ₹1 crore each, ten years. The hedge fund investor's manager is good: 15% gross, year after year, well above the market's 12%. Fees: 2% of assets plus 20% of the remaining 13% gain, leaving 10.4% net. The index investor pays 0.2% and nets 11.8%. After ten years: the hedge fund crore becomes ₹2.7 crore; the index crore becomes ₹3.1 crore. The better-performing manager made the client poorer by ₹36 lakh, the entire difference being the fee structure, not the skill. Scale the lesson correctly: exceptional managers exist whose net returns clear the index for decades, and they are rare, closed to new money, or both. The question 2-and-20 poses to every allocator is not "is this manager good?" but "is this manager good enough to overcome a fee that converts 15% into 10%?" That is a very high bar, and most capital that clears it is inside the ropes already.

Case 2: a unicorn's valuation, from funding round to IPO

Follow one illustrative Indian startup. Seed: ₹40 crore for 20%, a ₹200 crore dream-price. Series B: ₹200 crore at a ₹1,200 crore tag as the metrics impress. The 2021 vintage: ₹2,000 crore at ₹8,000 crore, unicorn confetti, terms including a 1x liquidation preference and anti-dilution protection, the fine print nobody reads at the party. The IPO arrives in a colder market at ₹4,500 crore. Watch who eats what: the seed investor still multiplies their money twenty-fold and toasts the journey; the 2021 fund takes a 44% write-down, protected partially by its preference at the expense of everyone below; founders and employees, holding common stock at the bottom of the stack, discover that a ₹4,500 crore exit can pay them less than the headline suggests, and retail IPO buyers meet the company at a price set by the last private round's need to exit. Every chapter of this website was in that story: valuation is a negotiation, preferences are the real price, liquidity events are transfers between time horizons, and the number in the headline is never the number in the waterfall.
