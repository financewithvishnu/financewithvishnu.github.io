Part VI · Fixed Income and Credit

Chapter 44. Corporate Credit and Debt Funds

Chapter 44 of 85 · about 21 minutes

Lend to the government and you worry about interest rates; lend to a company and you worry about getting your money back at all. That worry has a price, the credit spread, and India has taught two unforgettable modern lessons in what happens when it is mispriced: the IL&FS collapse of 2018 and the Franklin Templeton fund closures of 2020. This chapter is about lending to companies with your eyes open, directly or through debt funds.

44.1What do credit ratings from AAA to D really mean?

A credit rating is an agency's opinion of the probability of full, on-time repayment, compressed into letters. The ladder runs from AAA, the strongest, through AA, A, BBB, the last rung of "investment grade", into BB and below, "speculative" or junk, down to D, default. Two truths keep the letters honest. Ratings are opinions, not guarantees: the agencies are paid by the issuers, a conflict that has never been fully solved, and Indian history includes AAA-to-default journeys measured in months. And the market prices ahead of the agencies: a bond's spread usually widens months before the downgrade confirms what the price already knew. Use ratings as the floor of your diligence, never the ceiling.

44.2What are credit spreads, and what moves them?

The credit spread, the extra yield a company pays over the equivalent G-sec, is the market's running price of default fear. It moves with three forces. The issuer: its leverage, cash flows and Chapter 10-style honesty. The cycle: spreads compress in booms, when everyone reaches for yield and fear goes on holiday, and blow out in busts, when the same paper must be sold to the few remaining buyers. And liquidity: Indian corporate bonds trade thinly, so in a panic the spread includes a "can I even sell this?" premium that has little to do with default. The investor's rule follows: the best time to buy credit is when spreads are wide and terrifying; the most dangerous time is when they are narrow and comfortable. Spread is fear's price, and fear, like everything else, should be bought cheap.

44.3How do debt mutual funds work: duration, credit and target-maturity?

Debt funds package the bond market, and the category names hide two independent dials you must set consciously. The duration dial: overnight and liquid funds hold near-zero rate risk; short and medium duration funds hold some; long-duration and gilt funds hold the full seesaw of Chapter 42. The credit dial: gilt and corporate-bond funds hold the safe end; credit-risk funds deliberately hold lower-rated paper for the extra spread, which is a legitimate strategy and a terrible surprise when bought unknowingly as "safe debt". Target-maturity funds, holding bonds to a fixed date, set the duration dial automatically to your horizon. Before buying any debt fund, read its modified duration and its rating-wise portfolio: two lines in the factsheet that tell you which of the two risks you are actually taking. The investor who cannot name both dials is not invested in debt; they are invested in a label.

44.4What happens in a credit event: defaults, downgrades and haircuts?

When credit goes wrong, it goes wrong in a sequence. The downgrade comes first, publicly repricing the bond; funds holding it mark the loss immediately, which is why debt-fund NAVs can fall on a downgrade before any default. The default follows: a missed payment, and the bond enters a workout whose outcome is a recovery, some fraction of face value, months or years later. The haircut is the gap between what was owed and what the recovery pays. The asymmetry is the whole game: a bond pays you a few percent a year for bearing the small chance of losing half. Credit investing is therefore the art of avoiding the left tail, not finding the best yield, and diversification is not a preference but the mathematics: no single corporate exposure should be large enough that its zero is your event.

44.5What are perpetual bonds and AT1 capital?

Bank additional-tier-1 bonds are the strangest instrument most Indian investors will ever be offered: bonds with no maturity ("perpetual"), callable at the bank's pleasure, whose coupons can be skipped without default, and which regulation can convert to equity or write down entirely if the bank's capital falls below trigger levels. In exchange for all that, they pay a fat yield. The design is deliberate: AT1 exists to absorb losses so that depositors and taxpayers do not. The Credit Suisse wipeout of 2023, where AT1 holders were zeroed while shareholders received something, taught the world that the waterfall in the documents is real. In India, where AT1s are sold to yield-hungry investors as "bank bonds", the discipline is plain: a perpetual is not a bond with a high coupon; it is bank equity wearing a coupon's costume, and it must be sized and feared accordingly.

44.6Worked examples and cases

Case 1: IL&FS and Franklin Templeton, the two halves of one lesson

In 2018, IL&FS, an infrastructure financier rated AAA weeks earlier, defaulted, and the discovery that a shelf of "safe" paper was rotten froze the entire Indian credit market: NBFC funding costs spiked, and the aftershocks ran for years. Then in April 2020, Franklin Templeton India shut six debt funds holding over ₹25,000 crore, because their lower-rated, illiquid bonds could not be sold to meet redemptions at any honest price. The two episodes are one lesson in two dialects. IL&FS: a rating is an opinion, and the opinion can be catastrophically late. Franklin: a fund that promises daily liquidity while holding monthly-liquidity paper has written a cheque the structure cannot cash, and the mismatch, not the default rate, is what kills. The takeaways are written into this chapter: read the fund's actual portfolio, respect the liquidity of the underlying, and treat any debt return above the obvious alternatives as a question, not a gift.

Case 2: the Credit Suisse AT1 wipeout, and its Indian echo

March 2023: UBS absorbs Credit Suisse over a weekend, shareholders receive a sliver, and ≈$17 billion of AT1 bonds are written to zero. Legal, disclosed, and still a global shock, because many holders had never priced the clause. The echo for the Indian investor holding bank perpetuals: pull the offer document, find the trigger, the write-down clause, and the coupon-skip rights, and then hold the position only if its zero is a survivable line in your portfolio. If it is not, the extra 1.5-2% of yield was never compensation; it was bait. The general principle outlives the episode: in credit, the yield you are paid is the market's estimate of a risk. Believing the risk is lower is your entire edge, and it must be earned with documents, not vibes.
