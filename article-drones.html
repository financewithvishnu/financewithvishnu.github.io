Drones: when useful flights become a repeatable business

By Finance With Vishnu · Source period: approximately early November 2025 · Sector study

A drone can be an impressive machine and still be a difficult business. Flying is only the visible part. Someone must choose a useful task, obtain the required permissions, deliver a reliable result, support the equipment and collect enough money to pay for all of it.

That is why I would start with the customer's problem rather than a forecast for the drone market. A farmer wants a job completed at an acceptable cost. A mine operator wants dependable survey information. A utility wants inspection findings that help it decide what needs attention. These customers are buying different outcomes.

The industry also contains different suppliers: component makers, aircraft manufacturers, service operators and data-software businesses. A company can participate in several layers, but the revenue, capital needs and risks should still be separated.

1. Which part of the value chain earns the revenue?

A complete drone system can include an airframe, propulsion, power, electronics, navigation, a camera or another task-specific payload, control software and ground equipment. The payload is the equipment or load carried for the intended job. It is not necessarily a camera.

A component supplier sells part of that system. A manufacturer integrates a finished product. A service operator uses equipment to complete work for customers. A software provider processes or organises the resulting data. The same customer payment should not be counted as a separate market opportunity at every layer without adjusting for purchases between them.

The attractive layer depends on differentiation and competition. Components are not automatically low value, and software is not automatically high margin. A specialised sensor can have difficult qualification requirements. A software business can incur substantial processing, support and customer-acquisition costs.

I would write down the payer, deliverable and charging basis before comparing any two companies. Otherwise an equipment sale can be mistaken for a recurring service contract.

2. Why should the task come before the aircraft?

Aircraft configurations are ways to solve operating problems, not a ranking of investment quality. A rotorcraft, an aeroplane-type system and a hybrid can suit different jobs. The January 2022 certification document distinguishes these categories. It does not say that one category has superior business economics in every setting. [6]

The relevant questions are practical. How much useful work can the system complete? What staff, transport, maintenance and supporting equipment are needed? Does the customer require a survey, an inspection, a spraying service or simply an image?

Do not compare a headline endurance figure from one product with a revenue estimate for a different application. The payload, conditions and operating constraints affect what the customer actually receives. Nor does an impressive demonstration establish that a service can be delivered repeatedly at a profit.

I would compare systems against the same customer requirement and the same definition of a completed job. Specifications matter, but they belong inside that comparison rather than replacing it.

3. Is the valuable output a photograph or a decision?

Data capture is not the same as useful information. A large folder of images may create more work unless it can be processed, checked and connected to the customer's existing process.

Aereo describes a workflow covering aerial data capture, cloud processing, outputs such as point clouds and elevation models, analysis, quality checks and sharing. A point cloud is a collection of measured points representing a surface or object. The commercial lesson is the chain from collection to a usable deliverable, not a claim that every data platform has the same accuracy. [2]

DJI's powerline-inspection page likewise describes visual and thermal imagery, analysis, inspection reporting and links to asset-management work. This is a vendor's description of an inspection workflow, not an independent finding that all inspections detect every defect or remove the need for professional judgement. [4]

Ask what the buyer does differently after receiving the result. Does it support planning, maintenance prioritisation or measurement? Who verifies quality? What happens when the output is incomplete? The answer helps distinguish a useful service from an attractive demonstration.

4. When does software become genuinely recurring?

A software interface does not by itself create subscription revenue. The customer needs a continuing reason to use and pay for it.

That reason could be repeated analysis, collaboration, records over time or integration with an established work process. Aereo's description of cloud processing and sharing illustrates why the service can extend beyond the day of the flight. It does not establish the commercial terms, renewal rate or profitability of any particular contract. [2]

ideaForge's FY2025 annual report identifies its first year of revenue from drone-as-a-service and software platforms. That establishes the presence of those activities in that reporting period. It does not mean the whole company had become a mature subscription business. [1]

I would look for separately disclosed recurring revenue, renewals, customer retention, implementation work and the cost of storage, processing and support. If those details are unavailable, keep the uncertainty visible instead of applying a software valuation to all equipment revenue.

Data ownership, permitted use and customer access also matter. A service that creates dependence without clear rights can be harder to sell, particularly where the information concerns sensitive assets or private property.

5. What makes agricultural adoption an economic question?

Agriculture offers an understandable use case, but a potential use is not the same as a profitable local service network. The buyer must see value after considering the crop, timing, local alternatives and the price of the completed job.

Garuda Aerospace describes agricultural spraying services using its Kisan Drone platform. Its case-study page makes speed, water-use, cost and yield claims. Those are provider claims, not controlled evidence that the same outcome applies to every farm, crop or season. I would not use its most attractive percentage as a universal forecast. [3]

From a business perspective, demand can be seasonal and geographically scattered. A service operator needs enough nearby work, suitable staff and functioning equipment when customers need the job completed. Transport, scheduling and downtime belong in the model.

A subsidy can help an equipment purchase without ensuring repeat customer demand. Separate the person financing the machine from the person paying for each service. Also separate equipment capacity from the area that customers actually pay to have serviced.

This is not agricultural or chemical-use guidance. The investment question is whether the operator can deliver an appropriate, compliant service that customers choose again without depending on an unrealistic utilisation assumption.

6. Why does utilisation matter more than fleet size?

A fleet is a collection of assets. Revenue comes from paid work. The gap can be large when equipment spends time travelling, waiting, charging, being repaired or sitting between projects.

Utilisation needs a clear definition. Flight hours, available days, completed jobs and billable days answer different questions. A high flight-hour figure can still include unpaid demonstrations, rework or work awaiting acceptance.

For a service company, I would track paid jobs per asset, downtime, repeat customers, travel intensity and collections where disclosed. For an equipment manufacturer, I would instead examine product demand, delivery acceptance, warranty obligations and support costs.

Mixing these measures produces false comparisons. An operator buying more drones can increase the manufacturer's sales while weakening its own returns if the additional fleet remains underused.

7. What does a simple break-even model reveal?

Consider an original hypothetical service business. Assume annual fixed costs of ₹6 lakh, revenue of ₹10,000 per completed job and variable costs of ₹4,000 per job. Each job contributes ₹6,000 before fixed costs.

The simplified break-even point is 100 paid jobs a year. At 80 jobs, contribution is ₹4.8 lakh and the business loses ₹1.2 lakh before financing and tax. At 140 jobs, contribution is ₹8.4 lakh and the corresponding surplus is ₹2.4 lakh.

This is not a quoted drone price or a forecast for any named operator. It leaves out financing, tax and changes in the cost structure. Replacement equipment and working capital also need funding.

The useful question is what happens if jobs arrive later, customers negotiate prices or support costs rise. Scale helps only when contribution remains positive and the additional work can be delivered without disproportionate new investment.

8. Does localisation automatically create an advantage?

Local manufacturing can improve control of supply, product support and customer-specific development. But an assembly operation, local sourcing, owned designs and ownership of critical intellectual property are different claims.

ideaForge's FY2025 report discusses vertical integration and a localised supply chain. Its disclosed company-level localisation measure is not proof that every component of every model is made domestically. The definition and product boundary matter. [1]

The government's December 2023 reform summary described the 2022 import-policy distinction between drones and drone components. That is historical policy context, not a blanket claim that every foreign product is currently prohibited or every component is freely usable in every application. Current restrictions, exceptions and permissions must be checked separately. [5]

Even a protected market can contain several domestic competitors. Protection does not determine which one controls costs, delivers reliable products or earns a return. I would ask which parts the company actually designs, which it buys, how easily customers can substitute them and what happens when a supplier changes price or availability.

9. Is certification the same as commercial success?

No. Regulatory conformity, customer acceptance and business economics are separate layers of evidence.

The January 2022 Certification Scheme for Unmanned Aircraft Systems sets out a certification framework and identifies its scope. The document is linked here through a hosted copy of the official gazette. It contains categories and exceptions, which is why a statement that every drone follows one identical approval route would be wrong. [6]

The government's December 2023 summary also distinguishes the drone rules, airspace arrangements, certification and remote-pilot certification. These are historical references explaining the industry's framework. They are not a substitute for checking the applicable current requirements for a specific activity. [5]

For investment research, record exactly what approval exists, the product and entity it covers, and what it does not establish. An approval does not prove customer demand. A customer trial does not prove a purchase order. A purchase order does not prove successful delivery or payment.

I would avoid turning the number of certified models into a forecast for industry revenue. It measures one part of readiness, not the size of the paying market.

10. How should we read orders in a young product business?

A development agreement, a trial, an expected tender and a firm order should not share one column. Their chances of producing revenue differ, and their funding requirements can differ too.

Even firm orders need interpretation. Delivery dates, customer acceptance, service obligations and cancellation terms can affect when revenue and cash appear. Concentration in a few large customers can make results uneven.

ideaForge's FY2025 report discusses a difficult year while identifying civil and defence activities, new products and software or service initiatives. The reporting boundary is important: group results should not be described as revenue from commercial inspection alone. [1]

I would compare several periods of order intake, opening backlog, delivered revenue and closing backlog where those measures are available. Do not annualise one strong quarter without understanding the delivery schedule, and do not assume one weak quarter proves that the entire use case has disappeared.

Most importantly, keep current order claims dated. An old presentation is evidence of what was disclosed then, not proof that the same order remains unexecuted today.

11. Where can accounting profit differ from cash?

Product development requires money before the result is certain. Spending can include research, engineering, prototypes, testing and software. The accounting treatment affects reported profit but does not remove the cash outflow.

ideaForge's FY2025 financial statements describe development expenditure and the conditions for capitalisation. The auditor also identifies capitalisation of product development as an area requiring audit attention. That is not an allegation of improper accounting. It is a reason to read the policy and judgement rather than treating all development spending as an ordinary current-period expense. [1]

Compare cash spent on development, amounts recorded as assets, amortisation, impairment and evidence of commercial demand. A capitalised asset must eventually earn its place through future economic benefit.

Inventory, receivables and warranty obligations can consume cash as well. A manufacturer may need parts before receiving an order or may wait for acceptance before collecting. A service operator may pay staff and travel costs before the customer pays.

I would assess the funding runway alongside growth. A promising technology with repeated financing needs can create a different shareholder outcome from a business funding its own expansion.

12. Is counter-drone equipment the same market?

No. Providing a drone service and supplying equipment intended to protect a site are different businesses. They can share a broad industry theme without sharing customers, contracts or revenue patterns.

For a counter-drone supplier, the business questions concern the paying customer, the contracted scope, acceptance evidence, maintenance, software updates and replacement demand. A demonstration or a claim about technical capability is not the same as an accepted, paid installation.

It is also unsafe to assume that every additional drone creates one corresponding protection-system sale. Demand depends on the site's needs, procurement decisions and the solution already in place. Market forecasts that combine equipment, installation and ongoing services require careful boundaries.

This article does not assess specific countermeasure effectiveness or describe operating techniques. The useful financial distinction is between a supplier with repeat, supported contracts and one whose story depends mainly on possible future tenders.

I would apply the same discipline to a diversified listed company: identify the disclosed contribution of this activity before labelling the entire group a drone or counter-drone business.

13. What evidence would make the business easier to trust?

I would become more confident when useful customer outcomes lead to repeat orders, improving cash conversion and clearer disclosure. I would become less confident when product launches multiply but the route from spending to collections remains vague.

The opportunity can be real without every supplier being a winner. A good drone business must connect engineering, customer value and cash. The machine leaving the ground is the beginning of that test, not the end.
