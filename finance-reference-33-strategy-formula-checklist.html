Chapter 33. Stock Strategies: Formula and Checklist Strategies

Chapter 33 of 85 · about 20 minutes

What if the entire craft of Part IV could be compressed into two ratios and a rebalance date? The formula strategies claim exactly that, and the shocking thing is how often the compression works: mechanical, emotionless rules have beaten most human stock-pickers across most measured decades. The catch is never in the formula. It is in the human asked to follow it through the year it looks stupid. This chapter gives you the great formulas, their Indian track records, and the temperament test that decides whether they will work for you.

33.1What are Graham’s defensive and enterprising checklists?

The originals, from the man who taught Buffett. Graham's defensive checklist demanded size, financial strength (current ratio above 2, debt within limits), uninterrupted dividends for twenty years, no losses in ten, moderate growth, and a price capped at 15x earnings and 1.5x book. His enterprising version loosened each constraint and added the net-net hunting of Chapter 27. The lists read quaint until you notice what they are: a complete risk-management system expressed as admission criteria. Nothing speculative enters; nothing overpriced enters; nothing financially fragile enters. Run Graham's defensive list over Indian large-caps in a normal year and a handful of familiar, dull, profitable names pass, the same names that quietly compound while the exciting names make news. The checklist's genius was never prediction; it was refusal.

33.2What is the magic formula of high ROCE plus high earnings yield?

Greenblatt's famous compression: rank all stocks by return on capital (good companies), rank them by earnings yield, the inverse of P/E (cheap companies), add the ranks, buy the top thirty, rebalance annually. That is the entire strategy, and it works because it mechanises the two engines this book has taught separately: Chapter 11's quality and Chapter 16's cheapness. US backtests showed large long-run edges; Indian versions, applied to the listed universe with the obvious exclusions, financials, where ROCE means something else, and the forensically unclean, show the same shape. The formula's deeper lesson: you do not need to find the best stocks. You need a rule that systematically prefers good-and-cheap over bad-or-dear, applied without exception. The formula is a factory for slightly better odds, run thousands of times.

33.3What is CAN SLIM, and how do momentum-growth hybrid formulas work?

O'Neil's CAN SLIM is the growth side's formula: Current quarterly earnings accelerating, Annual earnings growing, New products or management or price highs, Supply and demand (small float, low debt), Leader in its industry, Institutional sponsorship building, Market trend confirmed. Where Graham buys dull and cheap, CAN SLIM buys strong and dear, and its rules include the growth investor's safety gear: buy only in confirmed uptrends, cut every loss at 7-8%. The honest reading of the two traditions is that they are systems for different temperaments, both complete: Graham's loses slowly and wins slowly; O'Neil's loses fast and small and rides few big winners. The hybrid that Indian factor funds increasingly implement, quality plus momentum plus value in one basket, is the admission that no single formula owns the truth.

33.4What is the dogs-of-the-index strategy?

The simplest formula ever published: each year, buy the ten highest-yielding stocks in the index, hold a year, repeat. The logic is pure contrarian mechanics: in a large-cap index, a high dividend yield usually marks a sound company at a depressed price, so the "dogs" are tomorrow's recoveries bought automatically. In India the flavour works on the Nifty or the Sensex with the obvious caveats: the index is small, the dogs cluster in PSUs and cyclicals, and a yield can mark a genuine decline rather than a bargain, so the Chapter 29 coverage check still applies. The strategy's real lesson is not its modest edge; it is that a rule this simple, requiring one hour a year and zero judgement, competes seriously with the average fund manager. Fees, taxes and behaviour are the opponent, and a mechanical rule beats all three by never showing up to play.

33.5Do formulas still work after everyone knows them?

The sophisticated worry: published edges get arbitraged away. The evidence so far is kinder and more interesting. Formula edges shrink after publication, typically by a third to a half, and then persist, because the forces that create them are behavioural, and behaviour does not read papers. Value keeps working because buying the hated is psychologically expensive forever; momentum keeps working because crowds keep under-reacting; and every formula keeps working precisely because it is unbearable to follow in its losing years, which is the moat no arbitrageur can cross. The practical conclusions: expect smaller edges than the backtest's, expect years, plural, when the formula loses, and judge any formula over a full cycle. The investor who abandons the formula in year three of its drought has supplied the edge that pays the investor who did not.

33.6Worked example: the magic formula on Indian stocks, year by year

Case: five years of two ranks and one rebalance

Apply the formula to the Indian universe, financials excluded, forensic failures excluded, thirty stocks, annual rebalance, through a full illustrative cycle:

Year (illustrative cycle)Magic-formula basketNifty 500Note
Year 1 (bull)+42%+28%Cheap quality leads the recovery
Year 2 (bull)+24%+22%Keeps pace
Year 3 (growth mania)+6%+21%The formula refuses expensive stories; looks broken
Year 4 (crash)-18%-25%Cheap cushions, does not protect
Year 5 (recovery)+38%+26%The rebound pays the patient
5-year CAGR+16.1%+12.3%The edge, earned in the years it felt worst

Every line of that table is a behavioural test. Year three is where most followers quit: the formula trails by fifteen points while the market pays for stories, and the rule feels like a superstition. Year five is where the quitting is paid for. Notice also what the formula never required: no view on rates, no sector call, no reading of the Budget, no opinion about anything. Two rankings, honestly maintained, through one full cycle, beat the index by nearly four points a year, which compounds into a different retirement. The formula's only real demand is that you be the kind of person who can own year three.
