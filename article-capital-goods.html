Capital goods: follow the customer's investment, then test the supplier's cash

By Finance With Vishnu · Source period: June 2025

A factory expansion can create orders for machinery, engineering services and specialised components. Once the factory starts operating, it may need spares, maintenance and consumables. These activities share an industrial customer, but they do not share the same earnings cycle.

I would begin by asking what makes the customer spend. Is it a new plant, replacement of an old machine, higher production on an existing line or a requirement to keep equipment working? That distinction is more useful than treating capital goods as one uniform sector.

The second question is whether the supplier can turn the opportunity into cash at an adequate return. A growing order book is encouraging. It does not settle the questions of execution, margins, collection or capital allocation.

1. Which customer decision creates the order?

Capital goods help customers create or operate productive capacity. But buying a complete machine is different from buying a part for an existing production line, and both differ from buying consumables used during production.

A customer might postpone a new plant while continuing to maintain existing equipment. Another customer might run its factory at a lower utilisation level and consume fewer industrial supplies without cancelling all maintenance.

A company can participate in several categories. I would estimate which activity drives its profit rather than classify the whole company using its most attractive small division.

The map also helps avoid double counting. One customer's announced plant budget is not automatically revenue for every supplier attached to that theme.

2. Why must we study the customer's economics?

The customer's ability and willingness to invest influence the supplier's demand. Attractive end-market growth can support spending, but financing, existing capacity, expected returns and project approvals still matter.

A supplier serving an expanding industry may receive more enquiries before it receives binding orders. An announced customer project can be delayed, resized or cancelled. Suppliers may compete for only part of the budget.

I would follow the chain from customer intention to funding, ordering, execution and payment. These are separate pieces of evidence. A macro headline establishes none of them by itself.

The operating cycle matters too. A company selling to customers in several industries can have one strong business and another weak one at the same time. Its overall revenue growth may hide that difference.

Rather than assume that a national investment cycle benefits every company equally, ask which customers are spending, which products they need and whether this supplier has the capability and commercial access to win that work.

3. What does an order book tell us, and what does it leave out?

An order book is a stock of work under the company's reporting definition. Order intake is new work received during a period. Revenue reflects work recognised during a period. The three measures should not be used interchangeably.

A simplified bridge is opening backlog plus new orders, less executed work and cancellations, with other relevant adjustments. The reported figures may require changes for currency, scope or different recognition boundaries.

Praj's FY2025 results release reports period revenue, order intake and closing backlog separately. It is a useful primary-source example of why the measures deserve separate columns. [3]

I would read the terms behind the backlog: duration, customer concentration, advances, price escalation, cancellation rights and execution requirements. A large fixed-price order can carry substantial risk if costs rise before completion.

Backlog divided by annual revenue is only a rough coverage measure. It is not a promise that revenue will grow at that rate or that the entire backlog will convert in the next year. Timing and profitability remain separate questions.

4. Why can a bigger order be harder to execute?

Customised industrial work can require design, procurement, testing, installation and customer acceptance. Skilled people, specialised vendors and engineering capacity can limit execution even when factory space is available.

More orders therefore do not always produce immediate operating leverage. A company may need to hire, train, buy equipment or carry additional work in progress before revenue arrives.

Consider a hypothetical ₹100 crore fixed-price project with expected total cost of ₹85 crore. It has ₹15 crore of expected surplus before financing, tax and other costs. If total cost rises to ₹92 crore without a matching price revision, the surplus falls to ₹8 crore.

That is a fall of about 46.7%, even though the contract value has not changed. The example does not imply that every project is fixed-price or accounted for identically. It shows why order value is not a substitute for contract economics.

I would examine cost estimates, provisions, warranty obligations and completion performance. Winning work is only the first commercial test. Delivering it at the expected return is the next.

5. When does a service network become an advantage?

Customers buying equipment also care about what happens when it stops working. Downtime can interrupt a project or production line, so the cost of the machine is only part of the customer's decision.

Ajax's FY2025 annual report describes concrete equipment alongside a dealer and service network, spare parts and after-sales support. It identifies maintenance, troubleshooting and equipment availability as parts of that offering. [1]

A capable network can make the product easier to operate and maintain. It takes trained people, parts availability and consistent service quality to make that advantage real.

I would not assume that the presence of service centres guarantees pricing power or that every equipment sale creates a large annuity. The recurring business may still be small relative to new-machine sales.

The evidence to seek is repeat customer demand, service economics, parts availability and the cost of supporting the installed fleet. The network is valuable when it improves outcomes for the customer and returns for the supplier.

6. Is an installed base the same as recurring revenue?

No. An installed base is a population of equipment. Revenue depends on how much of it remains active, what it consumes, which services customers buy and whether the supplier retains that business.

Control Print's FY2025 report describes coding and marking printers, inks and other consumables, plus service and spares. It also discusses the importance of customer accounts and aftermarket engagement. This is a different model from earning the entire lifetime return when the machine is first sold. [2]

For an original simplified example, suppose 1,000 active machines each generate ₹50,000 of annual consumable revenue. That creates ₹5 crore of revenue. If the active base rises 10% and spending per active machine falls 10%, revenue becomes ₹4.95 crore.

The installed base grew, yet revenue declined 1%. Increased installations can be offset by lower usage, lower pricing, lost customers or a different equipment mix.

I would track active machines, customer production, revenue per machine and retention where disclosed. Recurring does not mean unconditional, and an installed-base count is not enough to establish future profit.

7. Are industrial consumables protected from the cycle?

They can have a different cycle from new equipment, but they are not immune. Demand can depend on customer production, product life, usage intensity and the supplier's share of that customer's requirements.

Vesuvius India's calendar-2024 report describes refractory products and consumable ceramic systems serving steel and other high-temperature industries. It also discusses raw-material inflation and the need to manage costs. [5]

A product can be necessary for production and still face pressure if customers produce less, negotiate harder or use the product more efficiently. Longer product life can improve customer economics while reducing replacement frequency.

Different refractory formulations and applications also have different economics. I would not apply one material cost, replacement interval or margin to every supplier.

The useful questions are how much the customer produces, how much material is used per unit of output and what the supplier earns after inputs and service. Necessity supports relevance. It does not guarantee an unchanged margin.

8. Why do components and aftermarket need separate analysis?

A component supplier can sell into original equipment, replacement demand or both. The customer, pricing process and demand drivers can differ between those channels.

Schaeffler India's calendar-2024 report separates activities including automotive technologies, vehicle lifetime solutions and bearings and industrial solutions. It describes products and services across original applications and ongoing equipment use. [4]

For a new application, technical requirements, testing and approval can matter before commercial production. For replacement demand, availability, trusted performance and distribution can influence the customer's choice.

Qualification can make a relationship harder to replace, but it is not a guarantee of volume or an unlimited right to raise prices. The customer's own output and sourcing decisions remain important.

I would also distinguish the complete bearing or assembly from its inputs and subcomponents. A supplier of rings or cages should not be assigned the economics of every finished product using those parts.

9. How does operating leverage change the result?

Some costs change with output while others remain broadly fixed over a relevant range. When volume rises, fixed costs can be spread over more sales. When volume falls, the same mechanism works in reverse.

In a hypothetical supplier, revenue is ₹100 crore, variable costs are 60% of revenue and fixed operating costs are ₹25 crore. Operating profit before depreciation and other excluded items is ₹15 crore.

If revenue rises to ₹120 crore with the same cost assumptions, variable costs rise to ₹72 crore and profit rises to ₹23 crore. A 20% revenue increase produces a 53.3% profit increase. At ₹80 crore revenue, profit falls to ₹7 crore.

Real costs can change in steps, and expanding output may require new investment. I would stress-test the downside rather than extrapolate the strongest margin indefinitely.

10. Why can policy create both demand and uncertainty?

Policy can affect the cost of buying equipment, the standards it must meet or the timing of customer investment. The impact depends on the specific rule and the company's exposure.

Ajax's FY2025 report discusses the transition between construction-equipment emission standards. Such transitions can affect buying patterns, product costs and comparisons between periods. A strong shipment period can include purchases brought forward rather than an entirely new long-term demand level. [1]

Solar-pump economics provide a different illustration. The MNRE PM-KUSUM page describes distinct components and region-dependent assistance, with conditions based on benchmark or tender costs. Its listed scheme period runs through March 2026. It cannot establish a current universal subsidy rate or the availability of a new application today. [6]

I would separate the customer's need, the funded programme, the supplier's award and the cash collected. A policy allocation is not the same as a company order.

Even after an order, installation, inspection, acceptance and payment may occur on different dates. Policy-linked demand needs the same execution and cash discipline as private-sector demand.

11. Where can reported growth consume cash?

Inventory, work in progress and receivables can rise before the supplier collects payment. Customer advances and supplier credit can offset part of that need, but they are not permanent free capital.

In a simplified example, receivables increase ₹30 crore and inventory increases ₹20 crore. Payables increase ₹15 crore and customer advances increase ₹10 crore. The net working-capital requirement rises ₹25 crore before other movements.

A business can report profit while borrowing to fund that gap. Strong order intake can therefore create a funding requirement rather than an immediate cash surplus.

I would compare operating cash flow with earnings across several periods, then read the reasons for the difference. A temporary project ramp and a persistent collection problem require different conclusions.

12. When does expansion stop creating value?

New capacity is useful only if customers need it and the return justifies the capital committed. Commissioning a factory is not the same as filling it profitably.

Capacity can also be application-specific. Machinery, people, qualification and supply arrangements must fit the work. One unused production line may not solve a bottleneck elsewhere.

Acquisitions add another layer. Control Print's FY2025 report describes international subsidiaries and acquired capabilities. That broadens what must be analysed beyond the original domestic business. Standalone performance does not automatically describe the consolidated group. [2]

I would assess new projects and acquisitions using total cash invested, working capital, ongoing losses where relevant and a realistic ramp. A bigger revenue base does not establish a better return on capital.

The same discipline applies to promising technologies. Technical capability, an enquiry, a framework agreement and a profitable commercial order are different stages. Treat the opportunity as evidence develops rather than booking its entire imagined market in advance.

13. What belongs in the research file?

I would gain confidence when useful products earn repeat business, delivery remains reliable and cash follows growth. I would be more cautious when a thesis depends on announced budgets, peak margins or a project ramp with no room for delay.

Capital goods is a collection of different business models. Understanding the customer's next decision helps explain demand. Understanding the supplier's costs, cash and capital allocation helps explain whether that demand becomes a good business.
