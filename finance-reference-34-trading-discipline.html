Chapter 34. Trading Stocks: The Trader's Discipline

Chapter 34 of 85 · about 20 minutes

This is the first of four chapters about trading, and it opens with the truth the trading industry does not advertise: most active traders lose, the losers are not stupid, and they lose for structural reasons that can be named, measured and, for the disciplined few, survived. If you are going to trade, and this book will not pretend you should not, these are the rules that decide which side of the statistics you land on.

34.1What is the honest difference between investing and trading?

Investing buys a share of a business's future cash flows; the asset works for you while you sleep, and time is your ally. Trading sells the next move to another participant; the asset does nothing, the counterparty is often better equipped than you, and time is your enemy, because costs and noise compound against you daily. Both are legitimate; confusing them is fatal. The investor who "trades a little on the side" with his long-term money has merged a game where patience wins with a game where it bleeds. The honest separation: separate accounts, separate capital, separate scorecards, and a written rule about which pool of money plays which game. Trading capital is tuition until proven otherwise, and it should be sized so that graduating summa cum laude from total loss changes nothing about your life.

34.2How much should you risk per trade: position sizing and the maths of survival?

The only edge that matters before all others: survival. The framework is Chapter 17's, made exact. Decide the rupee risk per trade as a small fixed fraction of capital, 0.5-1% for most humans; place the stop where the trade idea is objectively wrong; and compute the position size backwards from the two: quantity = (capital x risk fraction) / (entry minus stop). This one formula, applied without exception, is the entire difference between trading as a business and trading as a slow-motion accident. The table below shows why: the strategy is identical, the sizing is not, and the outcome is a different life.

34.3Why does every trader need a journal and statistics?

A trader without records is a gambler with a good memory for wins. The journal is the strategy's accounting system: every trade logged with the setup, the reason, the size, the exit, and the rule followed or broken. From fifty logged trades the statistics emerge, and they are the only honest description of your edge: win rate, average win versus average loss (the payoff ratio), and their product, expectancy, the average rupee earned per trade. A 40% win rate with 3:1 payoffs is a business; a 60% win rate with 1:4 payoffs is a funeral. The journal also catches the invisible leak: rule violations. Most traders who "have a strategy" in fact have a strategy plus improvisations, and the journal is where the improvisations' true cost appears in writing, trade by trade.

34.4Why do most active traders lose money to costs, slippage and STT?

Run the arithmetic that the apps do not show you. Each round trip in Indian equities carries brokerage (small now, thanks to discount brokers), exchange and regulatory charges, stamp duty, GST, the securities transaction tax, and, largest of all for the active, slippage: the spread and impact between the price you wanted and the fill you got. Stack realistic numbers and an active intraday trader gives up roughly 0.05-0.15% of traded value per round trip; at daily turnover, that is 10-30% of capital per year, a headwind the strategy's edge must first repay. Add the zero-sum truth, that your counterparty is increasingly an institution with faster data and better execution, and the statistical base rate, most active traders lose, stops being mysterious. It is not that trading is rigged; it is that the toll booth sits between you and the game, and most strategies are not good enough to pay the toll.

34.5When is trading a business, and when is it gambling?

The dividing line is written evidence, not self-image. Trading is a business when it has: a documented strategy with measured expectancy, fixed risk per trade, records of every decision, capital sized to survive the worst realistic drawdown, and performance reviewed monthly like an accountant, not a fan. It is gambling when it has: positions sized by conviction, stops that move, losses chased, wins attributed to skill and losses to luck, and no record that could prove otherwise. The two activities look identical from the outside, same screens, same app, same stocks. The test is internal and simple: could you hand your trading to an auditor? If the answer embarrasses you, the market is already auditing you, in real time, with your money.

34.6Worked example: the 1% rule in action

Case: two hundred trades, one rule, arithmetic instead of drama

Take a trader with ₹5,00,000, risking 1% per trade, running a strategy with a 45% win rate and a 1.8:1 payoff, both ordinary, achievable numbers. Expectancy per trade: 0.45 x 1.8R - 0.55 x 1R = +0.26R, about ₹1,300 per trade on ₹5,000 risked. Two hundred trades a year, a busy swing-trading pace: expectancy near ₹2.6 lakh, a 50%-ish gross year before costs, with a worst-case drawdown along the way of perhaps 15-20 consecutive-loss-scarred percent, survivable, sleepable, repeatable. The point is not the projection, which reality will modestly tax. The point is the shape: a mediocre edge, sized for survival, compounded with discipline, beats the brilliant edge sized for glory that dies in its third losing streak. Trader A from the table above had the same strategy. The market never beat him. Arithmetic did.
