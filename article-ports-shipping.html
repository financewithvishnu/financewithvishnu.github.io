Ports and shipping: follow the cargo, the contract and the cash

By Finance With Vishnu · Source period: October 2025

A ship, a port and a storage terminal can all appear in the same cargo journey. That does not make them the same business. One earns from transporting goods, another from handling them, and another from storing them. Their risks can move in different directions.

I would begin with the customer's payment. Is the company paid for a voyage, for making a vessel available, for handling cargo or for reserving storage? Then ask what costs and obligations sit behind that payment.

This approach keeps us from treating every maritime company as a freight-rate bet, or every hard asset as a dependable annuity. The asset matters. The contract and the capital required to keep it useful matter just as much.

1. Which maritime business are we actually studying?

A vessel owner can earn freight from carrying cargo or charter income from providing a vessel under agreed terms. A service company can provide specialist vessels and staff. A port or terminal operator earns under its own handling, storage, access and service arrangements.

Shipbuilding is another activity again. A company buying a vessel does not become a shipbuilder. A company managing someone else's vessel does not necessarily own the asset.

A group may combine several layers. That can create coordination benefits, but the economics of each layer remain worth separating. Owning more assets increases the opportunity to earn and the amount of money that must earn a return.

I would identify the contracting entity, ownership boundary and revenue model before comparing margins. The shared maritime label is the start of the classification, not the conclusion.

2. Why are dry bulk, tankers and containers different?

Dry bulk refers to unpackaged bulk commodities. Tankers carry liquid or gas cargo appropriate to the vessel. Container shipping uses standardised boxes. They are not interchangeable descriptions of the same fleet.

Great Eastern Shipping's FY2025 report identifies crude oil, petroleum products, gas and dry bulk commodities among the cargoes it serves. Its exposure should not be casually described as a container-shipping business. [1]

Different vessels face different demand, supply and trading patterns. A strong tanker market does not prove that every dry-bulk route or container service has the same pricing environment.

Even within a category, vessel size, condition, trading capability and charter terms matter. A fleet-wide average can hide a contract that is repricing, a ship awaiting repair or a vessel serving a different market.

I would keep the vessel category and earnings measure beside every rate comparison. A dollar-per-day figure without its vessel, period and cost boundary can create more confusion than insight.

3. Does the oil price determine the freight rate?

Not by itself. Freight depends on cargo demand and the amount of suitable vessel capacity available to carry it. Distance, congestion, routing, fleet changes and vessel availability can also affect that balance.

UNCTAD's 2025 review describes rerouting and longer journeys as important influences on transport demand and freight volatility. Tonne-miles combine the quantity of cargo with how far it travels. The same tonnes travelling farther require more transport work. [7]

In an original simplified example, one million tonnes travelling 1,000 nautical miles create one billion tonne-nautical-miles of transport work. At 1,500 nautical miles, the figure becomes 1.5 billion even though cargo volume is unchanged.

That does not translate mechanically into a 50% increase in freight rates. Fleet availability and other conditions still matter. It shows why cargo tonnes alone do not explain demand for ships.

Oil prices can affect fuel costs and customers' decisions, but the contract determines who bears the fuel bill. I would examine both the transport market and the cost allocation rather than using one commodity price as the whole model.

4. What does a charter contract protect, and what does it leave open?

A charter is an agreement to use a vessel under defined terms. The charging basis, duration, responsibilities, availability requirements and treatment of costs can differ. It is more than a simple rent figure.

A longer contract may provide revenue visibility while limiting the benefit from rising market rates. When it expires, repricing can help or hurt. A new vessel increases potential capacity but does not guarantee profitable employment.

SEAMEC's FY2025 report describes offshore support through diving support vessels and other assets, with bulk-carrier activities in subsidiaries. Its customer and fleet mix therefore differ from those of a general cargo carrier. [2]

For a specialist service, staff, equipment and performance obligations can form part of the commercial offering. A quoted day rate may include items excluded from another quote. Fuel pass-through can reduce direct fuel-cost exposure without removing the customer's broader exploration or maintenance cycle.

I would read the contract boundary before comparing rates. Ask about unpaid downtime, mobilisation, repairs, crew, fuel and early termination. A large contract value is meaningful only alongside the cost of delivering it.

5. Why do utilisation and maintenance belong in the same model?

Revenue depends on days that earn, not simply on days a vessel exists. Planned maintenance, repairs, repositioning and gaps between contracts can reduce earning time.

Consider a hypothetical vessel earning ₹1 lakh per paid day, with operating costs of ₹30,000 per calendar day. At 300 paid days, annual revenue is ₹3 crore. Calendar-day operating costs are ₹1.095 crore, leaving ₹1.905 crore before other costs.

At 240 paid days with the same assumptions, revenue falls to ₹2.4 crore and the corresponding amount falls to ₹1.305 crore. A 20% fall in paid days reduces this simplified contribution by about 31.5%.

The example is not a market rate or a forecast for any company. Real costs and contract terms vary.

SEAMEC's FY2025 report discusses deployment, dry-docking and the accounting for vessel and modification costs. These disclosures show why an EBITDA comparison needs the maintenance and replacement requirements beside it. [2]

6. Is net asset value a floor under the share price?

No. A fleet valuation is an estimate under particular assumptions. Vessel prices, selling costs, debt, commitments and the ability to sell without disrupting operations affect what shareholders might ultimately receive.

Net asset value is generally considered by estimating asset values and deducting relevant liabilities. It is not the same as historical book value, and neither automatically represents a guaranteed liquidation payment.

Cycle timing matters twice: when the company buys ships and when it earns from them. Buying expensive assets during a strong market can leave a long payback if earnings weaken. Selling an asset can release cash while reducing the future earning fleet.

I would compare earnings power, vessel values and funding under several conditions. Include maintenance, replacement and debt service. A low earnings multiple at a peak can be less attractive than it looks if the numerator is about to normalise.

A company's capital allocation record is therefore part of the business model. Growth in fleet size is useful only if the price paid and the returns earned make sense.

7. Why does a good port need more than a berth?

A berth is a place for a vessel to dock. A commercially useful facility also needs appropriate access, equipment, handling capability, storage and links to the cargo's origin or destination.

The customer considers the full route, not only one handling charge. A cheaper port service can lose its advantage if onward transport, waiting or unreliable connections increase the total cost.

Adani Ports' FY2025 report describes a network spanning ports, marine services, rail-linked logistics, logistics parks, warehouses and other connections. JSW Infrastructure's report describes ports and terminals alongside logistics expansion. These are examples of broader networks, not proof that either company is always the cheapest route. [3, 4]

Integration can improve coordination and support volumes. It can also require substantial capital in activities with different returns. Owning every link does not automatically make the whole chain efficient.

I would assess service reliability, relevant cargo connections, customer choice and returns after investment. The network's usefulness must show up in business results, not just in the number of assets on a map.

8. What is the difference between capacity and throughput?

Capacity is the amount a facility is designed or permitted to handle under specified conditions. Throughput is the amount actually handled over a period. Neither is automatically equal to revenue.

For ports, cargo type, service mix and price per unit influence revenue. For storage, tank capacity, occupancy, turnover and the charging arrangement can all matter. Capacity can remain underused during a ramp or when another part of the route is constrained.

Suppose a hypothetical terminal has annual capacity of 10 million tonnes and handles six million tonnes. On those matched definitions, utilisation is 60%. Expanding capacity to 15 million tonnes while handling the same volume reduces utilisation to 40%, not an automatic increase in earnings.

I would track the ramp project by project. Announced capacity, commissioned capacity and profitable throughput are separate milestones.

9. Does captive cargo remove demand risk?

A group customer can support volumes and make investment easier to plan. It also creates concentration and the need to understand related-party terms.

JSW Infrastructure's FY2025 report distinguishes group and third-party cargo and describes efforts to broaden the customer base. That is a useful way to analyse how a port business is developing beyond its initial anchor demand. [4]

Do not assume that all group volume is guaranteed at any price. Read duration, minimum-volume commitments, pricing, termination terms and the financial position of the customer where disclosed.

Third-party growth can reduce one concentration while introducing different commercial demands. It may require new handling capability, connections, working capital or more competitive pricing.

I would compare the quality of volume with its quantity. A tonne handled under a durable contract and a tonne won temporarily through low pricing do not necessarily create the same return.

10. Is a container freight station the same as owning a port?

No. A container freight station supports activities such as handling, storage and movement of containers or cargo connected to the port system. Its location near a port does not make it the owner of that port.

Navkar's facility page describes its Panvel container freight stations, railway sidings, storage and links into Nhava Sheva Port. JSW Infrastructure's FY2025 report discusses Navkar within its logistics network. These sources support a logistics-facility classification, not the claim that Navkar owns the port itself. [6, 4]

The distinction changes what we should study. A logistics facility needs cargo flow, connectivity, service quality and customer economics. Its rights, capital requirements and competitive alternatives differ from those of a port concession.

I would be equally careful with ownership, operation, management and concession rights elsewhere in the chain. These words describe different claims on the asset and its cash flows.

11. What does a liquid or gas terminal sell?

A storage-terminal business provides suitable capacity and associated handling services for customers' products. Depending on the contract, fees can reflect reserved space, time, throughput or additional services. It is not automatically a bet on owning the commodity stored.

Aegis Vopak Terminals' FY2025 report distinguishes gas and liquid terminalling. That reporting boundary matters: it should not be mixed indiscriminately with every trading, distribution or bottling activity associated with a wider group. [5]

Static capacity measures how much can be stored at a time. Annual throughput measures how much passes through during a year. A tank can turn over several times, so the units and period must be kept visible.

Connections influence turnover. A completed tank may not achieve the expected throughput if the jetty, pipeline, rail connection or customer's receiving facilities are not ready. The existence of one asset does not prove that the entire route is operating.

I would ask what is contracted, who pays, what capacity is reserved and what happens if volumes fall. Also account for safety, environmental obligations, maintenance and the investment needed to keep the facility fit for its cargo.

12. Why is EBITDA not the cash available to shareholders?

Ports, vessels and terminals are capital-intensive. Depreciation is non-cash in the current period, but the underlying asset eventually needs maintenance, renewal or replacement.

EBITDA also comes before interest and tax, and it does not capture changes in working capital or capital spending. A business can report growing EBITDA while spending more cash than it generates.

In a hypothetical year, operating cash after working-capital changes is ₹100 crore, maintenance capex is ₹20 crore and expansion capex is ₹110 crore. Cash after those investments is negative ₹30 crore before any further financing flows. The expansion might be worthwhile, but it still requires funding.

Concession length and remaining rights matter too. A facility with a limited remaining contract cannot be valued as though the same cash flow continues forever without renewal costs or conditions.

I would compare returns on the total capital committed, including projects still ramping. Integration, expansion and acquisitions create value only when their eventual returns compensate for the money and risk involved.

13. What would I keep in the research file?

Keep historical results, current disclosed facts and future ambitions in separate columns. A large capex plan does not establish that capacity has been commissioned, and commissioning does not establish full utilisation.

I would gain confidence when useful assets attract repeat business, earn sensible returns and fund their obligations. I would be more cautious when growth depends on peak rates, perfect project timing or assumptions that every new asset immediately fills.

The best starting point is simple: follow the cargo to understand the service, follow the contract to understand the revenue, and follow the cash to understand the return.
