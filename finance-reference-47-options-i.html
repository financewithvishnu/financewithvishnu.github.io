Chapter 47. Options I: Payoffs and Basic Strategies

Chapter 47 of 85 · about 20 minutes

A future binds both parties; an option binds only one. That asymmetry, the right to walk away, is the most useful and the most mis-sold idea in the derivatives market. Used as designed, an option is an insurance policy with a quoted premium. Used as most Indian retail traders use it, it is a lottery ticket with a stock-exchange code. This chapter is about the design: payoffs, the language of strikes and premiums, and the three basic strategies that cover most legitimate needs.

47.1What are call and put options?

A call is the right to buy at a fixed price; a put is the right to sell at a fixed price. The buyer pays a premium upfront and in exchange owns a one-sided payoff: the call buyer profits without limit as the price rises and loses only the premium as it falls; the put buyer, mirror-image. The seller (or "writer") collects the premium and accepts the mirror-image obligation: small certain income now, potentially large payment later. Keep the two archetypes in mind. The option buyer is the insured: pays rent, owns the tail. The option seller is the insurance company: collects rent, carries the tail. Both are honourable businesses. What is not a business is buying options because they are cheap, the way most retail money does it, because cheap things that expire worthless in a fortnight are expensive per unit of hope.

47.2How do option payoffs work: strike, premium and expiry?

Four words define every option: the underlying, the strike (the fixed transaction price), the expiry, and the premium (the price of the right). An option whose right is already worth exercising is in the money; at the current price, at the money; worthless if exercised now, out of the money. The payoff chart below shows the two fundamental shapes, a Nifty 24,000 call bought for ₹180 and a 24,000 put bought for ₹150, profit or loss at expiry against the index level. Read them until they are reflexes: the buyer's line is flat at the loss of the premium on one side and climbs on the other; the kink sits at the strike; the flat segment is what the premium bought, the right to stop losing.

A worked reading: the index expires at 24,500. The call pays 500, profit 320 after the premium; the put expires worthless, loss the full 150. At 23,800 the call loses 180 and the put pays 200, profit 50. Notice that the put buyer was right about direction in the second outcome and still made almost nothing, because the premium is a hurdle the market must clear before the buyer earns a rupee. Chapter 48 prices that hurdle.

47.3What are covered calls, protective puts and spreads?

Three combinations cover nearly every sane use. A protective put is stock plus a put: portfolio insurance with the premium as the explicit cost and the strike as the deductible. A covered call is stock plus a sold call: you rent out the upside beyond the strike, collecting premiums in quiet markets in exchange for capping your best months. A spread buys one option and sells another of the same type: a bull call spread, buying the 24,000 call and selling the 24,500 call, cuts the premium by more than half in exchange for capping the gain, turning "insurance plus lottery" into a clean, bounded bet on a range. The design principle is constant: every option you sell pays you for a risk you have decided you can afford; every option you buy costs you for a risk you have decided you cannot. Strategies are just those two decisions, combined.

47.4How are options settled in India: European exercise and cash settlement?

Two settlement facts shape Indian options. Index options, on the Nifty, Bank Nifty and the rest, are European (exercisable only at expiry, which matters little since they can be sold any day) and cash-settled, the difference paid in rupees, no shares moving. Stock options and futures have been physically settled since SEBI's phased move in 2019: hold an in-the-money stock option to expiry and shares change hands, which means a naked position can suddenly require delivery money many times the margin, a trap that has ambushed more than one retail trader in expiry week. The practical rules follow: know the settlement before you hold past the last few days; expect liquidity to migrate to expiry day, when Indian index volumes peak; and treat expiry-week gamma, the violent sensitivity of near-dead options, as a professional's arena.

47.5What is the difference between index options and stock options?

Index options are the mass market: deep liquidity, tight spreads, weekly expiries, and no single-stock surprises, since an index cannot be suspended, caught in fraud or halted on results. Stock options are the specialist's tool: wider spreads, richer premiums, event risk around earnings, and the physical-settlement obligation. One honest statistic belongs in every chapter on options: SEBI's 2024 study of individual F&O traders found that about 93% lost money over FY22-FY24, with average losses around ₹2 lakh, and the losses concentrated exactly where the excitement is, in short-dated index options. The instrument that looks like a cheap leveraged bet on direction is, in aggregate, a transfer from retail buyers to institutions and sellers. That is not an argument against options; it is an argument for using them as this chapter teaches, as insurance and income against holdings, where the premium's drag is the known cost of a defined benefit, rather than as a directional lottery.

47.6Worked examples and cases

Case 1: insuring a stock holding with a put

You hold 500 shares of a company at ₹2,000, ₹10 lakh, and worry about the quarter but not the decade. You buy 500 of the three-month 1,900 puts at ₹35, costing ₹17,500, 1.75% of the holding. Now the floor: whatever happens, you can sell at 1,900, so your worst outcome is (1,900 − 35) = ₹1,865 per share, a bounded loss of 6.75% including the premium, against an unbounded loss uninsured. If the quarter is fine, the puts expire worthless and the ₹17,500 is gone, which you record the way you record your house insurance premium: the price of sleeping. Two judgment calls were embedded. The strike set the deductible, deeper out of the money is cheaper cover for bigger disasters only. And the cost recurs: insure a portfolio every quarter at roughly 1.5-2% and you spend 6-8% a year, which is why permanent insurance is a drag and selective insurance, around events that genuinely scare you, is the craft.

Case 2: the real cost of a covered call

You own a stock at ₹1,000 and, unimpressed by near-term prospects, sell the one-month 1,050 call for ₹20. If the stock dozes at 990, you keep the ₹20, a 2% monthly yield on a dead asset, and feel clever. Do this for a year of dozing and the premiums stack impressively. Then the month comes when the stock gaps to ₹1,200 on a re-rating: your upside stops at 1,050 plus the premium, ₹70 of gain against the ₹200 the unhedged holder banks, and the year's cleverness is returned in one stroke. This is the covered call's true trade: it systematically sells the right tail, the rare months that drive most of equity returns, for a steady rent. That can still be worth doing, on a stock you would happily sell at the strike anyway, with eyes open about what is being sold. It is not free income. Nothing in Part VII is.
