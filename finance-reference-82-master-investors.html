Chapter 82. Master Investors and Their Methods

Chapter 82 of 85 · about 20 minutes

The masters disagree about everything except the things that matter: temperament over intellect, price versus value, and the discipline to do nothing most of the time. This chapter distils five traditions into their working rules, and then does the honest thing, applies two of them, checklist and questions, to one real style of company, so you can see where the methods agree, conflict and transfer.

82.1What is the Buffett and Munger investing method?

Berkshire's method, compressed: buy wonderful companies at fair prices, then hold them for decades while compounding does the heavy lifting. The working rules. Circle of competence: only businesses you can genuinely understand, the boundary matters more than the size. Moat: a durable advantage, brand, network, switching cost, scale, that protects returns on capital from competition; Munger's contribution was making quality the first filter rather than price. Owner earnings: cash the business truly generates after maintaining its position, not reported profit. Management: able and honest, because you are their silent partner for decades. And the temperament rules that carry the rest: the twenty-slot punch card, treat lifetime decisions as scarce; the fat pitch, wait indefinitely for the obvious one; and Mr Market, whose daily moods exist to serve you, never to instruct you. Note what is absent: no macro forecasting, no trading, no diversification into the mediocre for its own sake.

82.2How did Peter Lynch invest in what he understood?

Lynch's edge was attention to ordinary life: the ten-baggers, his word for stocks that return ten times, were found in malls and workplaces before analysts found them in databases. His working system sorted every company into six buckets, slow growers, stalwarts, fast growers, cyclicals, turnarounds, asset plays, because each type is bought, held and sold by different rules: you hold a stalwart for 30 to 50% and rotate, you hold a fast grower for years, you buy a cyclical at high P/Es (trough earnings) and sell at low ones. His metrics were pragmatic: PEG, the P/E divided by growth rate, below one interesting, above two expensive; debt on the balance sheet as the survival variable in turnarounds; and the two-minute drill, the requirement to state in two minutes, to a child, why you own the thing. The catch he himself named: his method required relentless legwork and a professional's access; the transferable part is the classification and the two-minute drill, not the fund-tour lifestyle.

82.3What is Benjamin Graham’s margin of safety?

The founder of the whole discipline, and still the cleanest statement of it. Three ideas carry everything. Mr Market, prices as a moody partner's daily offers, to be used or ignored. Margin of safety: buy materially below conservative value, so that errors, bad luck and the unknown are prepaid; Graham wanted price at two-thirds of value or less, and this single concept is the ancestor of every risk control in this website. The defensive checklist, his mechanical rules for the non-professional: adequate size, current ratio above 2, debt below equity, unbroken earnings and dividends for ten and twenty years, ten-year EPS growth of at least a third, P/E under 15, P/B under 1.5, or their product under 22.5. Graham's deep lesson is philosophical: the future is not knowable, so price must carry the burden of proof. His formula era passed with the cheap-stock era he operated in, and his student Buffett adapted the philosophy to quality; but the defensive checklist remains the finest crash course in reading a balance sheet with suspicion.

82.4What can you learn from Jhunjhunwala, Damani and India’s market masters?

India's masters prove the methods transfer, with local seasoning. Rakesh Jhunjhunwala: conviction sizing plus trading fluency, enormous concentrated positions in businesses he believed in for decades, Titan most famously, funded by an active trader's feel for cycles; his rule set was confidence in India's growth, respect for valuation discipline even in conviction, and the willingness to be early and lonely. Radhakishan Damani: the quiet operator's path, value investor first, then the builder of DMart, applying the same obsessions, cost, cash, customer value, as an operator; his lesson is that the deepest understanding of a business is owning one. The wider tradition, Pabrai cloning Buffett in Indian mid-caps, the Chennai and Mumbai value circles running Graham screens on the BSE, shows the same pattern: foreign frameworks, Indian patience. What the Indian cases add is governance as a first-class filter, Chapter 81's flags, because in a market with weaker enforcement, the margin of safety must include a margin for the promoter.

82.5Which investing lessons transfer, and what is survivorship bias?

The honest audit. What transfers: margin of safety in some form, temperament as the binding constraint, position sizing matched to conviction and liquidity, the two-minute drill, and the refusal to grade yourself by daily prices. What does not: the specific entry prices of another era, the concentration levels of men with unique access and asymmetric information, and the implied promise that studying the winners reproduces them. For every Jhunjhunwala a thousand equally confident investors concentrated into the wrong story and vanished from the record; the masters' biographies are written by survival. The synthesis this website recommends: Graham for the defence, the balance-sheet checklist that keeps you solvent; Buffett and Munger for the offence, the moat and quality filters that let winners run; Lynch for the process, classification and the drill; and the index fund of Chapter 75 as the honest default for the hours you do not have. The masters' real common inheritance is not a formula but a posture: independent, patient, price-conscious, and quietly aware of their own limits.

82.6Worked example: two masters grade one company

Case: the Graham checklist and Buffett's questions applied to one listed consumer company

Take an illustrative large listed Indian consumer company, EPS ₹100, price ₹4,200, P/E 42, P/B 11, current ratio 1.9, debt-to-equity 0.05, unbroken profits and dividends for over twenty years, ten-year EPS growth of 9.5% a year, that is 148% cumulatively, comfortably above Graham's one-third bar. Graham grades it: safety tests pass, size, liquidity, negligible debt, earnings record; price tests fail completely, P/E 42 against his ceiling of 15, P/B 11 against 1.5, product 462 against 22.5. His formula, V = EPS x (8.5 + 2g) x 4.4/Y with g = 9.5 and the bond yield Y = 7.2, returns a value near ₹1,681, the price is 2.5 times that, and with his two-thirds margin-of-safety rule he would not look twice above ₹1,126. Verdict: wonderful company, uninvestable price, the defensive investor passes. Buffett grades it: inside the circle of competence, yes; moat, brands, distribution reach, pricing power through inflation, yes; owner earnings real and growing; management honest by the record. His question is only whether a 2.4% earnings yield against a 7.2% bond yield can compound acceptably, and his answer would be a watchlist entry and a prayer for a panic, 2020 offered exactly that entry, at roughly half today's price. The synthesis: the same facts, two legitimate verdicts, because Graham buys insurance against the future and Buffett rents the future's growth. The reader's job is to know which game their portfolio is playing before the facts arrive.
