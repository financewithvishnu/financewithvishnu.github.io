Wood panels: the tree, the freight and the factory

By Finance With Vishnu · Source period: approximately July 2025 · Exact class date unconfirmed

Every wardrobe, kitchen cabinet and office desk in India starts as a flat board. The company behind that board has planted or bought trees years earlier, hauled logs across states, pressed them into panels and pushed them through a dealer network to a carpenter. It is a business where the raw material grows slowly, the product is heavy and cheap per kilo, and the factory arrives in one giant lump.

Three features follow. Timber procurement decides cost and security of supply. Freight decides how far a plant can sensibly sell. And capacity arrives in lumps, so margins swing with utilisation while a new plant fills. Follow the tree, the freight and the factory, and the financial statements explain themselves.

1. What does a panel company actually sell?

Four related but distinct products. Plywood is layers of veneer glued into a board, the traditional Indian choice. MDF, medium density fibreboard, is wood fibre and resin pressed into a smooth, uniform panel suited to modular furniture. Particleboard is the cheaper cousin made from wood particles. Laminates and other surfaces are the decorative skin pasted on top. Each has its own raw material, machinery, customer and margin structure.

Century Plyboards' FY2024-25 report gives one company's mix: plywood contributed 56% of revenue, MDF 23%, particleboard 3%, with laminates and other products making up the rest, and plywood volumes grew 14% to 4,16,476 cubic metres.[1] Rushil Decor sits at another point on the ladder: MDF boards were 75.55% of its FY2024-25 revenue and laminates 22.64%.[4] Greenpanel is almost pure MDF, India's largest manufacturer with 8,91,000 cubic metres of annual MDF capacity and a company-cited 21% share by capacity.[2] The mix decides which economics the company lives by.

2. Where does the timber come from?

Increasingly, from farms, not forests. Century reports that approximately 98% of its timber comes through plantation-based agroforestry, with more than 50% of procurement from FSC-certified sources, and that it has diversified by importing plywood timber from countries such as Vietnam.[1] Farmers grow fast-rotation species on short cycles, sell to collection networks, and the panel company aggregates thousands of small lots into plant-scale supply.

That supply is neither smooth nor guaranteed. Greenpanel's FY2024-25 report notes that its working capital investment rose by 8 days because it deliberately built wood inventory to mitigate supply risks.[2] Timber is a crop with a growing season, bought from an unorganised supplier base, in competition with other plants and other uses. The company that secures wood cheaply and steadily has solved the first half of the business.

3. Why is timber procurement a moat?

Because wood is heavy and every kilometre costs money, the advantage goes to the plant sitting inside the plantation belt. Greenply's report makes the logic explicit while describing its new Odisha plywood facility: by virtue of being proximate to plantation resources, the logistics costs will be lower, and the combination of scale, sophistication and proximity should make the plant competitive from day one.[3] Location is a permanent cost advantage that a rival cannot buy except by building nearby and waiting for the plantations.

Procurement depth also builds over years: relationships with farmers and aggregators, grading skill, credit terms and collection infrastructure. A new entrant can order a press line from Europe in months; it cannot order a timber catchment. When studying a panel company, the map of plants against plantation regions tells you more about durable cost position than the machine list.

4. How do plywood and MDF economics differ?

Plywood is an assembly business: veneers peeled or sliced from logs, dried, glued and pressed, with significant manual labour and quality grading. MDF is a process business: a continuous line chips wood, refines fibre, blends resin and presses board around the clock. The plywood plant's cost moves with veneer and labour; the MDF plant's cost moves with resin, energy and, above all, how hard the line runs.

The financial signatures differ accordingly. Greenply's plywood segment grew a steady 8.1% in FY2024-25, while its new Vadodara MDF plant generated Rs 530 crore in its first full year, selling about 1,68,000 cubic metres against a capacity of 2,40,000, with a stated peak revenue potential of Rs 700 to 800 crore.[3] A plywood franchise compounds steadily; an MDF plant's economics are dominated by the climb up the utilisation curve. Comparing the two on one margin line confuses a habit with a ramp.

5. Why does freight shape the industry?

Panels are bulky, heavy and low in value per tonne, so transport cost eats the margin quickly as distance grows. Plants sell most naturally into their own region, and national players are really networks of regional plants. Greenpanel's FY2024-25 report shows the international version: export volumes dropped because of higher ocean freight and wood costs, with reduced container availability and a significant rise in ocean freight rates hurting the MDF export business.[2] The same arithmetic that blocked exports governs domestic selling radius.

6. Why does capacity arrive in lumps?

You cannot build half a press. An MDF line comes as one large, indivisible investment, and a plywood unit still arrives as a full plant. Century operationalised a new MDF plant in Badvel, Andhra Pradesh, doubling its MDF capacity to 6,27,000 cubic metres.[1] Greenpanel is adding 2,31,000 cubic metres at its Andhra Pradesh plant on top of 8,91,000.[2] Greenply commissioned 2,40,000 cubic metres of MDF at Vadodara in one go.[3] Each addition is a step, not a slope, and the step lands on the cost base immediately.

7. What does utilisation do to margins?

Everything, for the capital-heavy products. Depreciation, interest, maintenance and salaried supervision run whether the line runs or not, so each additional cubic metre spreads the fixed base thinner. Greenply's Vadodara plant ran at roughly 70% of capacity in its first full year, 1,68,000 cubic metres sold against 2,40,000 of capacity.[3] Greenpanel talks of lifting plywood capacity utilisation to 80 to 85%.[2] These percentages are the difference between a plant that earns and a plant that waits.

This is why panel companies celebrate volume growth even in flat pricing years, and why a new plant's early losses are not automatically a mistake. The study question is the ramp's credibility: how fast did past plants fill, how much unserved demand sits within economic freight distance, and what price concessions were needed to buy the volume?

8. Why did FY2024-25 squeeze margins?

All four reports tell the same story from different seats. Century's EBITDA fell 12% to Rs 497 crore even as consolidated revenue rose 16.5% to Rs 4,528 crore, and consolidated profit before tax dropped 36.9%.[1] Greenpanel's revenue fell to Rs 1,436 crore from Rs 1,567 crore and profit after tax nearly halved to Rs 72 crore, with MDF EBITDA margin at 11.6%, hit by competitive price reductions, intense domestic competition and rising raw material prices.[2] Rushil Decor's EBITDA slipped 3.7% to Rs 90.1 crore on inflationary and market pressures, though profit after tax rose 12% to Rs 48.31 crore.[4] Only Greenply grew both revenue and EBITDA, helped by its new MDF volumes.[3]

The shared ingredients: timber and resin cost inflation, ocean freight on the export leg, and price competition as fresh capacity chased the same demand. The lesson is that this industry can grow volumes and shrink margins at the same time. Volume, price and cost must be read as three separate lines, because the FY2024-25 story is exactly their divergence.

9. What role does the unorganised sector play?

A large one, and it is the branded players' opportunity and ceiling at once. Local, unbranded plywood and board makers compete almost purely on price, and Greenpanel's report lists the unorganised market among its principal challenges.[2] Century's report frames the counter-trend: a steady transition from unorganised to branded products, with organised players gaining share.[1] Branded share gains are real, but the price umbrella the unorganised sector holds over the market caps what brands can charge.

For the analyst, the practical reading is twofold. First, premiumisation and brand-led growth are multi-year tailwinds, not quarter-to-quarter facts. Second, in a weak year the unorganised sector does not defend price, so branded realisations face downward pressure precisely when costs rise, which is part of what FY2024-25 shows.

10. Are laminates and surfaces a different game?

Yes, closer to fashion than to timber. A laminate sells design, texture and brand more than wood content, and the cost structure follows printing, décor paper and distribution rather than logs. Rushil's report describes laminates as design- and décor-oriented products needing continuous new design development, and its growth investment is a jumbo-size laminate facility of about 1.2 million sheets a year in Gujarat aimed primarily at export markets.[4] Size and design, not fibre cost, are the differentiators there.

Surfaces also attach to the panel sale: the customer buying board usually needs the laminate too, so companies cross-sell through the same dealer network. When a panel maker reports surfaces revenue, ask whether it is earning a design premium or merely bundling volume. The margin gap between the two is the whole question.

11. What does distribution add?

The last competitive layer. Panels reach furniture through dealers, distributors, carpenters and increasingly OEMs and e-commerce, and the brand that the carpenter trusts gets specified. Century's 14% plywood volume growth in a difficult year, and Rushil's emphasis on strengthening distribution networks through disruption, both point to the channel as the demand engine.[1][4] Distribution is slow to build, expensive to maintain and invisible in a cost breakdown, which is exactly why it protects margins once built.

Channel breadth also decides how a new plant ramps. A factory with an existing dealer network behind it, as with Greenply's Vadodara MDF feeding an established plywood franchise, fills faster than a greenfield brand. When a company announces capacity, check the distribution that will sell it, not just the machine that will make it.

12. What can go wrong?

Start with the input. Timber, resin and chemicals are volatile, and Greenpanel's report flags exactly these alongside fuel and transportation cost escalation.[2] A plantation crop cannot be ramped in a season, so a supply squeeze raises wood costs for years, not weeks. Import dependence adds currency and trade-policy risk on the other side.

Then capacity itself. The industry's announced additions, including Century's doubling, Greenpanel's 2,31,000 cubic metres and Greenply's Vadodara line, all chase the same demand pool.[1][2][3] If everyone ramps together, the price cuts of FY2024-25 repeat. Exports offer a relief valve, but ocean freight and South-East Asian competition can close it, as Greenpanel's export decline shows.[2] The risks compound: the same year can bring cost inflation, price competition and a freight spike, because they share a logistics root.

13. What should the tracking sheet explain?

My tracking sheet for a panel company would answer, with dates: What is the product mix across plywood, MDF, particleboard and surfaces, and which economics dominate? Where do plants sit relative to plantation belts, and what share of timber is plantation-grown, certified or imported? What did wood and resin costs do this year, and what inventory of wood is being carried? How much capacity exists by product, what was added, and at what utilisation is each plant running? How did realisations, volumes and margins each move, separately? What did freight, domestic and ocean, do to the numbers? How much revenue comes from exports, and what blocked or helped them? How strong is the dealer and distribution network behind any new capacity? And what does the unorganised sector's pricing do to realisations in weak quarters?

None of this requires predicting timber prices or furniture demand. It records whether the business is understood well enough that a margin squeeze, a slow ramp or a freight spike would be noticed and read correctly. That record is the real output of the study.
