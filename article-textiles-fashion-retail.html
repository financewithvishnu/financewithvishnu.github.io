Textiles and fashion retail: the maker, the brand and the store

By Finance With Vishnu · Source period: November 2024 · Company data through: March 2026

A shirt passes through three very different businesses before it reaches a wardrobe. One company spins, weaves and stitches it for a price per piece negotiated down to the paisa. Another designs it, owns the name on the label, and buys the stitching from the first. A third owns the store and decides what the shopper finally pays. Each business earns a different margin on the same garment, and confusing them is the most common mistake in reading this sector.

FY2025-26 shows all three at work in India: a garment exporter that absorbed an estimated Rs 97 crore of US tariffs while earning about eleven paise of operating profit per rupee, a celebration-wear brand earning a 66% gross margin on nearly flat sales, and a retailer that added 289 stores and grew revenue 17%. Follow the maker, the brand and the store.

Source period: November 2024. Company data through: March 2026. FY2025-26 annual reports provide dated checks. They are not current data. This is education, not investment advice.

What does a fashion company actually sell?

Three answers, three industries. The garment manufacturer sells capacity: sewing lines, compliance certificates and reliable delivery to a global buyer's calendar. The brand sells meaning: a name that lets a groom's family pay three times the fabric cost for a wedding sherwani. The retailer sells access: a store network, a data feed on what is selling, and the ability to put fresh stock in front of millions of shoppers every week.

Each layer keeps a different slice of the final price, and the slices are not equal. In FY2025-26, Gokaldas Exports, one of India's largest garment manufacturers, earned an EBITDA margin of roughly 11% after absorbing steep US tariffs. Vedant Fashions, owner of Manyavar, earned 44.3% before interest, tax, depreciation and rent-equivalent costs. Trent, the Tata retailer behind Westside and Zudio, earned about 14% on a standalone base growing at 18%. Same garment chain, margins four times apart.

The first question about any textile or fashion company is therefore not "is it a good company?" It is "which layer does it actually occupy, and who in its chain has the power?"

Where does the margin live along the chain?

The fibre and fabric stage behaves like any commodity process industry: capital-heavy, spread-driven, judged on utilisation. The making stage is a service business with thin, defensible margins. The brand stage is where pricing power begins, because the buyer is no longer comparing fabric costs. The store stage converts all of it into cash, and it is where the sector's great operational skill lives: deciding what to buy, how much, and when to cut the price.

Notice what each layer fears. The maker fears an idle line and a demanding buyer. The brand fears a label that stops meaning something. The store fears a rack of clothes nobody wants at full price. That last fear has a name, and it is the sector's most important number.

Why is inventory the heartbeat of this sector?

A cement plant's inventory is limestone and clinker; it will be worth about the same next quarter. A fashion company's inventory is this season's taste, and it depreciates like fish. Every rack of unsold kurtas is cash stitched into cloth, losing value by the week, until the only way to convert it back is a discount that eats the margin the product was supposed to earn.

This is why the sector's professionals talk about inventory before they talk about sales. A retailer can grow revenue 20% and still destroy value if it bought 40% more stock to get there. The income statement shows the sales; the balance sheet shows what it cost to make them. Read them together or you have read neither.

The metric that ties them together is selling through: of everything the company bought or made for a season, how much sold at or near full price, and how fast?

Sell-through is the share of a season's stock that finds a buyer within the season, ideally at full price. A high sell-through means the buying team read demand correctly: little is left to mark down, the gross margin survives, and the next season's orders can be fresh instead of corrective. A low sell-through forces the end-of-season sale, where the store recovers cash but donates its margin to the shopper.

Every business model in this sector is, at bottom, a different technology for improving sell-through. Fast fashion answers with speed: small batches, constant newness, reorder only what the data confirms. Celebration wear answers with occasion: a wedding outfit is bought for a date, not a discount, so demand holds at full price. Value retail answers with price itself: start cheap enough that the markdown is rarely needed.

When you read a fashion result, the sell-through question is always the same: did the margin come from selling well, or from selling cheap?

What does the garment maker earn?

Gokaldas Exports is the cleanest Indian window into the making layer. It runs more than 30,000 machines across factories in India, with an expanding footprint in Africa through its Atraco acquisition, and produces garments for global brands who design and sell under their own labels. Its product is reliability: the right piece, at the right cost, on the buyer's ship date, from factories that pass the buyer's compliance audits.

FY2025-26 was a tariff year: consolidated revenue from operations rose 3.2% to about Rs 3,988 crore. Reported EBITDA was about Rs 434 crore, a 10.7% margin, after absorbing an estimated Rs 97 crore of US reciprocal tariffs; adjusted for that hit, EBITDA was about Rs 530 crore, a 13.0% margin. Consolidated profit after tax fell to about Rs 100 crore from Rs 159 crore. That margin is not a failure; it is the shape of the layer. The buyer holds the brand and the alternative factories, so the maker keeps a service margin, wins on scale, efficiency and execution, and wears the trade policy.

The maker's upside is real but earned slowly: volume growth, better product mix, vertical integration into fabric, and operating leverage as new plants fill. The maker's risks are just as structural: wage inflation the buyer will not fund, a rupee that moves the wrong way, a tariff in the destination market, and the buyer's own inventory cycles, which hit the factory a quarter before they hit the store.

Why does Trent source but not make?

Trent sits at the opposite end. It owns almost no garment factories; it designs and curates its own labels, buys production from a vendor base, and sells entirely through its own stores and sites. Its FY2025-26 annual report describes the model as "quintessentially direct-to-consumer", meaning no wholesale, no franchise dilution of the margin or the data.

The economics follow from the structure. Because Trent owns the customer relationship and the shelf, it captures the brand margin and the retail margin together. Because it owns no factories, its capital goes into stores and systems instead of sewing lines, and its manufacturing risk sits with vendors it can replace. The trade is that it must be an excellent buyer: every garment on its racks was chosen in advance, and the markdown bin is where bad choices surface.

Consolidated FY2025-26 revenue was about Rs 20,074 crore, up 17.2%, and profit after tax was about Rs 1,721 crore against Rs 1,534 crore a year earlier. The standalone operating EBITDA margin was 13.64%, and the company opened 60 Westside and 212 Zudio stores in the year, taking the two formats to 300 and 963 stores.

What does Zudio's store count tell you?

Zudio is Trent's value-fashion format, and its footprint is the sector's clearest scale story: 765 stores across 235 cities as of March 2025, up from 545 a year earlier, alongside Westside's 248 stores in 86 cities. A store a day, roughly, is what value fashion at full speed looks like.

The model behind the count is deliberate. Very sharp price points make the full-price sale the default, so the business is less hostage to the markdown cycle. Own labels keep the sourcing margin inside the company. A small-format, high-throughput store turns inventory faster than a department store can. The risk is that value retail is a volume treadmill: same-store growth and store economics must stay healthy as the base triples, because a format that works at 200 stores can behave differently at 765.

The annual report's own language is instructive: both formats are described as seeing "strong volume-led growth". Volume, not price, is the engine; watch that it stays so.

Why does Manyavar run on franchise stores?

Vedant Fashions chose a different machine for a different product. Manyavar sells wedding and celebration wear, a category where the purchase is planned, the date is fixed, and the shopper is comparatively price-insensitive. The company designs and markets the brands, sources production, and reaches customers through a network of 678 stores including shop-in-shop outlets, most of them franchisee-owned, spanning 1.79 million square feet across 244 Indian cities plus 16 international stores.

The franchise structure is a capital-light way to own the brand margin without owning every store. The franchisee funds the shop; the company supplies the label, the stock and the system. FY2025-26 shows what the structure produces at maturity: revenue of about Rs 1,435 crore, up 3.5% in a still-soft year for celebration demand, with a gross margin of 65.7%, an EBITDA margin of 44.3% and a profit-after-tax margin of 26.2%. Profit after tax was about Rs 376 crore, down from Rs 388 crore, as soft demand nibbled at the richest margins in the chain.

That is the brand layer's signature: even when the top line barely moves, the economics per garment stay extraordinary, because the customer is paying for what the garment means at a wedding, not what it cost to sew. The vulnerability is the mirror image: demand is concentrated in one occasion and one culture, so the brand must keep widening, into women's wear, into newer labels, into new geographies, to keep the growth story alive.

What does premiumisation do to the P&L?

Across Indian apparel, the shopper is trading up: from unbranded to branded, from utility to occasion, from one wedding outfit to a wardrobe of them. Premiumisation raises the average selling price faster than volumes, which is why branded and ethnic wear keep outpacing the market they sit in. Aditya Birla's fashion business, for instance, grew its ethnic portfolio 14% in FY2025-26 to about Rs 2,227 crore, with segment EBITDA margin up 560 basis points to 10.8%, even while the wider branded-apparel market grew modestly.

For the P&L, premiumisation is a gift with a condition. The gift: higher price points lift gross margin without needing more footfall. The condition: premium stock is slower stock. A Rs 40,000 sherwani sells fewer pieces per week than a Rs 499 t-shirt, and the inventory risk per unsold unit is proportionally larger. Premiumisation raises the stakes of the sell-through question, it does not retire it.

So read a premiumising company's inventory as carefully as its margin. Rising gross margin with stable inventory turns is genuine pricing power. Rising margin with ballooning inventory is a markdown deferred, not avoided.

How does working capital differ across the three models?

The maker, the brand and the store each carry a different working-capital shape. The garment exporter carries receivables: it ships today and collects from the global buyer in 60 to 90 days, so growth itself consumes cash, and a 62% revenue year strains the balance sheet even when it is profitable. The franchise brand carries inventory in the channel: stock sits at franchisee stores, and the quality of that stock decides next season's orders. The direct retailer carries inventory on its own books but collects cash at the till the same day.

These shapes create different warning signs. For the maker, watch receivable days stretching, because it can mean the buyer is slowing payment or the maker is extending terms to win volume. For the brand, watch inventory and franchisee health, because a network stuffed with unsold stock stops reordering. For the retailer, watch inventory days against store count: stock growing faster than stores is the earliest tell of a buying miss.

How should you read a fashion annual report?

Two habits make these checks useful. First, always pair the income statement with the balance sheet: revenue and margin are claims, inventory and receivables are evidence. Second, separate the year from the model: a single soft season does not break a brand, and a single strong season does not fix a maker's structural margin.

What can break the economics?

Each layer breaks differently. The maker breaks on concentration and cost: one buyer walking away, wages rising faster than prices, a tariff in the destination market, a rupee that strengthens at the wrong moment. The brand breaks on meaning: a label that over-extends, discounts too often, or loses its occasion loses the pricing power that was the whole business. The store breaks on stock: a couple of badly bought seasons, and the markdown cycle eats the year's margin while expansion costs keep running.

There are shared risks too. Cotton and crude-linked fibres move input costs for everyone, and pass-through is easiest at the brand layer and hardest at the making layer. Fashion risk is contagious: a misread season hits the maker's next order book a quarter after it hits the retailer's rack. And every layer is one ambitious competitor away from margin pressure, because in apparel the consumer's loyalty is always provisional.

The mitigants are structural: diversified buyers and geographies for the maker, occasions and repeat franchise economics for the brand, disciplined buying and fast data for the store. None removes the cycle; they decide who keeps earning through it.

What should you remember?

Textiles and fashion is not one sector but a chain of three businesses stitched together. The maker earns a thin service margin on volume and reliability. The brand earns a fat margin on meaning, and pays for it with marketing and occasion risk. The store earns the spread between sourcing and selling, and pays for it with inventory risk that never sleeps. FY2025-26 priced the layers cleanly: about 11% operating margin for the exporter even after absorbing US tariffs, 44.3% for the celebration-wear brand, and 17% growth for the retailer that owns its customer end to end.

So when any company in this space reports, first place it on the chain. Then ask the layer's own question: for the maker, whose orders filled the lines; for the brand, did the price hold; for the store, what sold through at full price and what is left on the rack. The garment is the same. The economics never are.
