Paper: the fibre, the grade and the capital cycle

By Finance With Vishnu · Source period: approximately October 2024 · Exact class dates unconfirmed

Paper looks like one commodity and is actually several businesses bolted together. One paper company grows trees and waits six years for its raw material. Another runs its machines on sugarcane residue that a sugar mill almost pays it to take away. A third buys waste paper by the truckload and washes it back into newsprint. Each is a different answer to the sector's first question: where does your fibre come from, and what does that decision cost you every single year?

FY2024-25 put the whole structure on display in India: a year of rising wood costs, a surge of cheap imports, and selling prices that fell even as volumes held. The largest branded paper maker earned a 14.6% operating margin, a bagasse-based mill earned nearly 12% and kept almost none of it after interest and depreciation, and a recycled-fibre mill earned under 8%. Follow the fibre, the grade and the capital cycle.

What does a paper company actually sell?

A sheet of paper is fibre, water, energy and chemicals pressed into a grade. The grade is the product: office copier paper, school notebooks, the board that becomes a pharma carton, the kraft that becomes a shipping box, the newsprint a daily is printed on. Each grade has its own customers, its own price cycle and its own quality bar, but they all come off variations of the same enormous machine.

That machine is the heart of the economics. A paper mill is among the most capital-intensive factories in manufacturing: digesters or pulping lines, a paper machine hundreds of metres long, a power plant, an effluent plant. It earns only when it runs near full, because depreciation and interest run whether or not paper comes off the reel. So the paper business is really two businesses: a fibre procurement system feeding a fixed-cost machine, and a grade portfolio that decides what the machine's output is worth.

Every question in this sector hangs off one of those two businesses.

Why is fibre the first fork?

The fibre decision is slow to make and nearly impossible to reverse. A company that builds around plantation wood is committing to forestry: JK Paper's model rests on farm forestry programmes that put trees on private land near its plants, securing supply but exposing it to the rising price of wood as more mills chase the same plantations. A company built on bagasse, like TNPL, is wedded to the sugar economy of its state. A recovered-fibre mill like Emami Paper is tied to the waste-paper trade, where domestic collection never quite meets demand and imported waste paper fills the gap at a price set in dollars.

None of these is simply better. Virgin fibre gives quality and certification but carries a rising, politically sensitive cost. Bagasse is cheap and renewable but seasonal and regional. Recovered fibre is circular and cheap but caps the product range. The fibre route is the mill's destiny; read it first.

How do grades decide the price cycle?

Writing and printing paper serves education and offices, a mature demand pool growing slowly. Packaging board rides consumption: FMCG cartons, pharma boxes, e-commerce shippers, food service. Newsprint is in structural decline worldwide. Kraft and containerboard track the corrugated-box economy. A mill's grade mix decides which of these cycles it lives on, and the grade mix is hard to change quickly because a machine tuned for copier paper does not casually become a board machine.

FY2024-25 showed the divergence. Demand for packaging board kept growing with consumer goods and e-commerce, while writing and printing stayed flat and newsprint weak. Companies with board capacity had the better demand story; companies weighted to print grades relied on brand and distribution to hold price. JK Paper, spanning office paper, writing and printing, and packaging boards, could lean on the stronger segments; a single-grade mill has no such lever.

When a paper company announces a capacity expansion, the first question is not how many tonnes. It is which grade, and into what part of that grade's cycle the tonnes will arrive.

Why do imports set the domestic price?

Paper trades globally, and India's paper demand grows faster than its efficient capacity. When international prices soften, or a trade agreement lowers the barrier, imported paper lands at prices domestic mills must match. The domestic mill's selling price is therefore not set by its cost; it is set by the import parity price, and the mill's margin is whatever is left after its own fibre, energy and chemical costs.

FY2024-25 was a textbook year. JK Paper states plainly that profitability and margins moderated because of a sharp rise in wood cost and lower selling prices caused by a surge in cheaper imports. TNPL sold a higher quantity than the previous year and still reported lower revenue, from Rs 4,689.62 crore to Rs 4,490.91 crore, because realisations fell. Emami Paper's volumes improved while sales value declined from Rs 1,993.84 crore to Rs 1,928.04 crore. Three different fibre models, one common outcome: the import price walked in and took the margin.

This is why paper investors watch trade data, anti-dumping petitions and the global pulp price as closely as company results. The domestic story is only half the P&L.

What does integration actually buy?

JK Paper is the sector's integrated benchmark: India's largest producer of branded papers, by its own description, with over 8 lakh tonnes a year of installed capacity across three plants located near plantation wood sources and consumption markets. Integration here means the company touches every step: forestry partnerships, pulping, paper machines, conversion, brands and a distribution network that reaches the stationery shop.

Integration buys two things. Cost control: the company is not bidding for market pulp when the global cycle turns up, because it grows and buys wood through long-standing farm forestry relationships. And price realisation: a branded copier paper sells at a premium to an unbranded reel, and that premium survives import pressure better than commodity grades do.

The FY2024-25 numbers show both the strength and the limit. Revenue grew to Rs 7,120.20 crore, but EBITDA of Rs 1,036.28 crore and net profit of Rs 411.98 crore were well below the exceptional FY2022-23 peak, which the company itself flags as a year it always expected to normalise. Integration cushions the cycle; it does not abolish it.

What is the bagasse model's bargain?

TNPL made a different fibre bet: build the mill on bagasse, the fibrous residue left after sugarcane is crushed, instead of wood. The raw material is renewable, it arrives from sugar mills that need it removed, and it gives the company a genuine sustainability story alongside a cost structure untied from plantation land.

The bargain is the cane economy itself. Bagasse supply follows the sugarcane harvest, which follows monsoon, acreage and state policy. The mill must also handle a fibre that is bulkier and more seasonal than wood, and it competes for that fibre with the sugar mills' own boilers. The model works, and TNPL has run it at scale for decades, but the cost line inherits every shock in the sugar belt.

FY2024-25: revenue from operations of Rs 4,490.91 crore, EBITDA of Rs 525.17 crore. An 11.7% operating margin in a weak pricing year is respectable. What happened next is the real lesson, and it belongs to the capital cycle.

Why does the capital cycle decide what shareholders keep?

Look at TNPL's FY2024-25 waterfall. EBITDA of Rs 525.17 crore. Finance cost of Rs 216.13 crore. Depreciation and amortisation of Rs 303.73 crore. Profit before tax: Rs 5.29 crore. Profit after tax: Rs 3.72 crore, against Rs 208.16 crore the year before. The mill ran, sold more tonnes than the prior year, earned over half a thousand crore at the operating line, and shareholders kept less than four crore.

This is the capital cycle made visible. Paper mills are built with debt and depreciation schedules that run for decades. In the fat years, operating profit outruns the fixed claims and earnings look spectacular, as they did industry-wide in FY2022-23. In lean years, the same fixed claims consume nearly everything. The operating business did not collapse between those years; the price of paper fell toward import parity while costs rose, and the leverage did the arithmetic.

So judge a paper company across a full cycle. Ask what the mill earns at mid-cycle prices, what it owes, and how much of each EBITDA rupee is already spoken for by the bank and the depreciation schedule before the shareholder's claim begins.

How does the recovered-fibre mill work?

Emami Paper Mills runs the third model: recycled fibre in newsprint and eco-certified boards, sold into food service, pharma and e-commerce packaging. Waste paper is cheaper than virgin pulp and carries a circular-economy story customers increasingly ask for. The mill's raw material department is really a collection network: aggregators, sorting, quality control, and imports to bridge the gap between what India's waste chain supplies and what the machines need.

The model's constraint is the fibre itself. Recovered fibre degrades each time it is recycled, so it suits newsprint and board but not premium printing paper. And when waste paper prices rise, or imports become expensive, the cost advantage narrows quickly. FY2024-25 squeezed both ends: sales of Rs 1,928.04 crore on improved volumes, but EBITDA of Rs 147.55 crore, a 7.7% margin, and profit after tax of Rs 26.01 crore, as realisations fell with the import-driven market.

Recovered fibre is a genuine structural position in a country short of fibre. It is not a shield from the price cycle.

Why is energy a hidden second business?

Paper machines are steam and power hungry: cooking fibre, pressing sheets and drying them consumes energy continuously, and it is one of the largest cost lines after fibre. This is why serious mills build captive cogeneration plants, burning bark, process waste or coal to make their own steam and power, and why energy efficiency shows up in annual reports as a strategic metric rather than a footnote.

The energy position quietly separates mills. A plant with efficient captive power and process-waste boilers holds its cost line when grid power and coal prices move. A plant buying power at market rates imports the energy cycle on top of the fibre and price cycles it already carries. When comparing two mills with similar EBITDA margins, check the energy setup before crediting one with better management; sometimes the difference is a boiler, not a manager.

How should you read a paper annual report?

Two habits sharpen the reading. First, normalise: compare this year's margin to a mid-cycle year, not to the best year anyone remembers. Second, separate the fibre story from the price story: a mill can execute perfectly on cost and still report a weak year when import parity sets the price below its cost curve.

What can break the economics?

The risks sit exactly where the model's choices were made. Fibre: wood costs can outrun paper prices for years when plantation supply lags mill demand, and recovered-fibre prices spike with the global waste trade. Price: imports can reprice the domestic market in a single season, as FY2024-25 showed. Capital: a leveraged mill entering a downcycle discovers that the debt does not shrink with the selling price.

Grades carry their own risks. Newsprint's decline is structural, not cyclical. Packaging board's growth invites everyone's new capacity at once, which is how a good demand story becomes a bad price story three years later. And energy and environmental compliance are slow-moving costs that arrive as regulation before they arrive as numbers.

The mitigants are the old ones: controlled fibre, efficient energy, a grade mix with growth in it, a balance sheet sized for the bad years, and the discipline to add capacity into the cycle rather than at its top.

What should you remember?

A paper company is a fibre procurement system feeding a fixed-cost machine, selling into a price it does not control. The fibre route, wood, bagasse or recovered paper, locks in the cost structure for decades. The grade mix chooses which demand cycle the mill rides. Imports set the domestic price, energy decides how much of the margin survives the boiler house, and the debt and depreciation schedule decides what is left for shareholders when the cycle turns.

So when a paper company reports, ask four things. Which fibre, and what did it cost this year? Which grades, and where is their demand heading? What did volumes do versus realisations? And after the bank and the depreciation schedule are paid, what actually remains? In this sector the machine is the moat and the millstone at once; the fibre, the grade and the balance sheet tell you which one is winning.
