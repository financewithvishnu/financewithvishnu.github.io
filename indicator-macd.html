MACD: the indicator that turns two moving averages into momentum

By Finance With Vishnu · Indicators series, Momentum · 22 November 2026

12 · 26 · 9

The three numbers behind the standard MACD. Gerald Appel developed it in the late 1970s, and it is still one of the most popular momentum indicators because it blends trend and momentum in one picture.

12 - 26fast EMA minus slow EMA = the MACD line
9periods in the signal line
No limitsMACD has no top or bottom, unlike RSI

A moving average shows where the price has been. MACD shows whether a fast moving average is pulling away from a slow one or falling back toward it. That gap tells you if the trend is gaining speed or losing it.

Short answer: the MACD line is the 12-period exponential moving average (EMA) minus the 26-period EMA of closing prices. A 9-period EMA of the MACD line is the signal line. The histogram is the gap between the two. Traders watch signal line crossovers, zero-line crossovers and divergences.

1. Who created MACD, and why?

Short answer: Gerald Appel developed MACD in the late 1970s. He wanted a simple way to turn trend-following moving averages into a momentum oscillator.[1]

Moving averages are good at showing trend but slow to react. Momentum tools react faster but do not say much about the trend. Appel's idea was to subtract one moving average from another. The result moves above and below zero like an oscillator, yet it is built from trend-following averages, so StockCharts describes it as offering "the best of both worlds: trend following and momentum".[1]

The histogram came later. Thomas Aspray developed the MACD-Histogram to anticipate signal line crossovers, because moving averages lag and a crossover can arrive late.[3]

2. What exactly is MACD?

Short answer: three pieces. The MACD line (fast EMA minus slow EMA), the signal line (an EMA of the MACD line) and the histogram (MACD line minus signal line).[1][2]

The name describes what the two averages do. Convergence is when they move toward each other. Divergence is when they move apart. The faster 12-period average does most of the moving, and the slower 26-period average reacts less to price changes. When the fast one is above the slow one, the MACD line is positive and upside momentum is increasing as the gap widens. When the fast one is below, it is negative.[1]

MACD has no upper or lower limit, so it is not particularly useful for judging overbought and oversold levels the way RSI is.[1][2]

3. How is MACD calculated?

Short answer: calculate a 12-period EMA and a 26-period EMA of the closes, subtract to get the MACD line, then take a 9-period EMA of that line for the signal line.[1]

An EMA gives more weight to recent prices. Each day: EMA = (close - previous EMA) x multiplier + previous EMA, where the multiplier is 2 / (periods + 1).[4] A shorter period has a bigger multiplier, so the 12-period EMA reacts faster.

A worked example with made-up numbers

The three multipliers: for 12 periods, 2 / (12 + 1) = 0.1538. For 26 periods, 2 / (26 + 1) = 0.0741. For 9 periods, 2 / (9 + 1) = 0.2000.

Say yesterday's 12-period EMA was 100.0, yesterday's 26-period EMA was 99.0, yesterday's signal line was 0.80, and today's close is 102.0.

New 12-period EMA = (102.0 - 100.0) x 0.1538 + 100.0 = 100.308.
New 26-period EMA = (102.0 - 99.0) x 0.0741 + 99.0 = 99.222.
MACD line = 100.308 - 99.222 = 1.085.
New signal line = (1.085 - 0.80) x 0.2 + 0.80 = 0.857.
Histogram = 1.085 - 0.857 = 0.228, positive because the MACD line is above its signal line.

The very first EMA of each length has no yesterday to build on, so it starts from a plain average: the simple average of the first 12 closes for the 12-period EMA, and of the first 26 closes for the 26-period EMA. The signal line starts the same way, from a simple average of the first 9 MACD values.

The values 12, 26 and 9 are the typical settings and they can be changed. A more sensitive version such as 5, 35, 5 may suit weekly charts, while longer averages give fewer and later signals.[1] Because MACD is an actual price difference, a Rs 2,000 stock will show much bigger MACD numbers than a Rs 200 stock. To compare momentum across stocks, StockCharts suggests the Percentage Price Oscillator (PPO) instead.[1]

4. What is a signal line crossover?

Short answer: a bullish crossover is when the MACD line moves up through its signal line. A bearish one is when it moves down through it. It is the most common MACD signal.[1]

Fidelity adds that a crossover is considered stronger the further it happens from zero in the direction of the move: a bullish crossover from well below zero, or a bearish one from well above it.[2] StockCharts warns that crossovers at extremes need caution. A strong move pushes momentum to an extreme, and even if the move continues, momentum usually slows and produces a crossover at that point. In its IBM example, two bearish crossovers came while the stock kept rising.[1]

5. What is a centerline crossover?

Short answer: the MACD line moving above zero means the 12-period EMA has moved above the 26-period EMA. Moving below zero means the opposite.[1]

Centerline crossovers are slower and rarer than signal line crossovers. They can last from a few days to many months, depending on the trend. In a sustained uptrend MACD stays positive. StockCharts shows both sides: in one stock the centerline crossovers led to strong trends, and in another, seven crossovers in five months produced mostly whipsaws because no strong trend followed.[1]

6. What is the histogram?

Short answer: the histogram shows the distance between the MACD line and its signal line. It is positive when MACD is above the signal line and negative when below.[3]

Think of it as the gap closing or opening. When the bars grow, the MACD line is moving away from its signal line and momentum is getting stronger. When the bars shrink toward zero, the two are converging and momentum is fading. Because shrinking bars show up before the lines actually cross, traders watch the histogram for early warning of a signal line crossover.[3] It is an indicator of an indicator, several steps away from price, so it can also be noisy.[3]

7. What is divergence?

Short answer: a bearish divergence is when the price makes a higher high but the MACD line makes a lower high. A bullish divergence is a lower low in price with a higher low in MACD.[1]

A divergence says upward momentum is weaker than before, not that it has turned negative. StockCharts points out that bearish divergences are commonplace in a strong uptrend, because an uptrend often starts with a surge in momentum and then continues at a slower pace. Upside momentum still beats downside momentum while MACD stays above zero. In its SPY example, four bearish divergences came while the uptrend continued.[1] Fidelity notes that a divergence is a stronger signal when it confirms a crossover.[2]

8. What mistakes do beginners make?

  • Trading every crossover. In a sideways market the lines cross back and forth and most signals lose.[2]
  • Using it as an overbought and oversold tool. MACD has no fixed limits, so there is no 70 or 30 to watch.[1]
  • Comparing MACD values across stocks. The numbers depend on the share price.[1]
  • Treating a divergence as a sure reversal. In strong trends divergences repeat while the trend carries on.[1]
  • Ignoring that it lags. It is made from averages of past closes, so signals come after the move has started.

9. How can intraday traders use MACD?

Short answer: as a rough read of short-term momentum, with a clear plan for the many false crossovers that fast charts produce.

MACD works on daily, weekly or monthly charts, and traders also apply it to shorter intraday charts.[1] On a 5-minute chart, the same 12, 26, 9 settings cover only a couple of hours, so crossovers are frequent. A trader may use the histogram to see whether the current push is gaining or losing strength, and avoid taking crossovers when the price is stuck between obvious support and resistance. Costs and slippage add up quickly with frequent signals.

10. How can swing traders use MACD?

Short answer: to join a trend after a pullback, and to spot momentum fading through divergence or a shrinking histogram.

StockCharts' own sample scans give one idea of how a swing filter can be built. The bullish scan looks for stocks above their 200-day moving average with a bullish signal line crossover while MACD is still negative, so the turn up comes after a pullback inside an uptrend. The bearish scan is the mirror image.[1] They are starting points for study, not recommendations. The stop belongs on the price chart.

11. How can long-term investors use MACD?

Short answer: as a slow check on the trend, using weekly or monthly charts, not as a trigger to buy or sell.

On a weekly or monthly chart a centerline crossover can last for many months. StockCharts shows one case where the 12-period EMA stayed above the 26-period EMA for 10 months.[1] An investor can use that kind of reading to see whether the long-term trend is still intact. It cannot say anything about the business behind the stock.

12. What can go wrong?

  • Whipsaws in ranges. The main weakness, shown in the chart below. Users of MACD generally avoid trading in such conditions.[2]
  • Late signals. Moving averages lag price, and crossovers can arrive late and hurt the reward-to-risk of a trade.[3]
  • Unbounded values. You cannot say a given MACD number is high or low without looking at that stock's own history.[1]
  • Misleading divergences. They can repeat many times in strong trends.[1]
  • Price-only. It cannot see earnings, news or liquidity.

13. A simple checklist

  1. Is the stock trending or moving sideways? In a range, skip MACD crossovers.
  2. Is the MACD line above or below zero, so which EMA is on top?
  3. Is the histogram growing or shrinking?
  4. Is there a divergence, and has price confirmed it?
  5. Where are the nearest support and resistance on the price chart?
  6. Where is the stop, and how much would be lost if it is hit?
