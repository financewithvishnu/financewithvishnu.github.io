Chapter 69. Global Markets and Cross-Border Investing

Chapter 69 of 85 · about 19 minutes

India is about 3% of world equity market value, which means a portfolio holding only India owns 3% of the world's businesses and ignores the other 97%, including most of the companies whose products fill its own house. Global diversification is the one free-ish lunch this book keeps serving, and India has spent two decades quietly building the pipes to reach it. This chapter is about the pipes, the costs and the risks.

69.1Why diversify beyond one country

The case is not that India will fail; it is that concentration is a bet you cannot hedge from inside. Country risk is real and undiversifiable domestically: a currency crisis, a policy shock, a decade of stagnation, Japan after 1989 being the standing warning that even a great economy's market can go sideways for a working lifetime. Global exposure buys three things: participation in sectors India barely lists, semiconductors at the frontier, global pharma platforms, the software giants; a currency hedge built into the assets, since foreign equity returns in rupees carry the dollar's strength against the rupee's drift, as Chapter 58 computed; and smoother sequencing, different countries' cycles offsetting, so that the bad decade in one market is somebody else's good one. The honest counterweights: India has been among the world's best-performing major markets for long stretches, global exposure adds currency and tax complexity, and the investor who diversified into the US in 2013 and India in 2021 each felt like geniuses and fools in alternating years. Diversification is insurance plus humility, priced in complexity.

69.2Routes for Indians: LRS, international funds, GIFT City

Three doors lead out, each with its own turnstile. LRS, the Liberalised Remittance Scheme, lets every resident Indian send up to $250,000 a year abroad for almost any capital purpose: you open an overseas brokerage account, remit, and buy the world's markets directly, with tax collected at source on remittances above the annual threshold as the friction to plan around. International mutual funds and ETFs from Indian AMCs are the simplest door: rupee in, a fund-of-funds holding a US or global index, taxed as a debt-type instrument, with no foreign account, no LRS paperwork, and one chronic caveat, SEBI's cap on the industry's overseas investments has repeatedly forced these funds to close or restrict inflows exactly when Indians most wanted to buy the dip abroad. GIFT City, Gujarat's international financial centre, is the newest door: dollar-denominated funds, brokers and banking on Indian soil under its own regulator, routing global investing through an offshore-that-is-onshore with its own tax sweeteners. The practical hierarchy for most: start with the fund door for simplicity, graduate to LRS for control and breadth, and watch GIFT City as it matures into the serious middle path.

69.3ADRs, GDRs and global depositary routes

Depositary receipts are the old bridge, still worth knowing because the logic reappears everywhere. An ADR, American depositary receipt, is a foreign company's share repackaged by a US bank into a dollar security trading in New York: the bank holds the underlying shares and sells you the receipt, handling conversion, dividends and voting through its machinery. Indian giants used the route for decades to reach global capital, Infosys and HDFC Bank in New York, the GDR cousins in London and Luxembourg, before India's own markets deepened enough to pull the action home. For the modern Indian investor the direction has reversed: the depositary logic now serves you through the feeders and funds of Section 69.2, an Indian AMC's fund-of-funds is economically an ADR in reverse, a local wrapper over a foreign asset. The transferable insight is the wrapper's anatomy: every cross-border investment you ever make is an underlying asset plus a wrapper, and the wrapper carries its own costs, taxes, tracking and counterparty questions. Ask of any international product the ADR question: what exactly do I hold, and who stands between me and the asset?

69.4Currency and country risk in practice

Two risk layers arrive with every foreign asset. Currency risk, Chapter 58's territory: your return in rupees is the asset's return multiplied by the exchange rate's move, and over the long run the rupee's structural slide has been a tailwind for the unhedged Indian holder of dollar assets, a tailwind to be enjoyed, never relied upon, because the direction over any one year is a coin the RBI partially steers. Country risk is broader: the foreign state's rules on your ownership, estate taxes on foreign holdings, the US levies estate tax on US-situs assets above a low threshold for non-residents, a genuine trap for large direct holdings; brokerage and custody risk in a jurisdiction you cannot sue in easily; and the geopolitical layer of Chapter 70, sanctions and freezes, which recently taught the world that reserves and assets abroad are only as safe as the relationship between the states involved. The mitigations are pedestrian: use large, regulated brokers and funds; know the estate-tax threshold and prefer Irish-domiciled UCITS ETFs, a standard solution, for very large US equity exposure; and keep the home-country portfolio deep enough that no foreign jurisdiction holds your solvency.

69.5Home bias: the global evidence

Every country commits the same error with a clean conscience: investors everywhere hold far more of their own market than its share of world value justifies, Indians in India, Americans in America, the Japanese in Japan. The behavioural roots are familiar, familiarity feels like safety, foreign feels like risk, but the arithmetic is universal: home bias concentrates the investor's fate in one economy's hands, and it stacks with the exposures already stacked there, your salary, your house, your business and your pension are already long India, so a 100% India portfolio doubles a bet your life has already placed. The global evidence on the remedy is kind: even modest international allocations, a fifth to a third of the equity sleeve, have historically cut portfolio volatility without costing much return, the diversification benefit arriving well before the allocation becomes complicated. Home bias is not stupidity; it is gravity. The point of the data is to know gravity's strength while you decide how much of your wealth should share your postal code.

69.6Worked examples and cases

Case 1: an Indian investor's US index allocation, taxed and converted

₹8.2 lakh becomes $10,000 at 82, into a US index fund via LRS. A good year: the S&P returns 12%, the rupee slides to 86.50, and the rupee value is ₹9.69 lakh, +18.1%, as Chapter 58 computed. Now the layers the brochure skips. Dividends: the US withholds 25% under the treaty before you see them, creditable against Indian tax with paperwork. The exit after three years: foreign equity is taxed as long-term at 12.5% beyond a small exemption, so the ₹1.49 lakh gain costs about ₹18600 in tax. The remittance itself: above the annual threshold, tax collected at source takes a slice upfront, adjustable in your return but a cash-flow fact. And the reporting: foreign assets must be disclosed in the Indian tax return, a compliance line that carries real penalties for omission. None of this argues against the allocation; the after-tax, after-friction result still beat most domestic years. It argues for doing it with the ledger open, because the investor who planned only for "12% in dollars" met an 18% year and understood none of its parts.

Case 2: GIFT City vs direct LRS investing

Two investors, same goal: $50,000 of global equity exposure. Investor A uses LRS: opens a US brokerage account, remits with TCS friction, buys the index ETF directly, and owns the genuine article, cheapest ongoing cost, widest choice, with the full compliance stack, FEMA rules, foreign-asset disclosure, estate-tax exposure above the threshold, and the broker-and-jurisdiction questions of Section 69.4. Investor B routes through GIFT City: a dollar fund or broker account in Gandhinagar's IFSC, regulated by IFSCA, no LRS TCS on the fund route's terms, Indian-law recourse, familiar documentation, at the price of a younger ecosystem, fewer products, slightly higher costs, and a track record still being written. The verdict is horizon-shaped. For a first global allocation or a hands-off investor, B's simplicity and regulatory familiarity win. For the large, engaged allocator who wants specific securities, rock-bottom costs and is willing to carry the compliance, A's directness wins. And the two are converging: as GIFT City's shelf deepens, India's default answer to "how do I invest globally" is slowly migrating home, which was rather the point of building it.
