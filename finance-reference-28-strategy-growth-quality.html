Chapter 28. Stock Strategies: Growth and Quality Investing

Chapter 28 of 85 · about 21 minutes

In 1994 you could have paid what everyone said was a ridiculous price for a small Indian finance company, a paint maker or a two-wheeler financier, held for twenty-five years through crashes, scams and a pandemic, and retired on the arithmetic. Growth and quality investing is the strategy of finding those companies, and its dirty secret is that the finding is easier than the holding. This chapter teaches both, because the second is where the money actually is.

28.1What makes a compounding machine: high ROCE, reinvestment and a long runway?

From first principles, a compounding machine needs three gears meshing. High return on capital: the business earns well above its cost of capital, so every rupee it keeps creates more than a rupee of value. Reinvestment: it can deploy most of its earnings back at that same high return, so the machine feeds itself. Runway: the market it serves is large and growing enough to absorb decades of reinvestment. Miss any gear and the machine stalls: high returns without reinvestment produce a dividend cow, fine but bounded; reinvestment at falling returns produces a destroyer of capital wearing growth's clothes. When all three mesh, the arithmetic is startling: 20% compounded for twenty years is thirty-eight times. The strategy is the hunt for meshing gears, and the patience to let arithmetic work.

28.2How do you judge whether a company’s growth is real and durable?

Reported growth lies in four familiar ways, and the book has already armed you against each. Growth bought with acquisitions, which inflates revenue while returns on capital quietly fall: check organic versus acquired growth. Growth that eats working capital faster than it earns (Chapter 9's cash conversion cycle): check CFO against profit over five years. Growth from price hikes alone in a flat volume market (Chapter 22's decomposition). And growth funded by dilution, where the company grows and your share of it does not: track shares outstanding. Durability is the harder judgement: the moat must defend the return against the competition that high returns attract. The practical test is the track record across adversity: did the company keep growing, and keep its margins, through a demand shock, a cost shock and a competitive attack? Growth that has survived something is the only growth worth projecting.

28.3How much should you pay for growth: what is growth at a reasonable price?

Quality is visible, so it is rarely cheap, and the strategy's central discipline is deciding how much to pay. GARP, growth at a reasonable price, frames it: the multiple should be defensible against the growth's rate, duration and certainty. A useful sanity arithmetic: if you pay 40x earnings for 20% growth, the growth alone brings your effective multiple to market levels in about five years, and everything after that is the return; pay 80x for the same growth and you have prepaid a decade. The mistakes are symmetric. Refusing to pay up for genuine compounders because the P/E "looks high" is how investors miss hundred-baggers while feeling prudent. Paying any price because "quality always wins" is how they buy 2021 peaks and wait a decade to break even. The multiple is a claim on the future; check what the claim assumes.

28.4What is the coffee-can approach to investing?

The coffee-can school, named for the old habit of burying valuables in a tin and forgetting them, carries the strategy to its logical end: select the compounders with extreme care, then do nothing for a decade. The evidence for inactivity is uncomfortable for active minds: most of a great company's long return arrives in unpredictable bursts, so being out of the market on a handful of its best days destroys the compounding; and every sell decision is two decisions, the sell and the reinvestment, each a chance to err. Sitting is also a skill with requirements: the thesis must be monitored, not the price (Chapter 14's one-pager says what would kill it); the position must be sized so a crash tests your conviction rather than your survival; and your own behaviour is the risk to manage, because the coffee can's real enemy is the itch to do something. The best trades this strategy produces are the ones you never made.

28.5What happens when growth stocks de-rate?

The strategy's bear case is arithmetic, and every growth investor should be able to run it in their head. A stock at 60x earnings growing 20%: the market has prepaid years of perfection. Growth slips to 10%, and two things happen at once: the earnings path lowers, and the multiple the market will pay halves, because a 10% grower is a different asset from a 20% one. Price = earnings x multiple, and both terms fall together: 0.7 x 0.5 is a 65% decline on a mere ten-point growth disappointment. This is why growth stocks fall further and faster than their earnings misses warrant, why the falls feel unjust, and why they are entirely just. The protection is not clairvoyance; it is buying at multiples where a growth disappointment bruises rather than kills, and treating every position as a thesis with a kill switch, not a marriage.

28.6Worked examples and cases

Case 1: a ten-year Indian compounder, decomposed

Composite of the classic Indian compounders, numbers illustrative but of the true shape. Over ten years: earnings compound 12.4% (a 3.2x), the stock compounds 20.8% (a 6.6x), and the gap between them is the P/E drifting from 20x to 41x as the market slowly re-rates quality.

The decomposition teaches three lessons. Most of the return came from earnings, the engine; the re-rating, the wind, roughly doubled the outcome and cannot be counted on in advance. The journey included three separate years when the stock fell 30% or more while the earnings line never bent, which is the price of admission. And an investor who paid "too much" at 30x in year one still made five times their money, which is Case 2's mirror: in genuine compounders, the price you pay decides your return, and the quality you buy decides whether there is a return at all.

Case 2: a growth stock that de-rated 70%

The 2017-2018 vintage in India supplied the cautionary set, composites of real episodes: a consumer lender growing 25% a year at 8x book, a mid-cap "next HDFC" at 70x earnings, a diagnostics darling priced for national dominance. Then the shared script: a credit shock, a liquidity squeeze, or merely growth gliding from 25% to 12%. The multiple collapsed first and hardest, 70x to 20x, 8x book to 2.5x, and the earnings disappointment, arriving later, finished the job: drawdowns of 60-80%, recoveries measured in years or never. The autopsy always finds the same two errors by the buyers: the multiple assumed the growth rate was a law of nature, and nobody had written down what evidence would prove them wrong. Growth investing without a kill switch is not investing; it is a lease on someone else's optimism.
