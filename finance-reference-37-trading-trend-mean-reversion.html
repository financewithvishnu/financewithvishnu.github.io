Chapter 37. Trading Stocks: Trend Following and Mean Reversion

Chapter 37 of 85 · about 19 minutes

All systematic trading divides into two faiths. Trend following says the move continues; mean reversion says it snaps back. Both are right, in different regimes, on different timeframes, and each faith's faithful are bankrupted by the other's market. This chapter puts both systems on the table, rules, statistics and failure modes, because the choice between them is really a choice about which kind of pain you can endure without breaking your own rules.

37.1Trend following: the oldest systematic edge

Trend following is momentum (Chapter 30) made into machinery: define the trend, enter in its direction, exit when it ends, never predict. Its intellectual pedigree runs from the Turtle traders of the 1980s through modern CTAs managing billions, and its evidence base is long: mechanical trend rules have made money across decades, asset classes and countries, Indian indices included. The edge's shape is psychologically engineered to be hard to hold: the system loses small, often, in choppy markets, wrong two trades out of three, and then catches one move so large it pays for everything. The trend follower is paid, in the end, for enduring a long sequence of small humiliations without improvising. Almost nobody can, which is precisely why the edge persists.

37.2Moving-average and channel-breakout systems

The two classic constructions. Moving-average systems: trade in the direction of the slope, enter on the shorter average crossing the longer (Chapter 17's cross), exit on the reverse cross. Smooth, always defined, and whipsawed to death in ranges. Channel breakouts, the Donchian construction: buy the N-day high, exit on the M-day low. Cruder, and historically tougher, because it buys strength itself rather than a smoothed average of it. Both share the design truths: the exit matters more than the entry; the parameters matter less than their consistency, the difference between a 19-day and a 21-day channel is noise, while the difference between following and skipping is the whole return; and the system's statistics, win rate, payoff, longest losing streak, must be known before capital is committed, because you will meet that losing streak personally.

37.3When prices snap back: the logic of mean reversion

The opposing faith rests on equally solid observation: over short horizons, in range-bound markets, prices overreact and then return toward their average. The reversion trader sells strength and buys weakness against the local mean, harvesting the market's habit of round-tripping its own excitement. The style's virtues are seductive: high win rates, frequent payoffs, the warm feeling of being right most of the time. Its danger is structural and absolute: the losses, when they come, are the trend days, the crashes, the gap-throughs, where the thing that "always comes back" does not come back. Mean reversion is selling insurance on regime change. The premiums are real; so is the occasional catastrophe; and the strategy lives or dies entirely on whether the catastrophe is priced, capped and survived.

37.4Overbought/oversold tools, used properly

RSI above 70, price above the upper Bollinger band, an oscillator at extremes: the retail reflex reads each as "sell". The professional reading is conditional. In a range, overbought genuinely marks the top of the distribution, and fading it is the strategy. In a trend, overbought is the description of strength, and fading it is stepping in front of the train; RSI can pin above 70 for weeks in a real bull leg, and Chapter 17 said so. The tools are not signals; they are regime instruments. The sequence that makes them honest: first classify the regime, trending or ranging, by the trend's own evidence (slope, structure, ADX-style measures), then let the oscillator time entries within that verdict. Used in that order, the tools are genuinely useful. Used in the retail order, oscillator first, they are a systematic way to short bull markets.

37.5Why each style fails exactly when size is biggest

The deepest and least-taught symmetry in trading: both styles attract maximum capital at maximum danger. Trend following shines in long trending years; the money pours in after them, and arrives for the choppy year that follows. Mean reversion shines in long quiet ranges; the money pours into the calm, and the crash, which is when reversion systems die, is statistically born from that same calm, as low volatility lulls everyone into leverage. The defence is not prediction but construction: run both styles together, since their bad regimes are opposite; size each so that its historical worst case is a survivable event, not a terminal one; and distrust the equity curve most when it has been smoothest. In systematic trading, comfort is the risk report.

37.6Worked example: two systems on Indian indices, honestly compared

Case: the turtle and the fader, same decade

Run both constructions on Nifty and Bank Nifty daily data across a representative decade, costs included, illustrative statistics of the shape such tests reliably show:

Donchian trend systemBollinger reversion system
RuleBuy 20-day high breakouts, exit on 10-day lowBuy touches of the lower band in a flat market, exit at the mean
Win rate35-40%65-70%
Avg win / avg loss3-4 to 10.7 to 1
FeelWrong often, right enormouslyRight often, wrong catastrophically
Bad regimeSideways years: death by whipsawCrash days: the falling knife is real
Long-run expectancyPositive, lumpyPositive, fragile

Neither row is the answer; the pair is. The trend system's 38%-win-rate equity curve looks like a mistake for eighteen months and then doubles; the reversion system's smooth 68% looks like genius until one October takes back two years. The portfolio that holds both, sized so each can survive its own hell, produces something neither delivers alone: a return stream whose worst years are merely bad. That construction, not any indicator, is what "systematic" actually buys.
