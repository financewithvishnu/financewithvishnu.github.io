Part XI · Alternatives and Real Assets

Chapter 64. Real Estate and Gold

Chapter 64 of 85 · about 20 minutes

The two assets every Indian family already understands are the two whose true returns are most systematically overstated. Property's costs hide in the paperwork and the years; gold's hide in the making charges and the locker. Part XI opens with the family's two favourites, priced honestly, so that the alternatives in the coming chapters can be judged against reality rather than against folklore.

64.1Property as investment: yields, cycles and the true costs

Start with the number nobody quotes: the gross rental yield on Indian residential property runs around 2-3%, against commercial property's 6-8%, and that single number disciplines every story told at a family function. A flat's total return is rent plus price appreciation, and the appreciation side carries the costs that folklore omits: stamp duty and registration of 5-8% at purchase, maintenance and society charges every year, renovation between tenants, brokerage at both ends, capital gains tax at sale, and the interest, if a loan is involved, which at 9% against a 2.5% rental yield means leverage against you until prices run. The cycles are long: Indian property moves in decade-length waves of boom and stagnation, city by city, micro-market by micro-market, with illiquidity as the entry ticket, a sale can take quarters, at a price you discover only when you must sell. Property is a real, usable, bankable asset, and for most families the first flat is shelter and forced saving, which justifies it without any return arithmetic. As a second and third investment, it must compete with the numbers of the rest of this book, and the honest version of those numbers is the subject of Case 1.

64.2Gold: jewellery, bars, ETFs, sovereign gold bonds

Chapter 56 priced gold the commodity; this section prices the wrappers. Jewellery is consumption with a salvage value: making charges of 8-15%, 3% GST, and a resale that credits the metal and not the workmanship, the worst financial form, bought rightly for weddings and wrongly for portfolios. Bars and coins cut the making charges but keep GST, storage and a resale haircut. Gold ETFs and funds give the metal at market price with an expense ratio under half a percent and same-day liquidity. Sovereign gold bonds, when the government issues them, are the outlier: the gold price plus 2.5% annual interest, no storage, no GST, and, for individuals, tax-free redemption at eight-year maturity, with tradability on exchange at market prices as the exit before that. The ranking for a portfolio is nearly mechanical: SGBs first for money that can stay locked, ETFs for flexibility, physical only for the reasons that are not financial. Case 2 runs the eight-year arithmetic.

64.3Comparing real assets to financial assets over long periods

Across the long spans where folklore forms, the Indian record, in round illustrative terms, runs like this: equities compounding in the low-to-mid teens with violent paths, gold in high single digits in rupees, helped by the currency's slide, residential property in mid single digits plus a thin rental yield, and fixed income a little above inflation. Real assets earn their place not by winning the race but by running it on a different track: property and gold correlate weakly with equities, and gold in particular shines in the exact decades, the 2000s globally, the crisis years, when equity disappoints. The comparison must also charge each asset its frictions: property's costs and illiquidity, gold's zero yield, equity's volatility and the behaviour tax of Chapter 61. The sensible household synthesis is not a winner but an allocation: equity for growth, debt for stability, gold's 5-15% for the storm, property for shelter and perhaps one income-producing unit, each sized to the job, none asked to do another's.

64.4RERA and the rules that protect buyers

Until 2016, the Indian homebuyer was the least protected party in the transaction: delays measured in years, diverting of funds between projects, and agreements written entirely by the developer. The Real Estate (Regulation and Development) Act changed the structure of the market. Projects must be registered with the state authority before a single unit is sold, with plans, timelines and the promoter's track record on public display. Seventy percent of buyer money must sit in a separate account, drawable only against construction progress certified by professionals, the direct answer to the diversion that stranded lakhs of buyers. Delays trigger interest liability to the buyer at prescribed rates, and structural defects carry a five-year liability. The buyer's disciplines follow from the design: buy only registered projects and read the registration, not the brochure; pay only against construction-linked stages; and remember that RERA regulates the promises, not the market, it cannot make a stalled project solvent or a bad location good. The Act turned a caveat-emptor market into a regulated one; the emptor should still caveat.

64.5Worked examples and cases

Case 1: a flat's real return after all costs

Buy a flat in 2016 for ₹80 lakh; sell in 2024 for ₹1.15 crore. The dinner-table return: 4.6% a year. Now the ledger. At purchase, stamp duty and registration take 7%, ₹5.6 lakh, so ₹85.6 lakh is truly invested. Across the eight years: maintenance at ₹3,000 a month costs ₹2.9 lakh; the flat rents for an average of ₹18,000 a month, earning ₹17.3 lakh gross. At sale, 1% brokerage removes ₹1.15 lakh, before capital gains tax. The honest total: ₹85.6 lakh in, about ₹128 lakh out, a compounded 5.2% a year, before tax, against roughly double that for a dull equity index fund over the same years. The flat was not a mistake; it housed a tenant, anchored a family, and forced saving. But as an investment competing for the next ₹80 lakh, its real rival is a number on a screen with no plumbing, and that rival compounded quietly while the flat compounded noisily. Run this ledger before the second property, not after.

Case 2: sovereign gold bonds vs physical gold over eight years

2016: ₹1 lakh into an SGB tranche at ₹30,000 per 10 grams, and ₹1 lakh into a gold bar. 2024: gold is ₹72,000 per 10 grams. The SGB: the gold value becomes ₹2.40 lakh, the 2.5% annual interest adds ₹20,000, and redemption at maturity is tax-free for an individual, about ₹2.6 lakh, compounding at 12.7%. The bar: 3% GST at purchase bought you less metal, the resale took a 2% haircut, and the 12.5% long-term capital gains tax took its share, leaving about ₹2.12 lakh, 9.9% a year. Jewellery, with its making charges, lands lower still. Same gold, same eight years, three different destinations, the differences entirely in the wrapper: interest, tax, spread and charges. The SGB's price for this superiority was the eight-year lock and the government's issuance calendar; for money meant to be the portfolio's permanent gold sleeve, that price has historically been the best bargain in Indian investing.
