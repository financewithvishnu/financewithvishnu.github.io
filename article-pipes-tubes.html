Pipes and tubes: follow the application, the spread and the cash

By Finance With Vishnu · Source material dated 25 February 2024

A structural tube in a warehouse, a stainless tube in a process plant and a ductile-iron water pipe can all sit inside a pipe-sector comparison. Yet their customers, manufacturing requirements, order patterns and cash cycles can be very different.

I would not begin by ranking every manufacturer on EBITDA per tonne. First identify what the product does, who approves it, how it is sold and what has to happen before the company collects cash.

The useful distinction is between tonnes of installed machinery and tonnes of qualified, profitable, paid-for sales. A capacity story becomes a business result only when those steps connect.

1. What kind of pipe business is this?

Start with material, manufacturing route, dimensions, specification and application. Steel structural sections, large project line pipes, stainless process tubes and ductile-iron water pipes should not be treated as one uniform market.

APL Apollo's FY2025 report describes its structural-tube model. Venus and Ratnamani describe products serving industrial applications. Electrosteel describes ductile-iron and cast-iron pipes. Those are different starting points for research, even before comparing financial ratios. [2, 3, 5, 6]

Consolidated capacity also needs a boundary. Does it cover one product, several materials, overseas plants or subsidiaries? A denominator with the wrong scope can make an otherwise accurate profitability calculation meaningless.

2. Does seamless automatically mean better?

A seamless product has no welded longitudinal seam. Welded products are made using a joining process appropriate to their design and specification. These descriptions are not a complete quality ranking.

What matters is suitability for the actual service: material grade, dimensions, manufacturing controls, testing, operating conditions and the applicable specification. It would be unsafe to select a product for pressure, temperature or corrosive service from an investment article's category label.

The Steel Tube Institute's structural-HSS discussion distinguishes direct-forming and continuous-forming routes and explains why seam details, standards and loading conditions matter. It is a useful reminder that a manufacturing label does not answer every engineering question. Its discussion of older standard editions should not be read as current approval for a particular design. [1]

For the business analysis, ask what technical capability the customer is paying for and what it costs to maintain. A specialised line can be valuable, but it needs eligible orders, appropriate quality systems and adequate utilisation.

3. Why does the customer matter as much as the process?

A distributor serving local fabricators can create a different order and collection pattern from a contractor buying for a long pipeline project. A process-plant customer may require product qualification and inspection before dispatch or acceptance.

The drivers also differ. Construction activity can affect structural demand. An energy project depends on investment approval, procurement and execution schedules. Water-network demand depends on the progress and funding of the relevant infrastructure programme.

Electrosteel's FY2025 report describes weaker government water-infrastructure spending affecting its market in that period. That is historical company evidence of a demand dependency, not a forecast that the same condition applies today. [5]

I would map revenue by actual application and customer type. A list of prestigious customer logos does not establish sales concentration, recurring demand or collection quality. Nor does selling into infrastructure make demand non-cyclical.

4. How should EBITDA per tonne be used?

It is a helpful operating measure when tonnes and earnings refer to the same business, period and entity scope. It is less useful when product-specific margins are compared with group volumes or when the product mix is changing.

Use actual saleable tonnes rather than automatically multiplying nameplate capacity by a favourable unit margin. Utilisation affects both the number of tonnes and the absorption of fixed costs.

The arithmetic does not tell us which scenario is reasonable. We still need the product mix, price formula, operating cost and demand evidence. Combining maximum tonnes with a peak spread can quietly turn an optimistic case into the supposed base case.

Units matter too. One million tonnes multiplied by ₹1,000 per tonne gives ₹100 crore, not ₹1,000 crore. Check the conversions before discussing a multiple.

5. What does value-added actually mean?

A product can add value through dimensions, coating, forming capability, material performance, precision, testing, availability or a customer-specific requirement. The financial question is whether that value leaves a better return after the extra cost and capital.

APL Apollo's historical report discusses differentiated products, technology and a cash-and-carry model. Venus discusses its product range and investment in additional capabilities. These are company-specific strategies to examine, not evidence that every product called value-added earns the same margin. [2, 3]

A new forming line does not create customers by itself. Track qualified products, actual tonnes, net realisation, rejection rates and the cost of serving smaller or more complex orders.

Also test the label over time. Has the definition changed? Is the mix measured by volume or revenue? Does a higher selling price simply reflect a more expensive metal input? Better product economics should show up in cash returns rather than only in a presentation category.

6. When does scale help, and when does it create risk?

Scale can support availability, distribution, procurement and fixed-cost absorption. But it can also create pressure to fill capacity, especially when several manufacturers expand at once.

The research bridge should separate installed capacity, capacity available for sale, qualified output, customer demand and actual dispatch. Maintenance, product mix, changeovers and ramp-up can keep commercial output below a headline maximum.

Venus's FY2025 report distinguishes installed capacity from subsequent expansion phases and expected commissioning. That is the right kind of distinction to preserve. It is not confirmation that every later phase has since been completed or sold out. [3]

I would test a slower ramp and lower utilisation before concluding that a new plant improves earnings per share. Depreciation, interest, staff and working capital can arrive before the full sales benefit.

7. How does a project order become cash?

A tender or enquiry is not an awarded order. An awarded order is not immediate revenue. Manufacturing, inspection, dispatch, acceptance and payment can occur at different times.

Jindal Saw's FY2025 report identifies high working-capital requirements among industry challenges. This matters because a large project can require inventory and work before collection, even when the order is commercially attractive. [4]

Read whether steel cost, freight and foreign exchange are fixed, linked or hedged. Check customer advances, performance guarantees, liquidated damages and receivable ageing. Order-book growth without these terms does not establish improving cash generation.

8. Is a low working-capital model the same as free cash flow?

No. Fast collection can be an important advantage, but EBITDA still differs from operating cash flow and free cash flow. Taxes, interest, maintenance, expansion spending and other cash items remain.

APL Apollo's historical description of its cash-and-carry model should be checked alongside its cash-flow statement and definitions. It should not be converted into an accounting identity that EBITDA equals cash available to shareholders. [2]

For any manufacturer, compare cumulative cash from operations and profit over several years, then explain the difference through inventory, receivables, payables, advances, tax and other items. Use consistent standalone or consolidated scope.

A weak year of cash conversion can reflect growth, timing or a project cycle. It is a question to investigate, not proof of wrongdoing. Repeated poor conversion, rising debt and old receivables require a clear explanation and evidence of eventual collection.

Similarly, profit plus depreciation is not a substitute for the cash-flow statement. It leaves working-capital changes and other adjustments unresolved.

9. How durable are qualifications and approvals?

Approvals can make entry harder when a customer needs a specified product, process or plant. But an approval's exact scope matters. It may apply to a particular facility, size range, grade, test or end use.

Ratnamani's FY2025 report describes a range of materials and applications together with product and customer approvals. This is a useful starting point for checking capability. It does not justify extending one approval to every product the group might make. [6]

I would ask how an approval turns into repeat commercial orders. Qualification may create eligibility without a minimum purchase commitment. Customers can still use multiple suppliers, delay projects or negotiate pricing.

The same caution applies to new applications such as hydrogen or nuclear-related work. An attractive theme is not evidence that the company's exact product and site are currently approved for the intended service. No technical suitability should be inferred without the relevant specification and current evidence.

10. What can make a high margin temporary?

Strong order pricing, a favourable input purchase, product mix, capacity shortages, foreign exchange or an unusually good utilisation period can lift earnings. Some benefits persist; others reverse.

Build a unit-margin bridge instead of accepting one year's result as normal. What changed in selling price, raw material, energy, freight, inventory and overhead absorption? When do contracts reprice?

A multiple based on the first case can look inexpensive while the business is heading toward the third. The reverse can happen in a trough. Test the cause of under-earning or over-earning rather than assuming every weak result is temporary and every strong result structural.

11. How should ductile-iron and alternative materials be compared?

Ductile-iron pipe is not another name for welded carbon-steel tube. Its manufacturing route, applications, installed system and supply chain need their own analysis. Electrosteel's report identifies DI pipes, fittings and cast-iron pipes separately. [5]

A business comparison should examine the project's specified pressure, dimensions, installation, lining or coating, operating environment, maintenance and total installed cost. Material selection belongs with the relevant engineer and specification, not a blanket investment claim.

Claims that one material never corrodes, always lasts a particular number of years or always has the lowest lifetime cost need application-specific support. A historical example of a long-lived pipe is not a warranty for a new network.

For the manufacturer, track customer funding, project execution, order terms, input costs and competing capacity. A large water need can coexist with delayed tenders, slower spending or pressure on selling prices.

12. What about integration, trade protection and dilution?

Producing an input internally can improve supply reliability or change the margin. It also adds machinery, costs, inventory, management demands and funding. A higher EBITDA per tonne does not prove a higher return on the additional capital.

Trade measures need equally precise treatment. An investigation is not a final duty, and a duty has a defined product, country, rate and period. Do not build a permanent competitive advantage from an old notice or a broad statement about imports.

Funding also matters per share. If a hypothetical company's profit rises 30% while its diluted share count rises 20%, earnings per share rise about 8.3%, not 30%. The new capital may still create value, but the bridge must include dilution.

When using enterprise-value measures, reconcile debt, cash, minority interests and other claims before moving to equity value. A capacity-to-EBITDA calculation is several steps away from an equity outcome.

13. What would a useful research sheet contain?

I would keep one sheet for each meaningful product family: material and process; customer application; capacity and actual tonnes; realisation and EBITDA per tonne; working capital; maintenance and growth spending; debt; and diluted shares.

Then attach the evidence for customer approvals, contract pricing and the latest capacity status. Keep guidance apart from achieved results and bids apart from awarded orders.

The downside case should include lower customer spending, too much new capacity, narrower spreads, slow collections and delayed project qualification. A good product does not remove the risk of paying for an earnings level that cannot be sustained.

The simple question remains: which application pays this company, what spread does it retain, and how much cash reaches shareholders after the capital needed to deliver it?
