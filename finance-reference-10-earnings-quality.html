Chapter 10. Earnings Quality, Accounting Red Flags and Forensic Basics

Chapter 10 of 85 · about 22 minutes

In January 2009 the chairman of one of India's largest software companies confessed that about ₹5,040 crore of the cash on his balance sheet did not exist. The accounts had been audited, the board was decorated, and for years the gap hid in plain sight inside numbers any careful reader could have questioned. This chapter teaches you to read accounts the way a forensic accountant does: assume nothing, follow the cash, and let small inconsistencies tell you where to dig.

10.1Where does accounting manipulation begin: accrual versus cash?

From first principles: accounting is a set of conventions for turning messy reality into tidy numbers, and every convention is a door through which judgement, honest or otherwise, enters. Accrual accounting books revenue when it is earned, not when cash arrives, and expenses when incurred, not when paid. The convention is right and necessary; it is also the entire playground of manipulation. A sale booked before it is real, a cost pushed into next year, an asset valued by optimism rather than evidence: each lives in the gap between accrual and cash. That is why Chapter 8's lie detector, profit versus operating cash flow over five years, is the foundation of everything in this chapter. Profit is an opinion. Cash is a fact. When the opinion and the fact disagree for years, believe the fact and find out who is forming the opinion.

10.2What are revenue recognition games and channel stuffing?

Revenue is the most manipulated line because it is the largest and the most rewarded. The classic games, in rough order of frequency:

  • Booking early. Revenue recognised before delivery, before acceptance, or on contracts where the customer can still walk away. Growth looks smooth; the cancellations arrive quietly later.
  • Channel stuffing. Distributors are loaded with more stock than they can sell, booking a great quarter by borrowing from the next three. The tell is receivables and inventory growing much faster than sales, and a distributor network that starts returning goods or disappearing.
  • Round-tripping. Money leaves as an investment or advance and returns as revenue. The company is, in effect, buying its own sales with its own cash, and the cash flow statement, not the P&L, is where the shape shows.
  • Changing the definition. A new revenue policy, a switch from agency to principal accounting, grossing up what used to be net. Policy changes are legal and disclosed; they are also where growth stories quietly re-rate themselves.

The single best check costs nothing: plot revenue growth against receivables growth for five years. Revenue up 15% a year with receivables up 35% a year means customers are "buying" and not paying, and somebody should ask why.

10.3How do companies hide expenses by capitalising them?

The mirror image: costs moved off the P&L and parked on the balance sheet as "assets". Capitalising genuine capacity-building is legitimate; capitalising routine costs is profit manufacturing. Watch capital work-in-progress that sits for years without commissioning (a classic parking lot for expenses and, in the worst cases, for siphoned cash), "intangibles under development" that grow while products never ship, and interest capitalised long after a plant is running. Every rupee capitalised is a rupee of current profit invented and future depreciation deferred. Compare the capitalisation rate of a company with its cleanest peer; a persistent gap is a question, not a coincidence.

10.4Why do related-party transactions and promoter pledging matter so much in India?

Two Indian-market classics, both disclosed, both under-read. Related-party transactions, sales, purchases, loans, advances and leases with entities the promoter controls, are where value quietly moves out of the listed company you own into private companies you do not. Sales to a promoter-owned distributor at odd prices, loans to group entities that never return, brand royalties paid to the family: each is a small leak, and the annual report's related-party note is the map of all of them. Promoter pledging is simpler and sharper: shares pledged as loan collateral can be sold by lenders if prices fall, which pushes prices down further, which triggers more selling. A high and rising pledge percentage is not a red flag; it is a red flare. Treat a promoter who has pledged most of his holding as a promoter who may soon not be one.

10.5What is the forensic checklist for judging earnings quality?

Compare five years of operating cash flow with cumulative profit, check receivables against revenue growth, read the auditor’s report and related-party notes, and track promoter pledging; the cheap checks catch most problems.

Run these in order; the cheap ones catch most problems:

None of these is proof of fraud; each is a place where the story and the numbers must be reconciled. One flag is a question. Three flags on one company is an answer.

10.6Worked examples and cases

Case 1: the Satyam accounts before the fall

Satyam Computer Services, then India's fourth-largest software exporter, reported years of margins comfortably above industry leaders, margins nobody explained convincingly, while interest income on its supposedly enormous cash pile stayed oddly small. In January 2009 the founder's confession letter put numbers on the gap: of about ₹5,361 crore of cash and bank balances on the books, roughly ₹5,040 crore simply did not exist, and the real operating margin for the September 2008 quarter was about ₹61 crore against the reported ₹649 crore.

The lessons were all available before the letter. Interest earned was far too small for the cash claimed (check 1, in spirit). Margins far above capable peers for years is a boast, not a moat. And when a company under pressure proposes to buy related-party real-estate firms with its "cash", as Satyam attempted weeks before the confession, the related-party note has stopped being a note and started being the story.

Case 2: the checklist on a real-shaped small-cap

Consider a small industrial company, numbers illustrative but typical of the pattern: revenue compounding 25% a year, a P/E of 12 that screens as cheap, and a glossy growth story. The checklist disagrees. Cumulative CFO over five years is a third of cumulative profit (flag 1). Receivables are growing at 45% a year (flag 2). The auditor changed twice in three years (flag 3). Promoters have pledged 60% of their holding (flag 5). The low P/E is the market pricing exactly these flags. An investor who ran the checklist saved the entire loss that followed when the receivables turned out to be uncollectible; an investor who ran only a P/E screen financed it. Cheap is the last thing you check, never the first.
