Part VIII · Commodities: Indian Markets and Strategies

Chapter 57. Commodity Strategies

Chapter 57 of 85 · about 19 minutes

Commodities reward a different temperament than stocks. There are no earnings to compound, no managements to trust, only weather, cycles and human need, and the strategies that work here reflect that: follow trends, respect seasons, trade the spreads, hedge what you physically own. This chapter covers the four, and closes with the question most Indian portfolios actually ask: how much gold, in which wrapper.

57.1Trend following on commodities: why it suits them

Trend following, buying what is rising and selling what is falling, systematically, without opinion, has an embarrassingly long record in commodities, and the reasons are structural. Commodity supply responds slowly: mines take years, crops take seasons, so a demand shift creates a long grind rather than a quick re-equilibration, and grinds are what trends are made of. Commodity prices have fat tails in both directions, squeezes up and gluts down, and trend systems exist precisely to be on board the tails. And there is no "fair value" anchor doing the work it does in equities: nobody can tell you copper is cheap at some P/E, so price itself carries the information. The honest description of a commodity trend system: many small losses as false starts are stopped out, paid for by a few large, long rides; low correlation to equities, earned by holding longs and shorts across metals, energy and crops; and years of boredom punctuated by the months, 2008, 2020, 2022, when the trends arrive and the strategy earns its decade's keep.

57.2Seasonal patterns and how to test them

Seasonality is real in commodities in a way it rarely is in stocks, because the seasons are real: crops arrive after harvest and tighten before it, gold demand peaks with Indian weddings and festivals, diesel demand follows weather and driving seasons. The trader's error is not believing in seasonality but believing it naively. A pattern that "gold rises in October" averages twenty Octobers including years of crash and rally; the average hides the variance, and the variance is what your margin experiences. Test a seasonal claim the way Chapter 54 taught: out of sample, with costs, against a large number of years, counting every pattern you checked and discarded. A seasonal edge that survives is a small tilt, worth perhaps a fraction of a percent of timing improvement, not a calendar you can retire on. Treat the wedding-season gold bid as background knowledge that mildly favours patience in buying and boldness in selling, and no more.

57.3Spread trading: calendar and inter-commodity spreads

Because commodities are chained along time (expiries) and across products (crude to products, soybeans to meal and oil, gold to silver), the professional game is often not direction but relationship. A calendar spread trades the curve's shape: long the nearby, short the deferred when you expect tightness to intensify, harvesting the steepening without caring whether the whole market rises or falls. An inter-commodity spread trades one product against its relative: the crack spread, crude against refined products, is a refinery's margin, and trading it is trading the processing economics rather than the price of energy; the gold-silver ratio, swinging between roughly 60 and 100 over the decades, prices silver's dual personality, half monetary metal, half industrial input. Spreads carry lower margins because the exchange sees the offset, and smaller, more frequent moves, which is exactly why professionals like them and retail finds them dull. Dull, in commodities, is usually where the edge is.

57.4Hedging for producers, traders and jewellers

The foundational use. Every commodity business carries an inventory whose price is not theirs to control, and the futures market lets them sell that risk. The principles are Chapter 46's, with commodity texture. Hedge the exposure you actually have: the jeweller's inventory, the refiner's crude in transit, the trader's warehouse stock, in matching quantity and tenor. Respect the basis: your physical price and the futures price move together but not identically, location, quality and duty create a wedge, and the hedged position still carries that wedge's wobble. And treat the hedge as the business's insurance budget, not its profit centre: the jeweller whose short futures "lost money" in a rally made it back on the inventory, and judging the legs separately is how hedging programmes get dismantled one quarter before they are needed. The goal is a business whose profit comes from craftsmanship, refining or distribution, not from accidentally running a leveraged metals fund on the side.

57.5Gold allocation strategies for Indian portfolios

For the household, Part VIII's actionable question is this one, and the answer has three layers. How much: the diversification case supports something like 5-15% of the financial portfolio, enough to matter in a crisis, not so much that a zero-yield asset drags the compounding; more than that is a macro bet, and should be admitted as one. Which wrapper: sovereign gold bonds, when issued, pay 2.5% interest atop the gold price and are tax-free at maturity for individuals, the government's bribe to hold paper gold, with an eight-year lock and market-price exits as the costs; gold ETFs and funds offer daily liquidity at small expense ratios; futures are for traders, not savers; and physical gold is the worst financial form, carrying making charges, GST, storage risk and a resale haircut, held for reasons that are cultural, which is a good reason, but should not be confused with an investment case. How to build it: accumulate the way you accumulate equity, steadily, in the paper forms, rebalanced annually back to target, which forces the sale of a little gold in its glory years and the purchase in its dull ones, the rebalancing bonus applied to the one asset Indians will never need persuading to buy.

57.6Worked examples and cases

Case 1: an MCX crude trend system, honestly described

A simple, real-shaped system: go long MCX crude when the price closes above its 100-day average, exit and go short when it closes below, one contract, always in the market. Described honestly, its year looks like this: eight or ten trades, most of them scratches, a 2% loss here, a 1% gain there, the system's salary paid in small frustration. Then crude spends four months in a war-driven surge from $70 to $110, the system is long the whole way, and one trade pays for two years of scratches. Then a quiet year of range-bound chop, the system's nightmare, whipsawed above and below the average, bleeding a percent a month. The performance drivers are exactly Section 57.1's: the edge lives in the tails, so the system must be in the market for the tail, which means enduring the chop, which is why most people who try trend following quit in the boredom before the payday. The lesson generalises beyond crude: in trend strategies, discipline is not a virtue layered on top of the edge; discipline is the edge.

Case 2: a jeweller's gold hedge, worked through

A jeweller holds 10 kg of gold inventory ahead of the wedding season, worth ₹7.2 crore at ₹72,000 per 10 grams. A price fall is the business risk: the stock was bought at yesterday's prices and will be retailed at tomorrow's. The hedge: sell 100 Gold Mini contracts, 100 grams each, covering the full 10 kg, posting perhaps ₹43 lakh of margin. Gold then falls 8% into the season. The inventory is worth ₹57.6 lakh less; the futures book gains ₹57.6 lakh; the jewellery margin is preserved, which was the point. If gold instead rallies 8%, the futures lose ₹57.6 lakh, funded from an inventory now worth that much more, and the CFO must have the credit line ready, because the hedge's cash moves daily while the inventory's gain is locked in metal. Three disciplines in the fine print: hedge the stock, not a forecast, so the ratio stays near one; roll the hedge as contracts expire, accepting the roll cost of Chapter 55 as an insurance premium; and report the two legs together, always, because the day management sees only the futures loss is the day the hedging programme dies.
