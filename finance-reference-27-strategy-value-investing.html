Chapter 27. Stock Strategies: Value Investing

Chapter 27 of 85 · about 22 minutes

Value investing is the oldest surviving strategy in markets for a reason that flatters nobody: humans systematically overpay for exciting stories and underpay for dull, hated, complicated realities. The strategy is simply to stand on the other side of that habit. It has made more documented fortunes than any other approach in this book, and it has also ruined more impatient people than any other, because cheap can stay cheap for years. This chapter teaches both halves of that truth.

27.1The margin of safety and Mr Market

Two ideas carry the entire strategy. Mr Market, Graham's parable: every day a manic-depressive partner offers to buy your share of the business or sell you his, at prices set by his mood. You are free to ignore him entirely; his only service is to quote. The investor who needs the daily quote to know what his holding is worth has already lost the plot, because worth comes from the business, not the quote. Margin of safety: because every valuation is an estimate (Chapter 15's sensitivity table proved this), you buy only when the price sits far below your estimate, so that being somewhat wrong still leaves you whole. The two ideas combine into the strategy's temperament: value the business independently, wait for the mood to offer you a discount, and let other people's urgency be your opportunity. Nothing in the strategy requires predicting anything; it requires refusing to overpay.

27.2Deep value: net-nets, cigar butts and asset plays

The original, statistical form: buy companies so cheap that the price is below the liquidation value of their own balance sheets. Graham's purest version, the net-net, asks for a market cap below net current assets, current assets minus every liability; you are buying the working capital at a discount and getting the entire operating business, its brand, its factories, free. Buffett called these cigar butts: one free puff left, and the puff is the profit. The strategy works in India, in small caps, in dull corners, and its risks are equally specific: the discount must not be burning away (a loss-making company spends its own discount), and it must be harvestable (a promoter who treats the listed company as a family purse will ensure the value never reaches you). Deep value is a game of averages played across many positions; the screen below is its Indian working form.

27.3Quality at a fair price: the modern value approach

Buffett's evolution, via Munger: a wonderful business at a fair price compounds for decades, while a fair business at a wonderful price gives you one puff and a problem. The arithmetic behind the evolution is Chapter 11's: a company earning 25% ROCE that can reinvest at 25% turns one rupee into a fortune over twenty years, and the entry multiple fades into rounding. The modern value investor therefore hunts the same discount as the old one, but in better prey: temporarily troubled compounders, great businesses in cyclical troughs, quality small-caps before the institutions can buy them. The danger is the word "quality" doing unlicensed work: everyone calls their holding high quality. The chapters of Part III exist precisely so that quality is a checklist, accounts, moat, management, and not an adjective.

27.4Value traps: cheap stocks that stay cheap

The strategy's graveyard. A value trap is a statistically cheap stock whose cheapness is the market correctly pricing decline: the industry dying (fixed-line telephony, print media), the moat filled in (a brand outflanked by distribution change), the capital misallocated (cash hoarded or squandered by a promoter with no intention of sharing), or the accounts flattered (Chapter 10's friends, all of whom screened cheap). The diagnostic question is one: what closes the gap? If the honest answer is "nothing in particular, it is just cheap", you are holding a price, not an opportunity. Real value always carries a mechanism, an industry normalising, a cycle turning, a demerger, a buyback, a new management, even if its timing is unknowable. Cheap with a mechanism is a position; cheap without one is a museum.

27.5Value screening in the Indian market

India rewards and punishes value investors in its own ways. The opportunity: thousands of listed small-caps, thin analyst coverage, and periodic panics that throw out sound businesses with the rest, 2008, 2013, 2018-19, March 2020 all produced genuine net-nets in profitable companies. The punishment: governance risk concentrated in exactly the cheapest names, promoters who can block the harvest indefinitely, and liquidity so thin that your own buying moves the price. The practical adaptations: run the forensic checklist (Chapter 10) as a pass-fail gate before any cheapness screen; size positions for the liquidity you actually have, not the liquidity you wish for; and diversify across more names than a concentrated quality portfolio, because deep value is a statistical game where any single name can be stolen by its own promoter. Cheap in India is plentiful; cheap and honest is the actual screen.

27.6Worked examples and cases

Case 1: a net-net screen on Indian small-caps

Run the screen above in the aftermath of a small-cap panic and you will find a handful of names, real companies, with products and employees, priced below the cash and receivables on their own books. Composite of several genuine finds, numbers illustrative: an industrial-parts maker, market cap ₹180 crore, net current assets ₹260 crore, no debt, ₹15 crore of average annual profit, promoter holding 65% and unpledged, auditor unchanged in a decade. Two years later the market re-priced it to ₹450 crore with nothing having changed except sentiment; the puff was a doubling and a half. The lesson is not that net-nets always work; three of ten fail or stagnate. It is that a portfolio of them, forensically cleaned, tilts the averages so far in your favour that time does the compounding.

Case 2: autopsy of a value trap

A PSU-adjacent capital-goods company, composite, trades at 0.6x book and a 5% dividend yield for six consecutive years, and is cited in every cheap-stocks column of the era. The autopsy finds the four wounds: order books dominated by cost-plus government work whose margins structurally sag; receivables stretching as its only customers are the state; land and assets carried at values nobody can realise while the business uses them; and a majority owner, the government, for whom the company is a policy tool, not a compounding vehicle. Cheapness was not the opportunity; it was the permanent condition, the price correctly reflecting that the gap-closing mechanism did not exist. The stock did eventually re-rate, when sectoral capital cycles and PSU reform changed the mechanism, which is the final lesson: traps are not always forever, but the burden of proof is on the mechanism, never on the multiple.
