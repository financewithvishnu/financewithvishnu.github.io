Banks: follow the funding, the losses and the capital

By Finance With Vishnu · Source class dated 2 July 2023

A bank can grow loans, report a high net interest margin and still produce a poor shareholder return. The missing pieces are often the price and stability of funding, the cost of serving borrowers, the losses that emerge later and the capital required to keep growing.

I would not start by sorting banks from the highest return on equity to the lowest. First ask what produced the return. Better underwriting and a useful deposit franchise are different from a temporary recovery in credit costs or simply using more leverage.

The useful research chain runs from deposits to earning assets, from interest income to profit, and from retained profit to per-share book value. Each step needs its own evidence.

1. What kind of banking franchise is this?

Large national banks, regional lenders, small finance banks and public-sector banks are useful starting groups for research. They are not complete descriptions of risk, and they are not an exhaustive legal classification.

Map the actual business: borrowers, loan products, ticket sizes, geography, deposits, distribution, operating costs and ownership structure. Two banks in the same category can have very different concentrations and loss histories.

A regional franchise can have local knowledge and customer relationships while remaining exposed to the same local industries. A national network can diversify some risks while adding complexity. Neither label settles the quality question.

Read the entity boundary too. Standalone bank accounts and consolidated group accounts can include different activities. Insurance, asset management or other subsidiaries may contribute value without having the same economics as the lending book.

HDFC Bank's FY2025 results separate standalone performance from consolidated results and discuss subsidiaries separately. That is a useful reporting distinction to retain before comparing margins or returns. [1]

2. Why does the deposit franchise matter?

A lender needs money before it can fund assets. The price, duration and behaviour of that funding affect how it competes and how much liquidity it needs.

CASA means current-account and savings-account deposits. A strong transaction relationship can help retain balances and support other services. But an account remaining open does not prove its balance is stable, and a high CASA ratio does not automatically mean the lowest total funding cost.

Current and savings balances have different economics. Retail term deposits are also retail funding, although they are not CASA. Wholesale deposits and other borrowings add further differences in pricing, maturity and concentration.

I would examine average balances as well as the reporting-date figure. HDFC Bank and ICICI Bank's FY2025 results disclose both average and period-end deposit measures. Those are different observations, not interchangeable growth rates. [1, 2]

Ask who can withdraw together, how rate-sensitive the balances are and how the bank funds a period when loans grow faster than deposits. Cheap-looking funding becomes less useful if its stability has been overestimated.

3. How does interest income become profit?

Net interest income, or NII, is interest income less interest expense. It is a rupee amount. Add other income, subtract operating expenses, then account for provisions, other relevant items and tax to reach profit.

The RBI glossary defines NII and a cost-to-income measure using net income after interest expense. That is why gross interest income should not replace NII in this simplified operating-income bridge. [3]

Interest is central to a bank's operations. Removing it as though the bank were an industrial company can make a standard enterprise-value-to-EBITDA comparison misleading.

Other income also needs detail. Fees, foreign exchange, investment gains, recoveries and subsidiary dividends may have different repeatability and risks. A distribution commission is not risk-free merely because the bank is not underwriting the product in that role.

4. Why are spread and NIM different?

A yield minus a funding rate is a rate spread. Net interest margin, or NIM, divides NII by a defined average asset base. Earning assets and interest-bearing funding are not necessarily equal amounts.

For example, an assumed 10% asset yield minus a 6% funding cost gives a four-percentage-point spread. It does not establish a 4% reported NIM without the corresponding balances, non-earning assets, investments, equity and other liabilities.

The RBI glossary defines NIM using average interest-earning assets. HDFC Bank's March 2025 results explicitly show NIM on both total assets and interest-earning assets, with different values. The denominator is part of the result, not a footnote to discard. [3, 1]

Also separate domestic and whole-bank scope and annual versus quarterly periods. SBI's FY2025 release presents distinct domestic and whole-bank NIM measures, as well as annual and quarterly observations. [4]

A higher NIM can reflect pricing power, a different funding mix or riskier, costlier-to-serve borrowers. Compare it with operating expense and through-cycle credit losses before calling it a superior return.

5. When is operating leverage genuine?

Branches, staff, technology and compliance can be built before they generate mature income. If a sound franchise grows into that capacity, income may rise faster than operating expense.

But a high cost-to-income ratio is not a promise of later improvement. Some costs recur. New business needs underwriting, service and collections, and reducing necessary risk spending can make today's ratio look better at tomorrow's expense.

Use the same income definition across periods. A one-off investment gain can lower cost-to-income without a lasting change in branch productivity. A temporary cost deferral can have a similar effect.

In the hypothetical profit bridge, expenses of 250 divided by operating income of 500 give 50%. Dividing by gross interest income plus other income would answer a different question.

I would track recurring operating income, expenses, productivity, new-branch maturity and actual returns. More loans per branch can be efficient, but it should not come from weaker assessment or an overloaded collection system.

6. How are asset quality and credit cost different?

Gross non-performing assets, or GNPA, describe a stock of impaired exposures at a date under the applicable rules. Credit cost describes a period charge relative to a stated loan or asset base. One is a stock; the other is a flow.

A bank can have a large old impaired book with substantial existing provisions and modest new charges. Another can report low current GNPA while new lending has not yet seasoned enough to reveal its eventual losses.

Separate fresh slippages, recoveries, upgrades, write-offs, sales of stressed exposures and changes in provisions. A lower closing GNPA ratio can come from several combinations of these movements, including growth in the denominator.

ICICI Bank's FY2025 results disclose additions, recoveries and upgrades, write-offs and sales separately. That bridge is more informative than the closing ratio alone. [2]

A higher provision charge is not automatic proof of worse new lending. It may reflect recognition of older problems or a more cautious buffer. The right question is what changed, why it changed and whether the allowance is credible.

7. What do NNPA and provision coverage leave out?

Net NPA is not simply a gross percentage minus a provision percentage. Relevant deductions and the net denominator matter. The RBI glossary lists adjustments in the net-NPA amount beyond a casual one-line description. [3]

Provisioning is an accounting recognition and allowance, not necessarily a separate cash pile for each loan. A write-off also should not be confused with a cash recovery.

Coverage ratios need their own scope. SBI's FY2025 release presents coverage both with and without its advances-under-collection-account measure. Federal Bank explicitly labels coverage excluding technical write-offs. Those labels matter before comparing reported percentages. [4, 5]

No single coverage percentage is a universal safety certificate. Loan ageing, collateral, recovery prospects, prior write-offs and the precise definition change its meaning.

8. Does secured and granular mean safe?

Secured and retail are different dimensions. A large corporate loan can be secured, while a small personal loan can be unsecured. Many small loans reduce single-borrower concentration but can still default together.

Borrowers may share geography, employment, industry, weather sensitivity or a collection channel. Collateral can reduce eventual loss severity without guaranteeing timely repayment or easy recovery.

Small finance banks illustrate the need to study origination and funding together. Specialised borrower knowledge can be useful, but building a stable deposit operation, systems and compliance capability requires investment.

AU's FY2025 report discusses its loan and deposit businesses, merger-related change and higher credit costs in unsecured lending. This is a dated company account, not a current licence-status assertion or a ranking against peers. [6]

I would map product, security, borrower cash flow, geography and vintage separately. A change in licence or a broader product list does not erase the risks already inside the book.

9. Why can high ROE conceal fragility?

Return on assets, or ROA, relates profit to average assets. Return on equity, or ROE, relates profit to average equity. When definitions align, ROE is approximately ROA multiplied by average assets divided by average equity. [3]

The second bank has not improved the assumed profit earned per rupee of assets. It has a thinner equity base relative to assets, which can amplify the effect of losses.

Likewise, a recovery-year decline in credit costs can lift both ratios without proving the same return survives a normal cycle. Compare several years and inspect the drivers.

Regulatory capital adds another framework. Capital ratios relate eligible capital to specified risk-weighted exposures and other requirements, not simply to the raw loan book. Capital strength and liquidity are related to resilience but are not the same measure.

10. How does growth become per-share book value?

Retained profit can add to equity and support a larger business, subject to funding, capital and risk constraints. Dividends, losses, other reserve movements and changes in shares alter the bridge.

The issue price affects book value per share, but accounting accretion is not proof the capital earns a good future return. New equity can be sensible and still require an explicit deployment case.

With constant ROE, unchanged shares and a stable payout, ROE multiplied by the retained fraction is a useful simplified growth relation. It is not permission to ignore risk weights, capital buffers, funding or changing profitability.

Keep total equity, book value per share and share-price return separate. They are connected, not identical.

11. What can make turnaround earnings look better than they are?

Lower credit costs, recoveries, provision reversals, asset sales, investment gains and operating improvements can all lift reported profit. Only some may repeat.

Split legacy and new lending where the disclosure supports it, but allow new vintages time to season. Very low early defaults do not establish lifetime credit quality.

A strong annualised quarter is also not the same as a completed full year. Federal Bank's FY2025 release identifies quarterly return measures separately from annual profit. HDFC Bank separately identifies income and provisioning items affecting comparisons. [5, 1]

Leadership changes and supervisory disclosures deserve direct reading, with the actual reason and scope preserved. A shorter appointment, regulatory action or accounting disagreement should prompt investigation, not an unsupported allegation.

I would rebuild a normalised profit using a plausible credit charge, recurring income and sustainable operating costs. Then ask whether the turnaround still produces an attractive return without the most favourable recovery assumptions.

12. Does low price-to-book establish value?

No. Book value is useful only if assets, loss recognition and claims on equity are credible. A low multiple can reflect weak expected returns, hidden losses, dilution risk or uncertainty about future capital needs.

A high-return bank can deserve a different valuation from one that earns little after normal losses. But extrapolating a peak ROE can make an apparently modest multiple expensive.

Keep the earnings period consistent: annual, annualised quarter, trailing or forecast. Use a matching share count and the appropriate standalone or consolidated equity base. Corporate actions can change the comparison.

Then stress the combination of lower margin, weaker fees, higher operating expense and more credit losses. The damage can arrive together, while slower deposits or a capital raise limit the response.

Shareholder return depends on entry valuation, future per-share earnings and book growth, dividends and the valuation eventually assigned. A book-value compounding chart is not a guaranteed investment-return chart.

13. What should the research sheet contain?

Begin with funding: average and closing deposits, CASA and term mix, cost, concentration and maturity. Then map loan products, customers, geography, security, vintages and growth.

Build the profit bridge from NII and other income through expenses, provisions and tax. Put every ratio's numerator, denominator, period, annualisation and entity scope beside it.

For asset quality, keep fresh stress, recoveries, upgrades, write-offs, stressed-asset sales and provision movements separate. For capital, reconcile eligible capital, risk-weighted exposures, retained profit, dividends and dilution.

The final question is not which bank reports the highest margin this quarter. It is which franchise can retain customers, price risk properly, recognise losses honestly and grow per-share value without depending on a permanently benign cycle.
