SaaS: recurring revenue is the beginning, not the conclusion

By Finance With Vishnu · Source period: January 2025

A software subscription can look simple from the outside. A customer pays, uses the product and renews. The difficult questions sit underneath: what does the customer actually buy, when does revenue arrive, how much does delivery cost and how much must be spent to keep that relationship?

I would begin with the customer's workflow rather than the valuation multiple. Software that saves time, reduces errors or supports a necessary process can be useful. Whether that usefulness creates an attractive business depends on retention, selling costs, competition and cash generation.

Recurring revenue is a helpful starting point. It is not guaranteed revenue, free growth or an exemption from normal business economics.

1. What makes software a service?

NIST describes software as a service as using a provider's applications running on cloud infrastructure, without the customer managing the underlying infrastructure. A browser or programme interface can provide access. [1]

That technical description is different from saying that every recurring software payment is the same business. A subscription can include different delivery models, licences, support and services. The accounting and cost structure need to be read alongside the commercial label.

A product may charge per user, per organisation, per transaction or according to consumption. Some contracts combine a minimum commitment with usage charges. The unit of charging shapes what causes revenue to grow or fall.

I would write the business in one sentence: who uses it, which problem it solves, how it is delivered and what event creates a charge. If that sentence is unclear, a spreadsheet full of recurring-revenue metrics will not fix the analysis.

Start with the service. Then identify the commercial and accounting model attached to it.

2. Are bookings, invoices, cash and revenue interchangeable?

No. A signed contract can create a commercial commitment without immediately producing an invoice, a cash receipt or recognised revenue.

Consider a hypothetical non-cancellable three-year service arrangement for ₹120 a year, with equal service throughout each year. The total contractual consideration is ₹360. If the first year is invoiced and paid at the start, cash of ₹120 arrives then, while revenue is recognised as that year's service is provided.

Salesforce's FY2025 accounting note distinguishes invoiced amounts, receivables, unearned revenue and recognised revenue. Its cloud-service revenue is generally recognised over the contract term, while some licence revenue is recognised at a point in time. [2]

The example is not an accounting rule for every software contract. Identify the performance obligations and actual recognition policy first.

3. What does ARR tell us, and what does it leave out?

Annual recurring revenue, or ARR, is an operating metric. It generally describes an annualised recurring business base at a point in time, but companies do not all calculate it in the same way.

Freshworks' calendar-2024 results define ARR using subscription, software-licence and maintenance revenue contractually expected over the next 12 months, with specified assumptions about changes, cancellations and recognition. That is a particular definition, not permission to apply a shortcut everywhere. [3]

ARR is not simply last year's reported revenue minus implementation services. A year-end run rate and revenue earned across the preceding year measure different things.

I would check whether usage, discounts, acquisitions, foreign exchange and service elements enter the calculation. Then compare the same definition across periods.

A longer contract can also raise total bookings without increasing the annual business run rate. If a ₹100 annual arrangement becomes a three-year ₹300 arrangement with the same yearly service, the contract total rises but the annual economics have not tripled. Read duration alongside any bookings-growth headline.

4. Why does usage-based software need a different lens?

A seat-based subscription can grow through more users, higher prices or additional products. A consumption model depends more directly on how much the customer uses the service.

Snowflake's FY2025 results explicitly distinguish product revenue based on platform consumption from subscription software that generally recognises revenue over a contract term. Customers have flexibility over consumption timing, so contract value alone does not determine a particular quarter's product revenue. [4]

That creates a useful discipline: connect revenue to the customer's activity. Is usage increasing because the product is valuable, because the customer is growing or because a temporary project is running?

Optimisation can also reduce spending without the customer leaving. A customer may remain active while using fewer resources. That is economically different from losing the account, but it still affects revenue.

I would separate customer counts, unit consumption, price and product mix wherever disclosures allow it. A single label such as cloud growth can hide very different operating changes.

5. What is the difference between customer and dollar retention?

Customer retention counts relationships. Dollar retention measures the retained value of a customer cohort. Losing one large account and losing one small account have the same effect on the customer count but not on revenue.

Gross dollar retention usually excludes expansion benefits and measures the effect of churn and contraction on an opening cohort. Net dollar retention includes expansion within that cohort. The precise treatment still needs the company's definition.

Here gross retention is 85% and net retention is 110%. Both are informative. A net measure above 100% does not mean no customers left.

Freshworks uses ARR from a defined customer population; Snowflake uses product revenue from a specified cohort over a two-year measurement window. Their retention metrics should not be assumed identical. [3, 4]

6. Why does churn become harder to replace at scale?

A small business can replace a modest absolute number of lost customers and still grow quickly. The same percentage loss becomes a much larger selling task when the installed base expands.

Suppose a hypothetical business begins with 1,000 equally sized customers, loses 100 and wants to finish with 1,200. It must add 300, not 200. Growth has to replace the leak before it expands the base.

The calculation does not prove that a particular churn rate is acceptable or unacceptable for every company. Enterprise customers, small businesses and consumer users can behave differently, and revenue concentration changes the stakes.

I would examine why customers leave, how quickly new customers become productive and whether the retained cohort continues to receive value. Renewal after a discount or a reduction in seats may look different from a healthy expansion.

Switching costs can support retention through integrations, workflow familiarity, data migration and training. They do not make a product immune to poor service, a cheaper alternative or a change in how customers work.

7. Is the next software customer almost free?

Reusing software can create scale advantages. That does not mean the next customer has zero cost. Hosting, storage, support, third-party software, security and payment processing can all grow with activity.

ServiceNow's calendar-2024 results separately show subscription revenue and professional-services-and-other revenue, along with their costs and gross profits. The distinction helps explain why the total margin may differ from the subscription margin. [5]

I would compare delivery costs for the actual product, not assume a universal gross-margin threshold. A usage-heavy service and a relatively lightweight application may have different infrastructure requirements.

New AI capabilities add another question: what must be paid to deliver each useful outcome, and does the pricing model recover that cost? This is a model to test, not a claim that every AI feature has the same economics.

Gross profit must then fund development, selling and administration. A high gross margin is valuable only if enough remains after the spending required to win, serve and retain customers.

8. How useful are CAC payback and lifetime value?

Customer-acquisition cost, or CAC, asks what was spent to acquire a customer. Payback asks how long the resulting contribution takes to recover that cost. Both depend on what is included and when the selling effort happened.

In a simple hypothetical, acquisition costs ₹120, monthly revenue is ₹20 and gross margin is 75%. Monthly gross profit is ₹15, producing an eight-month gross-profit payback before other costs, churn or discounting.

That is not a complete valuation. Implementation, retention spending, collection timing and expansion can change the cash outcome. A sales cycle lasting several quarters also makes a same-quarter spending-to-new-sales ratio hard to interpret.

A lifetime-value model based on gross profit divided by churn is especially sensitive when churn is low. Moving an assumed annual churn rate from 10% to 5% doubles the implied undiscounted value if everything else is held constant.

I would prefer observed cohort cash flows and sensitivity tests to a single impressive LTV-to-CAC ratio. A model that requires customers to remain indefinitely at unchanged margins deserves less confidence than one supported by actual renewal history.

9. Does a loss prove that growth spending is creating value?

No. Development and selling can cost money before the resulting revenue arrives, so an early loss can coexist with progress. It can also reflect a product that customers will not retain or a selling model that never pays back.

The distinction needs evidence: customer activation, renewal, gross profit, selling efficiency and the relationship between new spending and subsequent cohorts. Calling every expense an investment avoids the real question of whether it earns a return.

Accounting treatment also varies by cost and circumstance. Atlassian's FY2025 accounting note describes evaluation of cloud-platform and internal-use development costs for capitalisation, while Salesforce describes capitalised costs to obtain certain revenue contracts. [6, 2]

It is therefore wrong to assume that every software-related cost passes immediately through the income statement. Compare capitalised additions, amortisation and cash spending as well as reported operating expenses.

A transition between licence and cloud offerings can also change revenue timing. Read the actual product mix and policy rather than treating a temporarily weaker income statement as automatic proof of a hidden turnaround.

10. Why is upfront cash not all free cash?

Cash collected before service delivery can help fund operations. It also comes with an obligation to provide the service. Unearned or deferred revenue is not a deferred-tax liability.

In the earlier ₹120 example, collecting on day one does not mean the company has earned ₹120 of profit. It must continue to deliver, support and maintain the service while recognising revenue under its policy.

I would examine both the working-capital benefit and the costs still to be paid. Faster annual billings can temporarily improve operating cash flow; a change in payment terms can reverse that benefit without an equivalent change in customer value.

Free-cash-flow definitions also require checking. Freshworks' calendar-2024 release defines adjusted free cash flow after property-and-equipment purchases and capitalised internal-use software, with a restructuring adjustment. Snowflake provides its own definition and treatment of certain equity-award-related cash items. [3, 4]

Outsourcing infrastructure does not make it economically free. Nor does recurring revenue make a highly leveraged balance sheet safe. Debt still has interest, maturity and refinancing requirements.

11. How should stock compensation enter the analysis?

Paying employees with equity can reduce an immediate cash salary requirement. It does not mean their work is free to shareholders. Equity awards can transfer value and increase the share count.

Salesforce's FY2025 accounting note and Atlassian's FY2025 note describe stock-based compensation expense. Freshworks' release explains its non-GAAP exclusions, including stock-based compensation. The reported and adjusted views answer different questions. [2, 6, 3]

I would read the reconciliation, examine diluted shares and follow repurchases alongside issuance. Buying back shares to offset awards uses cash that is not then available for another purpose.

ServiceNow's January 2025 release explicitly describes managing dilution as the primary objective of an additional repurchase authorisation. That historical rationale illustrates the connection; an authorisation is not proof that every share was repurchased. [5]

Do not automatically subtract the same economic cost twice. Build a consistent valuation treatment of compensation, dilution and cash repurchases, then show the assumptions clearly.

12. Do RPO and AI announcements settle the growth question?

Remaining performance obligations, or RPO, describe contracted revenue not yet recognised under the company's definition. They are not the same as cash in the bank or revenue to be earned next quarter.

Snowflake's FY2025 release explains that its RPO includes deferred revenue and certain unbilled non-cancellable amounts, excludes some arrangements and is affected by contract duration, renewals, currency and consumption timing. [4]

I would pair RPO with revenue, recognised timing, billings, collections and the terms behind the commitment. A longer-duration contract can increase the balance without the same increase in near-term revenue.

For AI, separate a product announcement from paid adoption, and paid adoption from profitable delivery. Useful questions include how customers are charged, whether existing subscriptions are displaced and what infrastructure or model-provider costs remain.

Cheaper software creation may change competition, but code is only part of a functioning business. Distribution, integration, data rights, security and customer outcomes still need evidence. Neither automatic destruction nor automatic protection is a sound starting assumption.

13. What would make the recurring engine convincing?

I would become more interested when these layers tell a consistent story. Revenue growth supported by retained customers, sensible acquisition costs and cash generation is more convincing than a metric that improves only because its definition changed.

A subscription creates another opportunity to earn the customer's business. The quality of the company depends on what happens at every renewal.
