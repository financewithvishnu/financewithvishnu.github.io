Hotels: room yield is the beginning, cash return is the test

By Finance With Vishnu · Source period: February 2024

A hotel can look attractive when room rates rise, occupancy improves and new supply takes time to arrive. But the shareholder does not receive occupancy or room revenue. The shareholder owns what remains after operating costs, upkeep, financing, tax and the capital needed for growth.

The first step is to identify what the company actually owns. A hotel owner, a lessee and a brand manager can show the same hotel in a portfolio presentation while recording very different revenue, profit and obligations.

I would study the sector through a short chain: available rooms, occupied nights, realised room rate, total property economics and cash after reinvestment. Each link answers a different question.

1. Are we studying an owner, an operator or a brand?

Ownership, operation and branding are separate roles. They can sit together or be split between parties. A familiar name over the entrance does not by itself tell us who owns the building or carries its debt.

IHCL's FY2025 report describes management and incentive fees linked to revenue and profit under contracts. SAMHI describes an asset platform working with international hotel brands. These illustrate why the property's full revenue and the brand manager's fee revenue are not interchangeable. [1, 2]

A room added under a management contract does not add the same consolidated revenue or capital requirement as a room added to an owned hotel. Begin every comparison with the structure, then read the margins.

2. What do occupancy, ADR and RevPAR measure?

Occupancy is occupied room nights divided by available room nights. Average daily rate, or ADR, is room revenue divided by occupied room nights. Average room rate, often shortened to ARR in hotel reporting, is a similar room-rate measure. It is not software annual recurring revenue.

Revenue per available room, or RevPAR, divides room revenue by available room nights. With matching definitions and periods, it also equals ADR multiplied by occupancy. The denominator is available room nights, not just a count of rooms without a time period.

HVS explains the RevPAR calculation and its limitations as a room-revenue measure. Its older paper is used here for definitions and analytical boundaries, not current market benchmarks or valuation correlations. [3]

Check taxes, packages, complimentary rooms, commissions and treatment of temporarily unavailable rooms. A comparison is only meaningful if the revenue and inventory definitions match.

3. Why can the same RevPAR produce different profit?

One hotel may have restaurants, banquets, weddings and conference space. Another may mainly sell bedrooms. A room metric leaves these differences outside its scope.

HVS's discussion shows why non-room revenue and operating expenses can change the profit comparison even when one property has a better RevPAR. Do not turn room yield into a complete hotel valuation. [3]

Build the revenue bridge separately: room nights multiplied by realised room rate, then food and beverage and other services. Avoid using a universal revenue mix for every hotel. A city business property, a resort and a limited-service hotel can have different economics.

Then examine the contribution from each activity. A wedding can fill banqueting space and rooms, but it also requires food, staff, utilities and service. Incremental revenue is not incremental profit at a 100% margin.

Gross operating profit, property EBITDA, company EBITDA and free cash flow answer different questions. Preserve each company's definition and reconcile property measures to what the listed shareholders own.

4. How does operating leverage work in both directions?

A hotel needs staff, security, systems, maintenance and functioning common areas even when some rooms are empty. Other costs vary with occupancy or events. The useful model has fixed, semi-fixed and variable elements rather than assuming every expense is fixed.

When revenue grows faster than costs, operating profit can grow much faster than revenue. The reverse can be severe because costs may not fall at the same speed as room demand.

In the improving case, sales rise 15%, costs rise 10% and operating profit rises 35%. Subtracting 10 from 15 does not calculate the growth in profit. The starting rupee amounts matter.

The weak case assumes some cost reduction, not none. Profit still falls 65%. This is why a fixed-cost advantage in an upcycle becomes a risk when demand softens, especially with substantial debt or lease obligations.

5. Is a national supply shortage enough?

No. Hotels compete in particular locations, customer segments and travel occasions. An airport business hotel does not face exactly the same supply as a remote leisure resort. A new road or office district can change the relevant market.

I would map the actual competing inventory: existing rooms, credible projects under construction, likely openings, renovations and closures. Announced rooms are not guaranteed openings, but neither should they be ignored because the last cycle had delays.

A long development process can support existing hotels when demand grows faster than usable supply. It does not create permanent pricing power. Land, permissions, construction, financing and customer positioning all affect the time and cost of entry.

Compare the company's pipeline with its own funding and execution record. Chalet's FY2025 report describes a combination of operating hospitality assets, developments and commercial property. That is a reminder to analyse project and asset scope rather than treating every square foot as an immediately earning hotel room. [4]

National tourism growth is useful context. A property-level cash forecast still needs local demand and competitive evidence.

6. What does same-store growth leave out?

A portfolio's average rate can rise because existing hotels charge more, because an expensive new property joins the group or because a lower-priced property closes. Those explanations are not equivalent.

Same-store or like-for-like figures try to hold the comparison set more stable. But the label is not enough. Read the inclusion dates, acquisition treatment, renovation exclusions and whether prior periods have been restated.

SAMHI's FY2025 report defines exclusions from its same-store metrics, including acquired and renovating properties. That footnote changes what the room-yield trend represents. It should travel with the number. [2]

I would keep three bridges: performance of comparable operating hotels, contribution from acquisitions and new openings, and the effect of closures or renovations. Then reconcile them to statutory revenue and profit.

Portfolio occupancy also hides dispersion. A flagship running close to full does not prove every hotel has the same demand or pricing headroom. Nor can occupancy rise indefinitely. Once a property is near capacity, growth increasingly depends on price, mix, ancillary revenue or more inventory.

7. How should seasonality and customer mix be read?

Compare equivalent seasons before reading sequential changes as a trend. Corporate travel, weddings, school holidays, weather and local events can produce different patterns across cities and properties.

A strong quarter can also contain a large event or an unusual concentration of high-rate nights. Annualising it without adjustment can overstate normal earnings. A weaker quarter may reflect the usual calendar rather than a damaged business.

Break demand into useful customer groups where disclosures allow: corporate, leisure, groups, conferences, weddings and other channels. International guest share is a different measure from total foreign arrivals, absolute room nights or realised room rate.

Distribution matters too. An online travel platform can fill rooms, but commissions affect the net economics. Direct bookings also carry marketing, technology and loyalty costs. Neither channel should be called free without checking the cost.

I would ask whether higher ADR survives after distribution costs and whether the hotel is trading price for occupancy. Revenue management is a balance, not a contest to report the highest possible rate.

8. When does refurbishment become a good investment?

Renovation can protect a property's competitiveness or support a different price position. It can also close rooms, interrupt food and beverage operations and consume substantial cash before any benefit arrives.

Separate three effects: the capital spent, the temporary lost operating contribution and the post-renovation volume-and-rate improvement. Do not explain all three by pointing to a single EBITDA adjustment.

EIH's FY2025 results identify a property-asset derecognition loss connected with major renovation at The Oberoi Grand, Kolkata. That is a historical example of renovation reaching the accounts beyond routine operating expense. It is not a statement about the property's current opening status. [5]

A simple project test asks what incremental annual cash the work can generate after recurring costs and upkeep, compared with the total investment and disruption. Higher room rates alone do not settle the return.

For research, keep maintenance and competitive refurbishment separate from expansion. Calling all refurbishment growth spending can make the existing portfolio's cash generation look better than it really is.

9. Does asset-light mean obligation-free?

A management or franchise model can require less property capital than ownership. But contracts can still include performance conditions, guarantees, owner support, key money, systems investment and other commitments. Read what the particular company has agreed to provide.

Leases need separate attention. They can reduce the initial cost of buying land and buildings while creating future payment obligations. A variable lease, a fixed lease and a lease with minimum guarantees have different downside behaviour.

Under the recognised-lease model, a right-of-use asset and lease liability can appear on the balance sheet, with depreciation and interest replacing part of what might otherwise be presented as rent. The IFRS Foundation's effects analysis explains the presentation effects; SAMHI's Indian financial statements provide the relevant company accounting policy and exceptions. [6, 2]

Not every lease payment receives identical treatment. Check variable payments, short-term arrangements and the applicable accounting policy. A higher reported EBITDA caused by presentation is not automatically better underlying cash generation.

Valuation should treat lease liabilities and the earnings denominator consistently. Otherwise two otherwise similar portfolios can appear to have different multiples for an accounting reason.

10. What does deleveraging actually mean?

A fall in debt-to-EBITDA can come from debt repayment, higher EBITDA or both. Only the first necessarily reduces the debt principal. A capital raise or asset sale introduces another source of funding and another ownership calculation.

Read maturities, covenants, bullet payments, refinancing needs, interest rates and security. Scheduled repayment does not ensure the same reduction in net debt if a company borrows again for construction or acquisitions.

Asset sales also need a proceeds bridge. Subtract taxes, transaction costs, secured debt settlements and any retained obligations. If an owner sells a hotel and remains its manager or lessee, both the balance sheet and future earnings model change.

Lower interest can help profit, but do not count the same debt repayment twice as both extra enterprise value and extra equity value without a consistent model.

11. Which adjustments deserve extra care?

Pre-opening costs, acquisition costs, exceptional items and share-based compensation can make adjusted profit differ from statutory profit. Some adjustments help explain operations. None should disappear from the shareholder's economics merely because it is labelled non-cash or one-time.

SAMHI's FY2025 report presents pre-ESOP EBITDA and separately identifies the excluded compensation cost. Its audited notes describe equity-settled share-based payments and option movements. That supports a reconciliation, not a rule that such costs can always be ignored. [2]

A particular grant's expense can fall as vesting progresses. Future grants can still occur, and exercised options can change share count. Track the statutory expense, the adjusted measure and diluted ownership separately.

Likewise, a portfolio that continually opens hotels may continually incur pre-opening costs. Recurring expansion activity can make a series of individually unusual charges economically persistent.

I would compare several years of cumulative operating cash with profit, then subtract the upkeep needed to maintain the property standard. EBITDA is a starting point, not cash available for distribution.

12. How should a hotel forecast be stress-tested?

Begin with a room-night model. Available rooms multiplied by days, occupancy and ADR gives room revenue. Add other revenue on an explicit basis rather than burying it inside an unexplained margin.

Keep existing hotels, ramping properties and projects not yet open separate. Apply opening delays and lower initial occupancy where appropriate. A signed management pipeline is not the same as operating inventory or owned-property revenue.

Then model costs, recurring refurbishment, financing and tax. Past losses do not guarantee a zero tax bill forever. A future high EBITDA margin still needs a cash and capital bridge.

Use a downside with both lower demand and lower pricing, plus funding pressure or delayed openings. HVS's framework is useful because it links room yield to wider property profitability instead of treating RevPAR as a valuation on its own. [3]

Finally, test valuation compression and dilution. A forecast return that depends on full occupancy recovery, perfect project timing, cheap refinancing and an expensive exit multiple is a collection of favourable assumptions, not a margin of safety.

13. What would I keep on one research sheet?

For each important property or portfolio cluster: location, segment, ownership model, operating rooms, occupancy, ADR, RevPAR, non-room revenue and profit definition. Keep the same-store boundary beside the numbers.

Then list debt and lease obligations, maintenance and expansion spending, expected openings, renovation closures, management commitments, minority interests and diluted shares. Reconcile company-level earnings and cash to those operating pieces.

The monitoring questions are straightforward. Is local supply changing? Are higher rates holding after channel costs? Are new rooms earning cash? Is debt falling in rupees, not only as a ratio? Does recurring upkeep leave an attractive return on the capital tied up?

A good hotel can be a scarce asset and still be an expensive investment. A rapidly growing hotel portfolio can also consume cash for years. The useful conclusion comes from joining the room-yield story to the cash-return story, not stopping at either one alone.
