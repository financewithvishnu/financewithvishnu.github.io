Oil and gas: one barrel, several different businesses

By Finance With Vishnu · Source period: August 2024

A rise in crude oil does not produce the same outcome for everyone called an oil-and-gas company. The producer sells hydrocarbons. The drilling contractor sells equipment availability and work. The terminal handles somebody else's product. The refinery buys crude and sells a basket of products. The distributor buys gas and serves an end customer.

I would begin by asking a simple question: what does this particular business get paid for? Without that answer, a correct view on oil prices can still lead to the wrong conclusion about earnings.

The useful starting point is the economic chain, followed by the contracts, assets and cash flows at each link. An integrated group may own several links, but that does not remove the need to understand them separately.

1. Which part of the chain earns the money?

Upstream means exploration, development and production. Midstream covers activities such as transport, storage and terminals. Downstream includes refining, marketing and distribution. Equipment and service providers support these activities without necessarily owning the hydrocarbons they help produce or move.

This distinction is visible in reporting. ONGC's FY2025 release separates production and realised prices, while also describing downstream group companies. GAIL's FY2025 presentation identifies transmission, marketing, petrochemicals, LPG and liquid hydrocarbons, and exploration interests. Neither a broad sector label nor consolidated revenue identifies the source of incremental profit. [3, 6]

My first research page would show the asset, customer, charging unit and main cost for each material segment. Only then would I bring in an oil-price view.

2. Why is a discovery different from cash generation?

Finding hydrocarbons is one step. Establishing commercial recoverability, securing the right to develop, obtaining approvals, drilling development wells, installing facilities and connecting customers are further steps. Each can require money before the first commercial receipt.

A reported discovery therefore needs an execution bridge. What is the company's economic interest? Which approvals remain? Who funds development? When can production begin, and what processing or evacuation capacity is needed?

ONGC's FY2025 release reports discoveries and monetised discoveries separately. That is a useful distinction, even without treating a count of discoveries as a valuation measure. Different discoveries can have very different size, cost and commercial prospects. [3]

I would also separate the field's output from the listed company's attributable output. Partners, royalties and production-sharing arrangements can change the cash retained. A large project announcement is not automatically a proportionate increase in per-share cash flow.

3. What do reserves tell us, and what do they not tell us?

A resource estimate and a reserve estimate are not interchangeable. Under the cited US reporting framework, proved reserves require reasonable certainty of economic producibility under the specified conditions. Development status and the assumptions used matter. This framework helps explain the distinction; it does not replace the reporting rules applicable to an Indian company. [2]

Reserve replacement asks whether additions replace what was produced over a matched period and scope. But additions can come from discoveries, improved recovery, changed estimates or acquisitions. A ratio above one says little about shareholder value without the cost and quality of those additions.

Reserve categories also need discipline. Proved plus probable is commonly described as 2P. It is not an additional bucket to add to proved reserves again. Compare the same classification, date, economic interest and commodity scope before calculating reserve life or making a peer comparison.

Oil and gas can be converted into energy-equivalent measures for aggregation. That does not make them equally valuable per equivalent unit. EIA's energy-unit guidance explains comparison by heat content. Realised prices, transport costs and contracts still determine the financial result. [8]

A large reserve base can support future production. It is not cash in a bank account, and it may still require substantial development spending.

4. How do price and production work together upstream?

Start with attributable saleable volume multiplied by realised price. The realised price can differ from a familiar benchmark because the product, quality, location, contract and deductions differ. Gas may have another pricing mechanism entirely.

Consider an original illustration using indexed production and a simplified cash operating cost. At 100 units, a selling price of 80 and cost of 40 per unit give a contribution of 4,000. If price falls to 60, the contribution becomes 2,000. If production also falls to 90 units, it becomes 1,800.

This is contribution arithmetic, not an estimate of profit or free cash flow. Costs need not remain constant when production changes. It shows why a producer should be tested on both realisations and output rather than on one price assumption.

ONGC's historical disclosure separately lists standalone oil production, gas production and realisations. Keeping those series separate is more useful than assuming that one consolidated growth rate describes the whole operating picture. [3]

5. Why can production growth still leave little free cash?

A field can generate operating cash while the wider business spends heavily on development, replacement reserves or acquisitions. Depletion means that maintaining output can require investment before any growth begins.

I would split cash spending into sustaining existing productive ability, developing incremental output, acquiring assets and funding unrelated or adjacent activities. The boundaries can involve judgement, but combining everything into one capex headline hides the question that matters: what future cash does this spending buy?

ONGC's FY2025 release is a useful scope warning. Its stated capex includes investment in OPaL and renewable-energy-related acquisitions as well as exploration and other spending. It should not be read as a single number for drilling new upstream wells. [3]

Then add financing, taxes, contractual payments and eventual abandonment obligations. Stress-test delayed production, not only a lower commodity price. Interest and committed expenditure can continue while a project waits for an approval or connection.

6. Are drilling and oilfield services safer than owning oil?

They have different risks, not an exemption from the cycle. A rig owner may earn a day rate rather than the selling price of oil, but customers decide how much exploration and development work to commission.

For an original example, 300 paid days at a rate of 100 produce revenue of 30,000. If the next contract has 240 paid days at a rate of 90, revenue becomes 21,600, a 28% decline. The fleet can be unchanged while its earnings opportunity changes sharply.

Read the actual contract for operating rates, standby rates, downtime, mobilisation, customer cancellation rights and which party carries cost overruns. Also distinguish owned equipment from leased, managed or jointly owned equipment. The same visible rig can produce different economics for different companies.

Jindal Drilling's FY2025 annual report discusses pressure from released rigs, tender conditions and charter rates, alongside its operating performance. This is a reminder that technical uptime and commercial utilisation are not the same measure. A well-run asset still needs a paying contract at an adequate rate. [5]

Order book is another starting point, not a cash balance. Follow award, mobilisation, work, billing and collection, including the maintenance cash needed to keep equipment eligible for work.

7. What makes a pipeline or terminal valuable?

Infrastructure can solve a difficult physical problem: moving or storing product at the right location with the right connections and permissions. But the relevant economics depend on the asset and contract.

A pipeline may earn a transport tariff. A terminal can earn storage and handling income. A shipping business has freight rates and fleet supply to consider. Putting all three into a single supposedly predictable midstream category loses important differences.

Capacity is the ability to serve demand. Throughput is what actually moves through. Static storage capacity is not annual sales volume, and tonnes, cubic metres and energy units cannot be exchanged without the relevant conversion.

Aegis's FY2025 report distinguishes liquid logistics from its gas business, which includes sourcing, terminalling and distribution. A logistics investment should therefore not be analysed as though every rupee of group revenue were an identical storage fee. [4]

8. Does a long contract guarantee a good return?

A long contract can improve visibility, but only within its terms. The questions are what is committed, who must pay, when the obligation begins and which conditions permit revision or termination.

Minimum-volume, capacity-reservation and take-or-pay language need separate reading. None should be converted into an unconditional profit floor without examining exceptions, counterparty strength and the costs needed to perform.

Regulated pricing adds another layer. The cited PNGRB natural-gas pipeline tariff framework defines a transport tariff per energy unit and provides for tariff determination and review. The rule is not a guarantee that equity investors earn a fixed cash yield regardless of utilisation, spending or financing. [7]

I would match the asset life, contract life, debt maturity and major maintenance schedule. Long-lived steel funded with debt that matures well before dependable cash receipts can create refinancing risk despite a promising demand story.

9. Why is a refinery not simply a bet on cheaper crude?

A refinery buys crude and sells a product basket. Its opportunity depends on the difference between product value and feedstock cost, adjusted for its actual yields and operating requirements. A fall in crude does not guarantee improvement if product prices fall faster.

EIA's 3:2:1 crack-spread illustration uses three barrels of crude, two of gasoline and one of distillate. With all prices expressed per barrel, the spread per crude barrel is (2 × gasoline price + distillate price − 3 × crude price) ÷ 3. It excludes other variable costs and fixed costs. [1]

The actual refinery may process a different crude slate and make a different product mix. Complexity can permit more feedstock flexibility or conversion, but it also requires capital, energy and maintenance. It is a capability to evaluate, not a permanent margin guarantee.

Inventory timing matters too. Product sold today may have been made from earlier purchases. Separate underlying processing economics from inventory effects and do not mistake one strong quarter for a normal through-cycle margin.

10. How is city-gas distribution different from LPG logistics?

City-gas distribution concerns a natural-gas network serving uses such as compressed natural gas for vehicles and piped natural gas for homes, businesses and industry. LPG storage and cylinder distribution are different physical and commercial activities. Similar customers do not make the fuels or infrastructure interchangeable.

A distributor's revenue is volume multiplied by realised selling price. Its earnings depend on gas procurement, transport, compression or distribution, network operation and other costs. More expensive gas can raise revenue while reducing affordability or the retained spread.

Customer mix matters. Vehicle use, household consumption and industrial fuel switching need different demand checks. Count active consumption as well as installed connections, and test the economics of new network areas rather than assuming mature-area returns apply immediately.

PNGRB's CGD framework separately identifies authorisation, transportation-rate, exclusivity and safety regulations. A geographical authorisation should not be treated as evidence of indefinite, unrestricted pricing power. Current rights and obligations require the applicable documents. [9]

11. Why do integrated groups need segment analysis?

A group can own production, pipelines, gas trading, refining, marketing and petrochemicals. One segment's input is another's output. High revenue can therefore hide weak economics in a large trading activity or offsetting movements across divisions.

GAIL's historical presentation separates transmission and marketing from petrochemicals and other activities. A volume increase in a pipeline and a better buying-and-selling spread in gas marketing are different drivers, with different repeatability. [6]

Aegis's FY2025 discussion supplies another useful example: gas-division revenue fell with lower sourcing volumes while higher terminalling volumes supported EBITDA. That is why revenue growth alone cannot tell us whether the underlying infrastructure economics improved. [4]

For each major segment, compare capital employed, operating result and cash requirements on a consistent basis. Then reconcile joint ventures, minority interests, group debt and transfers before moving from segment performance to the listed shareholder's outcome.

12. What should go into the downside case?

I would avoid making the whole thesis depend on one oil-price forecast. Test lower prices, weaker volumes, delayed commissioning and tighter financing separately, then consider combinations.

For upstream, ask about decline rates and the cost of replacement output. For services, contract expiry and idle time. For infrastructure, utilisation and tariff or contract changes. For refining, product spreads, outages and inventory. For distribution, procurement mix, customer affordability and network spending.

Policy, environmental obligations and safety belong in the operating model. A spill, outage or compliance failure can affect cash and permissions as well as reputation. Energy transition can alter demand, investment and asset usefulness, but a broad transition slogan is not a dated forecast for every product or region.

The final test is financial survival. Can the company meet interest, debt maturities and essential spending without depending on an immediate commodity recovery or an easy equity issue?

13. What would make the business understandable enough to study?

I would want a short, reconcilable operating sheet: the economic layer; attributable assets and output; charging unit; customer and contract; main cost; capital still required; and the bridge from operating profit to cash.

Then I would write down what could disprove the thesis. Higher production without better cash conversion, new terminal capacity without throughput, a growing order book without adequate rates, or more gas connections without consumption each points to a missing link.

The attraction of the sector is that physical assets, contracts and volumes can often be studied directly. The danger is assuming that the whole chain responds to one headline in the same way. Understand the payment first. Then judge the price, volume, capital and risk attached to it.
