Chapter 68. Central Banks and Monetary Policy

Chapter 68 of 85 · about 19 minutes

The central bank is the only institution that can print the thing everyone else must earn, which makes it the most powerful and most misunderstood actor in finance. Its decisions move your loan EMI, your FD rate, your bond funds and your equity multiples, usually within the quarter. This chapter is the owner's manual: what central banks are for, how the RBI's toolkit works, what QE did to the world's assets, and why credibility is the whole game.

68.1What central banks do: price stability, lender of last resort

Two mandates, ancient and modern, define the institution. The modern one: price stability, keeping inflation low and predictable, because money that loses value unpredictably is a tax on every saver and a sandstorm in every price signal. India's version is explicit: since 2016 the RBI has a statutory inflation target, 4% with a 2-6% tolerance band, set with the government, with the Monetary Policy Committee's votes published, accountability in writing. The ancient one: lender of last resort. Banks are structurally fragile, they borrow overnight and lend for years, so when panic runs, someone must lend freely against good collateral to institutions that are solvent but illiquid, or the run converts fear into failure. Every financial crisis in this book, 2008, 2020, the IL&FS freeze, has a chapter where the central bank's willingness to be that backstop was the variable that ended it. Around the two mandates sit the supporting roles: issuing currency, running payment systems, supervising banks, managing reserves. All of it serves the same product: trust in money.

68.2The RBI's toolkit: repo, CRR, OMO, liquidity facilities

Four levers, one transmission. The repo rate is the headline: the price of overnight central bank money, the anchor from which Chapter 45's corridor, and then every loan and deposit rate in the country, takes its bearing, through a transmission that is fast in money markets and slow, lumpy and incomplete in bank lending. The cash reserve ratio is the blunt lever: a percentage of every bank's deposits frozen at the RBI without interest, so raising it drains lendable money system-wide, a tax on intermediation used sparingly for that reason. Open market operations manage liquidity directly: buying G-secs injects durable rupees, selling them drains, and India's toolkit adds fine-tuning variants, variable-rate repos and reverse repos, term repos, and once, a twist-style operation exchanging short paper for long. The corridor, the SDF floor and MSF ceiling, pins the overnight rate around the repo. The investor's map: repo moves prices, CRR and OMO move quantities, and together they set the level and slope of the yield curve your bond funds ride.

68.3Quantitative easing and tightening

When the policy rate hits zero and the economy still needs help, the central bank moves down the curve: quantitative easing is the large-scale purchase of longer-dated bonds, paid for with newly created reserves, pressing down long rates, compressing risk premia, and deliberately pushing investors out the risk curve. After 2008 and again in 2020, the Fed, ECB, Bank of Japan and Bank of England bought assets on a scale that reshaped world markets: trillions in sovereign and even corporate bonds on central bank balance sheets. The effects were exactly as designed and exactly as debated: financial assets boomed, yields vanished, risk was repriced upward everywhere, and the distributional argument, that asset inflation rewards asset holders first, fuelled a decade of political economy. Quantitative tightening is the hard reversal, letting the portfolio shrink, draining the reserves, discovering whether the market can stand without the buyer of last resort. India ran its own mild version, pandemic-era G-sec acquisition programmes, without ever joining the full experiment. The investor's takeaway is permanent: central bank balance sheets are a slow-moving tide under every asset class, and a decade of falling yields is not a law of nature but a policy that can be reversed.

68.4Currency intervention and reserves

Chapter 58 described the lifeguard; here is the equipment. The RBI's reserves, among the world's largest stockpiles of dollars and other hard currency, accumulated precisely by buying dollars in good times, are the ammunition for bad ones: sell dollars, buy rupees, and the slide slows. The toolkit extends beyond spot: forward and swap operations shift pressure across maturities, and administrative measures, the NRI deposit schemes of 2013, deposit-rate windows, flow rules, widen the menu when spot intervention alone is too expensive. Intervention cannot repeal economics: a central bank defending a level the fundamentals reject will eventually run out of other people's appetite for its reserves, as every failed peg in history attests. What it can do, and India's doctrine says exactly this, is smooth the path, break the one-way bets that turn depreciation into panic, and buy time for the adjustments, rates, flows, policy, to work. Reserves are insurance, and like all insurance, their price, the low yield on dollars versus the cost of the sterilisation, is paid in quiet years so that the loud ones are survivable.

68.5Central bank independence and credibility

Underneath every tool sits one asset without a line on the balance sheet: credibility, the market's belief that the bank will do what it says, even when it hurts. The reason independence matters is a time-inconsistency problem: every government, facing an election, prefers easier money now and accepts inflation later, so societies hand the rate lever to insulated technocrats with long terms and legal mandates, trading democratic immediacy for stable money. Credibility is what makes the tools powerful: a credible bank moves markets with a sentence, because the sentence is believed, while a captured bank must prove every move with costly action. India's arrangement is a workable middle: the inflation target is set with the government, the MPC's votes and minutes are public, the governor serves at the government's pleasure but within the statute's frame, and the occasional public friction between Mint Street and North Block is best read as the system working, the tension between growth and stability institutionalised rather than suppressed. For the investor, the credibility variable is practical: it determines whether "4% target" is a fact to build portfolios on or a slogan, and the difference compounds for decades.

68.6Worked examples and cases

Case 1: QE and the post-2008 asset boom

Between 2009 and 2021, the major central banks expanded their balance sheets from a few trillion dollars to over twenty, suppressing long rates toward zero and below. Trace the mechanism into prices. With the risk-free long rate pinned near zero, every asset priced off it re-rated: bonds delivered a forty-year bull market's final leg; equity multiples expanded as discount rates fell, the DCF denominator of Chapter 15 doing exactly what the formula says; property cap rates compressed; and the hunt for yield pushed capital into credit, venture, crypto, anything with a promised return. The reversal, 2022's synchronised tightening, ran the same mechanism backward, and the casualties were the trades that had quietly assumed the tide was permanent: long-duration bonds lost more in a year than in any modern period, profitless growth stocks fell 70%, and the "new normal" turned out to be a policy, not a physics. The lesson to keep: when you are tempted to call any valuation regime eternal, ask which central bank balance sheet it is built on.

Case 2: the RBI's 2022-23 tightening cycle

India entered 2022 with the repo at the pandemic floor of 4% and inflation, imported through oil and supply chains, breaking above the 6% ceiling for months. The response: 250 basis points of hikes in under a year, off-cycle at first, then at successive meetings, with liquidity absorption running alongside, taking the repo to 6.5% before the pause. Follow the transmission through one household and one portfolio. The household: the floating home loan repriced within the quarter, the EMI up several thousand on a ₹50 lakh loan, while the FD renewals finally crawled past 7%, the transmission running faster into pain than into gain, as always. The portfolio: short-duration and liquid funds quietly repriced to 6-7% yields, long-duration bond funds took their Chapter 42 seesaw losses and then offered the best entry in years, and rate-sensitives, banks, housing, leveraged small caps, took the multiple compression. The cycle's reading lesson: the RBI told the market, in the minutes and the statement's vocabulary, what it would do before it did it, and the investor who read the forward guidance held the answer key. Central banking is telegraphed on purpose; the surprise is only for those not reading.
