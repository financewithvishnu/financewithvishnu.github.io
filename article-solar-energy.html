Solar: the cell, the module and the tender

By Finance With Vishnu · Source period: 2024; additional sources June 2025

Every panel on an Indian rooftop carries the same quiet question: which part of it was actually made here, and by whom? A module is mostly an assembly job. A cell is a semiconductor process. A solar pump is a government tender with a factory attached. Three businesses wear the same word, solar, and earn money in completely different ways.

FY2024-25 made the distinction matter. India's module-making capacity nearly doubled from 38 GW to 74 GW in a single year, while cell capacity grew from 9 GW to 25 GW, and the first commercial ingot-to-wafer plants only just began.[2] The listed players reported record numbers, and yet the risks, technology obsolescence, policy dependence and slow government collections, did not shrink. Follow the cell, the module and the tender.

Source period: 2024; additional sources June 2025. FY2024-25 annual reports provide dated checks. They are not current data. This is education, not investment advice.

What does a solar manufacturer actually sell?

Start with the product ladder. At the bottom is the module: glass, cells, backsheets and frames laminated together on an assembly line. Above it sits the cell, the silicon wafer that actually converts light into current, made through a chemical and thermal process that resembles chipmaking more than assembly. Above the cell sit the ingot and wafer steps, and above them polysilicon, the purified raw material India still imports almost entirely.

The ladder decides the economics. Module assembly is a low-barrier, high-throughput business: buy cells, laminate, sell. Cell manufacturing demands process know-how, yield discipline and far more capital per gigawatt. Ingot, wafer and polysilicon sit further upstream, where India barely participates today. A company reporting "solar manufacturing capacity" can be anywhere on this ladder, and the first question to ask is which rung.

The rung also decides which market the product may enter. The same 550-watt panel can be built from domestic cells or imported cells, and only the first kind qualifies for domestic-content schemes. Two panels with identical output are therefore different products with different buyers and different prices. Identity, not wattage, is what a policy-gated market pays for.

Why is a module plant not the business it looks like?

A module line is cheap to build and quick to commission, which is exactly the problem. Anyone with capital can buy the same laminators and the same cells, so the assembler is a price taker on both sides: it buys cells at market and sells modules at market, keeping a thin conversion spread. When India's module capacity doubled in one year to 74 GW against installations of roughly 105 GW cumulative,[2] the industry was telling you how low that entry barrier is.

The cell plant is the opposite: several times the capital per gigawatt, a process that takes quarters to stabilise, and a technology choice baked into the equipment. That is why the government keeps tightening the rules to push value upstream, and why a manufacturer's cell share matters more than its headline gigawatts.

Where in the chain is the real value added?

The value chain runs from polysilicon through ingots and wafers to cells, modules and finally the system on a roof, in a field or on a farm. India's strength sits at the module rung and, increasingly, the cell rung. FY2024-25 marked the first commercial ingot-to-wafer facilities in the country; polysilicon remains imported.[2] Each step a company integrates backwards adds value added and capital, and removes a supplier who can squeeze it.

Premier Energies describes its ambition in exactly these terms: a Mission FY2028 target of 10 GW of fully integrated capacity across ingots, wafers, cells and modules, a 2 GW wafer joint venture with Global Wafers, and even an aluminium-frame plant with a partner.[2] Read integration announcements as a map of where the margin pool is expected to sit, not as proof it is already captured.

What does a technology shift do to a factory?

Cells are where solar's technology treadmill runs. The industry standard has already moved from Al-BSF to PERC, is moving now to TOPCon, and HJT and perovskite tandems wait behind it. Each shift raises efficiency and quietly strands the previous generation's lines: a PERC plant built in 2021 competes in 2025 against TOPCon output with better yield and better pricing. The equipment is the bet, and the bet expires.

Watch how the leaders describe their own moves. Premier Energies commissioned a new 1.4 GW TOPCon module line in Telangana and a 1.2 GW TOPCon cell line during FY2024-25, with a roadmap to 8.4 GW of TOPCon cell capacity.[2] Waaree commissioned 1.3 GWp of module capacity in the year and built a 1.6 GW module plant in Texas, serving the market where policy pays for local content.[1] In this industry, capacity announcements are technology declarations. Ask which generation each gigawatt belongs to.

How does policy decide who is allowed to sell?

Solar demand in India is policy-made. The Approved List of Models and Manufacturers, the ALMM, restricts which modules qualify for government-backed demand, and its extension to cells tightens the gate further. Domestic Content Requirement rules reserve whole segments, such as the PM Surya Ghar rooftop programme launched in February 2024, for modules built from Indian cells. Basic customs duty raises the price of imported cells and modules. Each instrument converts a manufacturing licence into demand.

The flip side is dependence. A business whose order book rests on ALMM lists, DCR windows and subsidy schemes earns policy margin, not just product margin. When the rule tightens, the compliant few win; when it loosens or lapses, everyone competes again. Premier Energies explicitly anchors its demand visibility to DCR compliance,[2] which is honest and also a risk disclosure. Policy giveth the moat, and policy can taketh it away.

Who pays for all this capacity?

Manufacturing solar hardware is a capital race. Integrated cell-plus-module capacity costs several times more per gigawatt than module assembly alone, and the technology treadmill means the spending never truly stops. The funding stack behind it is equity raises, debt against order books, and internal accruals from the very margins the race is supposed to protect. A company expanding into cells and wafers is committing today to returns that depend on technology and policy three to five years out.

The balance-sheet question is therefore not "can they build it" but "what carries the plant until it earns". Premier generated operating cash flows of Rs 13,480 million in FY2024-25, the year it commissioned new TOPCon lines.[2] Strong current earnings fund the next generation; weak ones would force dilution or debt. Judge the expansion by the cash engine behind it.

What is different about the solar pump business?

A solar pump maker is a manufacturer bolted to a government programme. Under PM-KUSUM, states tender for farmers' pumps; the winning company supplies, installs and often services the units, and collects from the implementing agency over time. Over one million pumps had been installed under the scheme by FY2024-25.[2] Shakti Pumps holds an approximate 25% share of PM-KUSUM and reported revenue of Rs 25,162 million, up 83.6%, with an order book of Rs 16,546 million.[3]

The economics differ from panel-making in kind, not degree. Product and service quality win the tender, but the government's payment cycle decides when revenue becomes cash. Exports, 17% of Shakti's revenue and growing 52.7%, are the deliberate hedge against that single-scheme dependence.[3]

How does a government tender become cash?

The tender cycle has its own physics. A scheme allocates targets to states; states tender; bidders qualify and win allocations; supply and installation follow in tranches; inspection and commissioning trigger milestone payments; and the receivable then ages until the agency pays. Every stage after the win is working capital. The manufacturer funds panels, pumps, transport and field crews months before the money arrives.

Shakti Pumps discloses the cost of that wait plainly: receivable days improved from 178 in FY2023-24 to 152 in FY2024-25, better, but still five months of sales sitting as IOUs from government-linked buyers.[3] A tender-driven business can be profitable on paper and cash-starved in fact. The collection line matters as much as the order line.

What do order books really promise?

Solar companies advertise order books the way airlines advertise fleets. Waaree reported an order book of about Rs 49,000 crore as of June 2025; Premier Energies Rs 84,456 million; Shakti Pumps Rs 16,546 million.[1][2][3] Each number promises revenue, but the fine print differs: domestic versus export mix, cell versus module content, DCR versus open market, and the creditworthiness of the buyer.

Concentration is the quiet qualifier. One customer represented 55.05% of Premier Energies' revenue in FY2024-25, barely down from 58.90% the year before.[2] An order book is only as good as the customers inside it and the policy windows behind them. Convert the headline into questions: who buys, under which scheme, at what margin, and paying how late?

What do the FY2024-25 numbers actually show?

Set the three listed businesses side by side and the three models emerge. Waaree, the scaled module exporter, earns from volume and global channels. Premier, the integrated cell-and-module player, earns the widest margins from technology and qualification. Shakti, the pump specialist, earns explosive growth from a government scheme, and carries its collection cycle. All three grew fast; the composition of that growth is the whole analysis.

Where does the technology risk actually sit?

It sits in the cell plant, silently. A module assembler can switch cell suppliers overnight; a cell maker owns a process whose efficiency edge decays as the next generation ships. The TOPCon wave is today's answer; the HJT and tandem questions arrive before the TOPCon lines are fully depreciated. Waaree's own disclosures pair record capacity additions with the observation that solar is the cheapest new electricity in history, which is another way of saying the customer captures most of the progress.[1]

The test for any manufacturer is whether its returns survive the next efficiency jump. Falling technology costs grow the market and shrink the moat at the same time. That tension is the sector's permanent condition.

What can break a solar business?

Five breaks matter. Policy reversal: an ALMM relaxation, a DCR window closing or a subsidy scheme stalling removes demand the factory was built for. Technology stranding: a process generation expires before its lines pay back. Overcapacity: module capacity doubling in a year means conversion spreads compress until the weakest lines shut. Concentration: one customer at 55% of revenue, or one scheme at the heart of the order book, is a single point of failure. And collection risk: 152 receivable days means the growth itself eats cash, so a slowdown in payments turns profit into a funding gap.[2][3]

Notice that sunlight is not on the list. The resource is free and reliable. Everything that can break sits in policy, process technology, competition and the payment cycle.

The balance-sheet stress test follows directly. Take the company's receivable days, apply them to next year's planned revenue, and ask who funds the gap if payments slow further. Then take the cell line's technology generation and ask what it earns in the year the next one ships. A solar business that survives both questions has a real case; most pitch decks answer neither.

What would make us walk away?

Walk away from module gigawatts quoted without cell content, because assembly spreads do not survive overcapacity. Walk away when the order book hides one customer or one scheme, or when receivable days stretch while management celebrates revenue. Walk away from capacity announcements with no named technology generation, because an undated process bet is a depreciating one. And walk away from any solar story that depends on the rules never changing; in this sector the rules are the demand.

The keepers are the mirror image: integration that moves value upstream, a named technology roadmap with cash flows to fund it, customers and export channels that dilute scheme dependence, and collections that keep pace with growth. In solar, the panel is a commodity and the policy is fickle. The business worth studying is the one that still earns when both truths bite at once.
