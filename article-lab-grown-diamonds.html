Lab-grown diamonds: when more carats do not mean more profit

By Finance With Vishnu · Source period: April 2025

A lower diamond price can attract a new customer. It can also reduce the revenue earned by a producer, expose old inventory to markdowns and change the economics of grading or retailing. The same development can help one part of the value chain and hurt another.

I would separate the product question from the business question. What is a laboratory-grown diamond? Why does a customer want it? And which supplier can earn a return after production, selling costs and the capital tied up in stock?

The opportunity is not simply that more people can buy a larger stone. The harder question is who retains the value when supply improves and prices change.

1. What is a laboratory-grown diamond?

Laboratory-grown diamonds are diamonds produced through controlled growth processes rather than geological formation. They should not be confused with materials such as cubic zirconia or moissanite that can resemble diamond but have different properties.

GIA explains that laboratory-grown diamonds have chemical and physical properties closely corresponding to natural diamonds, while their growth characteristics can provide evidence of origin. Its research describes specialised testing used for identification. [1]

That distinction matters commercially. Similar appearance does not make origin irrelevant, and a visual demonstration is not a reliable substitute for appropriate identification.

I would also separate a scientific description from a claim about value. Establishing that a stone is diamond does not establish its scarcity, brand appeal, purchase price or resale value.

For the business study, the starting point is therefore a clearly described product with a known origin. The next step is understanding what the customer pays for beyond the material itself.

2. How do HPHT and CVD differ?

High-pressure, high-temperature growth, or HPHT, uses high pressure and temperature to grow diamond. Chemical vapour deposition, or CVD, grows diamond through a gas-based process in a controlled chamber.

GIA's technical explanation of HPHT and CVD covers both processes and shows that both can produce gem-quality diamonds. It would be wrong to label HPHT as exclusively industrial or assume that every CVD stone is automatically suitable for jewellery. [1]

Growth method is only part of the production economics. Equipment, power, growth time, usable yield, treatment and subsequent cutting all affect what can be sold.

A reactor's nominal capacity is not the same as output of commercially desired polished stones. The product must meet the required characteristics at an acceptable cost.

I would therefore ask about saleable output and customer specifications, not just the number of reactors installed. Growing more material is helpful only if it can be converted into products that customers want at prices covering the full cost.

3. Where does the value chain change?

A grower makes rough material. Cutting and polishing convert rough into usable stones. Jewellery manufacturing combines stones with metal, design and skilled work. Retail adds customer acquisition, presentation, service and distribution.

Grading and identification support trust across parts of that chain. Some companies participate in several stages, so the legal entity and reported segment need to be checked before assigning the economics.

A grower, a jewellery exporter, a retailer and a grading laboratory should not be valued as if their revenue drivers and capital needs were identical.

Nor would I assign a permanent percentage of industry profit to each stage. Competition and consumer behaviour can change how much value each participant retains.

4. Can demand grow while industry revenue falls?

Yes. Revenue depends on volume and the price earned per unit, with product mix affecting the comparison. More carats sold do not necessarily offset lower realisations.

In a hypothetical producer, volume is indexed at 100 and price at 100. If volume rises to 150 while price falls to 60, revenue becomes 90% of the starting level. A 50% volume increase accompanies a 10% revenue decline.

GJEPC's Q3 FY2025 trade report records declining export value for polished lab-grown diamonds over April-December 2024 compared with the year-earlier period. Export value is not the same as worldwide retail demand, but it provides a historical check against assuming uninterrupted value growth. [2]

I would separate rough stones, polished stones and finished jewellery, then compare like-for-like product characteristics where possible. An average price can move because the products sold changed.

5. Why is a weak producer return not a guaranteed price floor?

A new factory needs to justify the full capital invested. An existing producer may keep operating as long as the cash earned covers the costs it can avoid by stopping. Those are different decisions.

Low returns can discourage new capacity without immediately removing existing supply. Equipment may change hands, processes may improve or some producers may have lower costs than others.

That is why I would not conclude that prices cannot fall further simply because a model shows a poor return on capital. The model describes its assumptions, not an enforceable market floor.

In May 2025, De Beers announced its intention to close its Lightbox jewellery business and discussed continuing price pressure. It was a company announcement with a clear strategic position in natural diamonds, not neutral proof of the future for every lab-grown producer. [3]

The useful questions are which costs are falling, which producers remain competitive and how quickly supply can adjust. A difficult upstream business can remain difficult for longer than a simple cost-of-production argument suggests.

6. What determines a grower's return?

I would begin with the cash earned from saleable output, then deduct all operating and capital requirements. Power and reactor cost matter, but they are not the whole calculation.

Quality distribution, cycle time, downtime, treatment, processing losses and working capital can affect the result. A lower headline production cost may not translate into a higher return if the saleable mix is weaker.

A hypothetical ₹20 crore facility earning ₹3 crore of operating profit produces a 15% return before considering how the chosen measure handles tax, financing or additional capital. If the same operation also requires ₹5 crore of working capital, that simple return falls to 12%.

The example is deliberately basic. It shows why the denominator must include what the business actually needs, rather than only the price of its machines.

I would compare cash generation through a range of selling prices and utilisation levels. A model that works only at full output or assumes unchanging realisations leaves little protection if supply grows faster than demand.

7. Why can finishing and jewellery manufacturing earn different margins?

Cutting and polishing require skill and influence the usable finished product. Jewellery manufacturing adds another set of capabilities: design, metal work, consistency, delivery and the ability to meet a retailer's requirements.

Goldiam's FY2025 presentation describes its lab-grown-diamond jewellery export activity, manufacturing capabilities and product mix. It is evidence of a business extending beyond selling loose rough material. [4]

I would ask what a customer is paying the supplier to do. Is it supplying a standard stone, making a specific design, delivering repeatedly at scale or managing a wider programme for the retailer?

A more capable offering may support better economics, but the conclusion must survive customer concentration, pricing negotiations and working-capital requirements. Being further downstream does not automatically make a business better.

Average revenue per jewellery piece also needs care. Larger stones, more metal or a different design mix can raise the selling price even if the price of comparable loose diamonds declines. Do not compare those two prices as if they measure the same product.

8. Can cheaper stones improve retail profit?

They can, if the retailer retains part of the cost saving and demand is strong enough. But competitors may also lower prices, customers may negotiate and selling costs may absorb the benefit.

Consider a hypothetical item selling for ₹100. It costs ₹40 to source the stone and ₹30 for all other product and selling costs included in this simplified model. Contribution is ₹30.

If stone cost falls to ₹20 and selling price falls to ₹80, contribution remains ₹30. The margin percentage rises from 30% to 37.5%, but profit per item has not increased.

I would follow gross-profit rupees, customer-acquisition cost, store productivity and returns on inventory. A wider product margin is useful only after establishing what must be spent to sell the product.

9. Why does stock remain a risk after it reaches a customer?

Physical location does not settle economic ownership, revenue recognition or collection. Goods held by a retailer may remain subject to consignment terms and return arrangements.

Goldiam's FY2025 presentation identifies a substantial portion of inventory held with customers on consignment as finished jewellery. It also describes the handling of returns. This is a useful reason to examine commercial terms rather than count everything shipped as a completed cash sale. [4]

I would check inventory ownership, payment triggers, ageing, markdown exposure and the time needed to recycle returned products. The metal, stones and manufacturing effort can have different recovery values.

Falling replacement costs can also make older stock less attractive relative to newly sourced products. The accounting treatment and actual loss depend on the product and circumstances, so a blanket markdown assumption would be misleading.

The practical test is whether reported growth converts into cash without a persistent build-up of inventory or receivables. A strong retailer relationship can be valuable while still requiring the supplier to fund part of the stock.

10. Does a retail launch prove that the model can scale?

No. Opening a store tests one location and its initial demand. A chain must repeat the result after rent, people, marketing, inventory and central costs.

Goldiam's FY2025 presentation describes its ORIGEM retail initiative separately from its established export business. That distinction matters because supplying another retailer and operating one's own stores require different capabilities. [4]

I would ask whether early sales include launch effects, how mature stores perform and how much capital is required per store. Store-level break-even is not automatically group-level profit or an adequate return on all invested capital.

A simple expansion plan should include the cost of stores that take longer to mature. Advertising, training, returns and central support may grow before the network reaches useful scale.

Brand, trust and service can support the customer's willingness to pay. But those advantages have to be built and maintained. A large planned store count is an intention, not evidence that every location will earn a satisfactory return.

11. Is grading insulated from diamond-price declines?

A grading laboratory is a different business from holding diamond inventory. Its revenue can depend on submission volumes, service mix, fees and customer requirements rather than directly owning the stones.

IGI's calendar-2024 annual report describes identification and grading across diamonds, gemstones and jewellery, including laboratory-grown diamonds. It also discusses operational capacity and customer service. [5]

This does not make grading risk-free. If stone values fall, customers may become more sensitive to the cost of a report, choose different services or change which products they submit. The effect needs evidence, not an automatic one-for-one link to stone prices.

I would assess the number and type of submissions, fee realisation, turnaround time, laboratory investment and customer concentration. Growth through a new laboratory or acquired operation should be separated from like-for-like performance where disclosures allow it.

Accuracy and trust remain central. A profitable grading service must be useful to the transaction and credible to the people relying on it. It is not simply a royalty on every carat produced worldwide.

12. What must be disclosed, and what must be proved?

Product origin should not be blurred by marketing. The US Federal Trade Commission's jewellery guides, available through the eCFR, give guidance on diamond descriptions and clear qualification of laboratory-created origin. The linked section 23.25 also addresses laboratory-created gemstone descriptions. These are US guides, not a complete account of rules in every market. [6]

Scientific identity also does not prove that a purchase will retain value. A grading report describes assessed characteristics; it should not be treated as a guarantee that another buyer will pay the same price.

I would apply similar caution to environmental claims. A claim about lower impact needs a defined comparison, evidence on energy and production, and clarity about the boundary being measured. Laboratory-grown is not, by itself, proof of zero environmental impact. The US Green Guides on general environmental benefit claims warn against broad unqualified claims that cannot be substantiated.

Industrial applications require another boundary. De Beers' May 2025 announcement distinguishes its jewellery strategy from Element Six's industrial focus. Jewellery capacity should not automatically be assigned semiconductor or other advanced-technology economics without evidence of relevant capability, qualification and commercial demand. [3]

The research file should keep product science, marketing, environmental evidence and investment claims in separate columns.

13. What would make the business more convincing?

I would gain confidence when a company earns repeat business for useful capabilities and converts growth into cash at an adequate return. I would be more cautious when the case depends on a permanent stone-price floor, an untested retail rollout or a large industrial opportunity disconnected from present capability.

Lab-grown diamonds can broaden access to jewellery. Whether that creates an attractive supplier business depends on what happens between the growing chamber and the customer's final payment.
