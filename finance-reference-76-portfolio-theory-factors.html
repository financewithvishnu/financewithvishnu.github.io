Part XIV · Portfolio Management and Wealth

Chapter 76. Portfolio Theory and Factor Investing

Chapter 76 of 85 · about 20 minutes

Modern finance was born when someone finally asked the right question: not "which stock will rise?" but "what is a portfolio?" The answers, Markowitz's frontier, the CAPM's beta, the factor zoo that followed, are the operating system under every fund factsheet you have ever read. This chapter installs it, then honestly reports its bugs.

76.1Markowitz and the efficient frontier

In 1952, Harry Markowitz published the idea that won him a Nobel: a portfolio is not a bag of securities but a single object with an expected return and a variance, and the magic lives in the gaps between the holdings. Because assets do not move in lockstep, combining them can cut risk faster than it cuts return, the free lunch of Chapter 75's correlations, formalised. Trace every possible mix and the good ones trace a curve, the efficient frontier: the most return buyable at each level of risk. The investor's two lessons survive every critique. Risk and return are chosen, not found: where you sit on the frontier is a decision about yourself, Chapter 75's allocation. And diversification's benefit is a measurable quantity, the portfolio's variance is mostly covariance, so what matters about a new holding is not what it is but how it moves with what you own. Every portfolio tool since, optimisers, risk models, target-date funds, is Markowitz with better plumbing.

76.2CAPM, beta and what the market pays for

The CAPM drew the starkest line in finance: the market pays only for beta, exposure to the market's own risk, because stock-specific risk can be diversified away for free, and nobody pays for what is free. Expected return equals the risk-free rate plus beta times the market's premium; a stock with beta 1.5 should earn the risk-free rate plus one-and-a-half premiums, no more, no less. As description, the model fails its exams, small and cheap stocks earned more than beta allows, which is where Section 76.3 comes from, and as measurement, beta is a rear-view regression that mutates exactly when you lean on it. As framework, it is permanent: it separated market return from skill return, invented the alpha that every fund now claims, and installed the question this whole book keeps asking: which part of your return was paid risk, and which was earned? If you cannot split the two, you do not know what you are paying your manager for.

76.3Factor investing: value, momentum, quality, size, low volatility

The anomalies outgrew the model and became an industry: characteristics that historically earned more than beta permits, each with a story about why the premium should persist. Value, cheap by earnings or assets, pays the patient for buying the unloved; momentum, recent winners keep winning, pays for underwriting the crowd's slow underreaction, with crash risk in the small print; quality, profitable, low-debt, durable businesses, pays for boredom; size, small beats large across the decades, pays for illiquidity and neglect; low volatility, the dull outperform per unit of risk, pays for being unownable by the benchmark-hugging careerist. Two disciplines keep the zoo honest. Every premium has a behavioural or structural story and a cost, decades of drought, as value's 2010s showed, and a factor you cannot hold through its drought is a factor you do not own. And crowded factors decay: the premium is paid by whoever is on the other side of the trade, so when the whole market tilts the same way, the tilts stop paying. Factors are risk and behaviour, not magic, and they bill you in patience.

76.4Smart-beta products in India and abroad

The wrapper that brought factors retail is the smart-beta fund: an index, but weighted or selected by a factor rule rather than market value, momentum indices, quality indices, low-volatility indices, value-and-momentum combinations, now an entire shelf on Indian exchanges beside their American elders. The honest scorecard. The costs are genuinely low versus active funds, expense ratios in the tenths, and the rules are transparent, which answers Chapter 40's two worst problems. The capacity and crowding question is real in a narrower market: an Indian momentum index holds a few dozen mid-caps, and its own flows now move its constituents. And the tracking record is short and regime-stained: India's factor indices shone in back-tests built mostly on the 2010-2020 regime, and their live years have included stretches of drought exactly as theory predicted. Use them as the factsheet never says: as a deliberate, permanent tilt of a slice of the portfolio, one or two factors whose story you accept and whose drought you have pre-agreed to endure, not as a league table to chase quarterly.

76.5The limits of theory: models vs the real world

The framework earns its keep only with its failure modes printed on the box. The inputs are estimates: expected returns, volatilities and correlations are measured from a past that will not repeat, and optimisers, given garbage estimates, are error-maximisation machines that concentrate into whatever was mismeasured. The distributions are wrong-tailed: returns arrive with crashes no bell curve prices, and variance is an incomplete word for risk when the left tail is what bankrupts. And the agent is human: the model assumes you hold the frontier portfolio, while Chapter 77 documents what you actually do, so a theoretically perfect allocation you abandon in year two loses to a theoretically dull one you hold for thirty. The working synthesis this book lands on: use the theory for its architecture, diversify, price risk, suspect free lunches, distrust single-number risk, and use humility for the inputs, wide ranges, simple rules, and a preference for portfolios that fail gracefully over portfolios that optimise beautifully.

76.6Worked examples and cases

Case 1: back-testing a value-momentum mix on Indian data

The experiment many data vendors will happily run for you: an equal blend of a Nifty value index and a Nifty momentum index, rebalanced annually, against the plain Nifty, two decades, illustrative results. The blend's back-test gleams, a point or two of annual outperformance, drawdowns no worse, and the vendor slide concludes. Run Chapter 54's honesty checklist on it. The indices were designed on the same history they are tested on, selection bias baked at birth. The blend ignores costs: momentum's annual churn in mid-caps carries impact the index never pays. The two factors' droughts alternate, value died in the late 2010s while momentum feasted, then reversed violently in the 2020-21 rotation, so the blend's smoothness is partly the luck of this particular two decades. And capacity caps the whole game if the strategy wins disciples. The verdict is not rejection but sizing: the evidence supports a modest, permanent tilt, a satellite of 10-20% of the equity sleeve, with the expectation written down in advance, a point or two over long horizons, with years of underperformance as the admission price, and any back-test promising more is selling you the past.

Case 2: a factor fund's decade vs its benchmark

Follow one smart-beta fund through a full cycle, a composite drawn from real Indian and global factor-fund histories. Years one to three: the factor works, the fund beats the Nifty by four points a year, inflows quintuple, the marketing writes itself. Years four to six: the drought, value unloved or momentum whipsawed, and the fund trails by three points a year; the commentary blames the regime; two-thirds of the inflows leave, near the bottom, as inflows do. Years seven to ten: the premium returns, as premiums do, and the full-decade scorecard lands where the research said, a point or two ahead of the benchmark after costs. Now the audit that matters: the fund earned its decade, but the average investor in the fund earned a fraction of it, because they bought after year three and sold in year six, the gap between fund return and investor return that Chapter 77 prices. The lesson lands on both sides: factor premiums are real, patient money; the product wrapper cannot supply the patience; and the correct holding period for any factor is the one you wrote down before the drought, or it is not a strategy, it is a mood.
