Chapter 66. Collectibles and Emerging Asset Classes

Chapter 66 of 85 · about 17 minutes

Every generation rediscovers the same idea in a new wrapper: that a beautiful, scarce thing must be a good investment. Art, watches, wine, sneakers, cards, NFTs, the wrappers change and the economics do not. This chapter is a field guide to passion assets: what genuinely drives their prices, why the manias rhyme, and the three practical problems that separate owning a thing from owning an investment.

66.1Can art, watches, wine and sneakers be investments?

The blue-chip collectible markets are real: paintings sell at auction for crores, steel sports watches trade above retail for years, fine wine has indices, and sneaker resale became a genuine, if thin, market. Their return drivers share a family resemblance. Scarcity sets the stage: supply is fixed or throttled by design. Status demand sets the price: these are Veblen goods, where part of the value is the display itself, which ties prices to the wealth and mood of the global rich. And fashion sets the path: the artist, reference number or vintage in favour this decade reprices violently when taste rotates. Long-run studies of repeat art sales find equity-like nominal returns over very long periods, before costs, and the costs are where the comparison collapses: auction houses take 20-25% across buyer's and seller's premiums, insurance and storage run annually, and each transaction is a negotiation, not a quote. A collectible can absolutely make money; it cannot make it cheaply, quickly or reliably.

66.2What do sports cards and NFTs teach about the mania pattern?

The mania pattern is the chapter's central exhibit, and NFTs drew it perfectly. A new technology or platform lowers the barrier to creating and trading the collectible; a few headline sales, a card at a record price, an NFT at $69 million, recruit the public's imagination; prices detach from any anchor because there is no anchor, no cash flows, no yield, only the next buyer; leverage and borrowed conviction arrive; and the unwinding takes 80-95% off the peak, leaving the genuinely scarce items down half and the mass of items near zero. Sports cards ran the pattern in the 1990s and again in 2020-21. Beanie Babies ran it. The NFT version added a twist worth remembering: the blockchain delivered provable scarcity of the token, and the market discovered that provable scarcity of a link to a JPEG is not the same as value, because the scarcity that matters, of demand, was the thing that vanished. The pattern's tell is always the same sentence: "they are not making any more of these", which is true of every collectible and has never, by itself, saved one.

66.3Why is scarcity alone not an investment case?

The seductive logic goes: supply fixed, demand growing, price must rise. Each word deserves attack. Supply is rarely as fixed as advertised, the watchmaker adds references, the artist's estate finds sketches, the card company reprints, the platform mints a new collection. Demand is the unstable term, built on taste, wealth effects and liquidity, all of which reverse; when the marginal buyer is a momentum-chaser rather than a lover of the object, the demand curve is a rumour. And "price must rise" confuses a price with a return: a painting that doubles in thirty years has returned 2.3% a year before its considerable carrying costs. An investment, in the sense this book uses the word, is a claim on future cash, dividends, coupons, rent, earnings; a collectible is a claim on future taste. That does not make it worthless; it makes its price a vote on fashion rather than a share of production, and votes reverse in ways cash flows do not. Buy the painting because you want it on your wall for thirty years. If it also appreciates, send taste a thank-you note.

66.4What custody, authenticity and liquidity problems do collectibles have?

Three operational frictions separate collectibles from every financial asset you own. Custody: the thing must be kept, climate-controlled, insured, secured, at annual cost, and its condition is its value, one damp wall, one dropped watch, one faded signature repricing it brutally. Authenticity: the market runs on provenance and expert opinion, fakes are an industry, and the buyer of last resort is their own diligence; certificates help until the certificate itself is the forgery. Liquidity: there is no exchange with a last traded price; there are auction seasons, dealer spreads of 20-30%, and months of consignment, with the nasty property that liquidity vanishes precisely in the downturns when you want it. Fractional platforms, Chapter 61's caution applies verbatim, sell shares in these objects but cannot sell away the object-level frictions, only add a platform on top of them. Any collectible allocation should be sized like what it is: an illiquid, high-friction, taste-priced asset, wonderful to own, dangerous to need.

66.5Worked examples and cases

Case 1: the NFT boom-bust, anatomy in one chart

The chart indexes an illustrative NFT floor-price series against the S&P 500 over four years. The NFT line runs 100 to 2,400 in eighteen months, profile pictures becoming houses, houses becoming profile pictures, then round-trips to 260, while the S&P grinds from 100 to 168. Every element of Section 66.2 is visible: the enabling technology, minting and trading in seconds; the recruiting headlines; the anchorless pricing, there was never a cash flow to disagree with any price; and the asymmetric unwinding, in which the decline outran the attention that had inflated it. The survivors of the bust are instructive: the few collections with genuine communities and cultural permanence retain real value, and the other 99% of tokens approach zero, the power law of Chapter 65 wearing jester's clothes. The transferable discipline: when an asset's entire pitch is that someone will pay more later, demand the mechanism, the cash flow, the use, the enduring community, that makes "more later" something other than a hope.

Case 2: a collectibles index vs the S&P 500

The industry's favourite chart shows a fine-art index keeping pace with equities over decades. Read it the way Chapter 54 taught. The index is built from repeat sales, works that sold twice at auction, which selects for the winners: the painting that fell from fashion never reappears at auction and quietly exits the average, a survivorship bias built into the methodology. The returns are pre-cost: subtract the 20-25% round-trip auction friction, insurance and storage, and several points of annual return evaporate. The price series is smooth because it is sparse: appraisals and occasional sales hide the volatility a daily market would show. And the index is uninvestable: you cannot buy "the art market", only specific objects, with single-object risk on top of everything else. Netted out, the honest comparison flatters neither side: great collections assembled with expertise and time have made real money, and the index that markets them overstates what a buyer of one object should expect. Expertise and love of the object are the only edges available here, and neither comes with the purchase.
