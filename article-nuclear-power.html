Nuclear power: from reactor ambition to supplier cash flow

By Finance With Vishnu · Class date: 19 April 2026 · Sector study

When I study nuclear power, I separate two questions. Can the country build more reliable electricity capacity? And can a particular business earn a good return while helping it do that? The first question can have a promising answer while the second remains uncertain.

A reactor programme can create demand for specialised equipment, construction, engineering and maintenance. But a national capacity target is not an order for every listed supplier. An order is not the same as revenue. And accounting profit does not tell us when the customer will pay.

We need a map that connects the science to the equipment, the equipment to a contract, and the contract to cash. That is how I would approach this sector without turning a long-term energy theme into a shortcut for choosing stocks.

1. What actually produces the electricity?

A nuclear power station is, at a broad level, a heat source connected to a power-conversion system. Controlled nuclear fission releases heat. The plant uses that heat to produce steam, which drives a turbine connected to a generator. Fission means splitting atomic nuclei. It is different from the fusion process that powers the sun. [1]

This simple explanation helps us locate suppliers. Some equipment is close to the reactor and must meet demanding nuclear specifications. Other equipment moves steam, converts mechanical energy into electricity, removes heat or connects the plant to the grid. Buildings, water systems and project engineering are also necessary.

Do not mistake the diagram below for an engineering design. Real plants have separate systems, barriers, control arrangements and safety functions. For business study, the useful point is that a station is an interconnected project rather than one machine.

The business question becomes more specific: which component does the company supply, to which design, under which contract, and at what stage of the project?

2. Why do reactor types matter to investors?

A reactor label tells us something about its technology and supplier requirements. It does not, by itself, tell us whether the project will be profitable.

Design familyBasic distinctionWhy the distinction matters
Pressurised water reactor, PWRUses light water; steam for the turbine is produced through separate steam generators.Equipment must fit that reactor and its specified systems.
Boiling water reactor, BWRUses light water; steam produced in the reactor is used by the turbine.The steam-system arrangement differs from a PWR.
Heavy water reactor, including PHWR designsHeavy water improves neutron economy and can allow natural-uranium fuel without enrichment.The fuel route and equipment requirements differ from light-water designs.

The IAEA explains these differences between light-water and heavy-water reactors. Heavy water is water containing a heavier isotope of hydrogen. It is not a synonym for enriched uranium. Fuel enrichment and the water used as a moderator are different subjects. [2]

I would therefore be cautious when a presentation moves from experience in one reactor design to an addressable market across every design. A supplier still needs to show the relevant product, qualification, testing and customer acceptance. Technical adjacency is a starting point for research, not proof of revenue.

3. Is capacity the same as annual generation?

Megawatts describe power capacity. Megawatt-hours describe energy produced over time. A 700 MW station does not produce 700 MW multiplied by every hour of its life without interruption. Outages, maintenance, operating conditions and other constraints affect output.

Use a deliberately hypothetical example. A 700 MW unit running at full output for all 8,760 hours of a non-leap year would produce 6.132 million MWh, or 6.132 billion kWh. At an illustrative 80% annual capacity factor, generation becomes about 4.906 billion kWh. At 60%, it becomes about 3.679 billion kWh. These are calculations, not a forecast for an actual Indian unit.

For an operator, the timing and reliability of generation matter to cash recovery. For an equipment supplier, the more immediate questions may be manufacturing milestones and dispatch schedules. The same project can therefore have different economic clocks for different participants.

4. Where does the project spending go?

I would divide the project into work packages rather than assign a universal percentage to each part. Reactor design, local costs, project scope and accounting boundaries can make a neat capex split misleading.

  • Reactor-related equipment: specialised vessels or assemblies, steam generators where applicable, fuel-handling equipment and other specified systems.
  • Power conversion: turbines, generators, condensers and associated mechanical equipment.
  • Plant infrastructure: buildings, cooling and water systems, electrical equipment, instrumentation and controls.
  • Engineering and project work: design integration, construction, installation, inspection and commissioning support.
  • Lifecycle needs: fuel services, maintenance, inspections, replacements and eventual waste-management and decommissioning obligations.

NPCIL's FY2024-25 annual report describes capabilities spanning siting, design, construction, operations, maintenance, ageing management and life extension. That breadth is a reminder that the economic life of the sector does not end when a new plant is built. [3]

But a necessary input is not automatically an attractive business. Civil work can be difficult to execute. General equipment can face competition. A specialised component may require costly testing and long production cycles. We need to understand both demand and the cost of serving it.

5. Where can a supplier build a moat?

The interesting barrier is not simply being inside the plant boundary. It is the ability to deliver a particular product repeatedly, to the required specification, with accepted quality records and dependable execution.

A customer buying critical equipment cares about more than the initial price. Materials, traceability, manufacturing processes, testing, documentation and prior performance can all matter. Replacing an established supplier may introduce fresh qualification and execution risk. That can support an advantage, but we should verify the actual scope rather than invent a standard qualification period for the whole industry.

A useful distinction is between being allowed to compete and earning an attractive return. Qualification may establish access to a tender. It does not establish the selling price, the number of competing suppliers, the cost of warranty obligations or the amount of cash tied up in inventory.

For safety regulation, keep the institutions straight. AERB describes its role as carrying out regulatory and safety functions. NPCIL is a nuclear-power enterprise and operator, not another name for the safety regulator. Customer acceptance and regulatory oversight should not be casually treated as the same approval. [4]

My test would be repeat business with acceptable cash returns. An impressive certificate is evidence of a capability. It is not a substitute for that test.

6. What do the L&T and KSB cases actually establish?

L&T announced on 4 April 2025 that it had dispatched a fourth steam generator for the Kaiga programme, completing a set of four for one unit within the indigenous 10 × 700 MWe PHWR fleet programme. The company reported that the fourth dispatch was nine months ahead of its contractual schedule. This is dated evidence of a specific manufacturing delivery. [5]

Notice the limits. Delivery of a steam generator does not establish that the whole reactor has entered commercial operation. Nor does one early equipment delivery prove that every package in the project is ahead of schedule. We should preserve the milestone the source actually reports.

KSB's investor presentation dated 12 June 2025 describes its energy/nuclear pump division, its history of primary-coolant-pump work and product applications across primary and secondary circuits. It gives us a grounded product map rather than a loose association with the nuclear theme. [6]

It does not justify treating all KSB sales as nuclear revenue, or treating a product catalogue as a current order. The next checks are the disclosed customer order, the delivery period, the nuclear share of revenue and the resulting margins and working capital.

I use these cases to identify questions worth asking. They are not a ranking of investments, and neither dated document is a current valuation assessment.

7. How does a national plan become supplier cash?

There are several gates between a policy headline and money in a supplier's bank account. A proposed project needs a defined scope, approvals, funding and procurement. A selected vendor must manufacture and deliver. The contract then determines acceptance and payment.

The order book is useful, but it needs context. How much belongs to nuclear work rather than other industries? What is the execution period? Are prices fixed? Are there escalation provisions, advances, retention amounts or performance guarantees? Is the customer order firm, or is management discussing a possible pipeline?

A large order relative to a small company's sales can be exciting and dangerous at the same time. It can stretch manufacturing capacity and require purchases before customer collections. We should check whether the company can finance the growth without repeated dilution or expensive borrowing.

There is no universal accounting rule that revenue begins only at dispatch or only at commissioning. Read the company's contract and revenue-recognition policy before mapping a physical milestone to reported sales.

8. Why can long projects damage otherwise good economics?

Time changes the return on a project. A company can quote a sensible margin and still earn a disappointing return if cash remains tied up much longer than expected.

Suppose a supplier has a hypothetical ₹100 contract, with ₹75 of production costs and ₹10 of other allocated operating costs. The expected operating surplus is ₹15 before financing and tax. If delays add ₹6 of financing and storage costs, the amount left falls to ₹9. If an additional ₹4 of costs cannot be recovered from the customer, it falls to ₹5. These illustrative amounts are not data for either company discussed above.

The lesson is not that every delay destroys value. Advances, contractual compensation and cost-escalation clauses may help. The lesson is that a headline gross margin ignores the timing and allocation of risk.

I would track inventory, contract assets, receivables, customer advances, operating cash flow and contingent obligations alongside revenue. One weak quarter may reflect milestones. A persistent gap between profit and cash requires an explanation. Also check whether the explanation changes each year without the old balances clearing.

For operators, construction delays can postpone electricity sales while funding costs continue. For suppliers, delays elsewhere in the project can disrupt delivery or acceptance even when their own product is ready. Those are related risks, but not identical ones.

9. Is uranium exposure the same as equipment exposure?

No. The fuel cycle and the plant-equipment chain are connected, but their economics can move differently.

The US Department of Energy separates the fuel cycle into front-end preparation and back-end management. At a high level, the front end includes mining, processing, conversion, enrichment where required and fuel fabrication. The back end includes the safe management, possible recycling and disposal of used fuel. The route depends on the reactor and the country's fuel-cycle choices. [1]

A miner is exposed to resource quality, production costs, commodity prices and contracts. A specialised fuel-services business depends on its own licensed capacity, customer commitments and technology. A physical-uranium holding vehicle has yet another structure, with costs and a market price that need not equal its underlying asset value.

A pump manufacturer is not a direct uranium-price instrument. A rise in uranium prices does not automatically increase its selling price. Equally, more reactor construction does not guarantee higher margins for every upstream participant. Supply responses, inventories and long-term contracts matter.

Before comparing two nuclear-related companies, write down what each one sells and what makes its revenue change. If those answers differ, a single sector multiple can hide more than it explains.

10. What changes with small modular reactors?

The IAEA describes small modular reactors, or SMRs, as advanced reactors with capacity up to 300 MWe per unit. Modular construction aims to move more fabrication into factories and assemble units at the site. Designs and deployment plans differ, so SMR is a category rather than one standard machine. [7]

The economic hope is understandable: repeat manufacturing, smaller project increments and potentially shorter site work. But lower total cost for a smaller unit is not the same as lower cost per unit of electricity. Factory utilisation, design repetition, financing, licensing, fuel and operation all influence the result.

The IAEA's explainer explicitly cautions that economic competitiveness needs to be proven in practice. Its dated operating examples should not be read as a current global project list. A technology collaboration, a power-purchase agreement or an announced customer is also not evidence that a new plant is already generating commercial electricity.

For a supplier, I would ask whether it has a funded development contract, a qualified production component or only a general cooperation agreement. Revenue from an initial prototype is different from a repeat production programme. The upside from serial manufacturing belongs in a scenario until the orders and delivery system support it.

11. How should we read India's longer-term roadmap?

India's three-stage programme is a technology pathway, not a calendar that guarantees commercial outcomes. Its broad direction connects the existing heavy-water-reactor base, fast breeder development and eventual thorium-based deployment.

DAE's release dated 7 April 2026 states that the 500 MWe Prototype Fast Breeder Reactor at Kalpakkam achieved first criticality on 6 April 2026. It explains the role of fast breeder technology as a bridge towards later thorium use. First criticality is the start of a controlled fission chain reaction. It is not interchangeable with sustained commercial electricity generation. [8]

Thorium resources therefore should not be translated straight into near-term listed-company earnings. There are technology, fuel-cycle, regulatory and deployment steps between a resource and an operating system. A company that handles a related mineral still needs to demonstrate its actual role and legal access to a commercial opportunity.

Policy update, after the class: DAE records an industry consultation on 14 May 2026 about the nuclear roadmap, the SHANTI Act and industry participation. That is evidence of policy engagement. This article does not interpret the Act's licensing, ownership or liability provisions. Those require the operative legal text, commencement details and applicable rules, rather than a simplified claim that private participation is now unrestricted. [9]

Ambition can enlarge the opportunity set. It cannot remove the need to verify each company's rights, project approvals, contracts and economics.

12. What can go wrong with the thesis?

Start with safety and social acceptance. Nuclear projects require careful oversight and long-lived responsibilities. Site acceptance, water requirements, waste management and emergency planning are real considerations, not inconveniences to be waved away because the energy story sounds attractive.

Then consider execution. A design change can affect a supplier's scope. A delayed civil package can hold up installation. Inflation can make a fixed-price order less profitable. A customer concentration problem can become visible only after new orders slow.

Finally, consider expectations. A company may execute well but still be a poor investment if its market value already assumes rapid order growth, high margins and smooth cash collection. Conversely, a diversified business may have genuine nuclear capability without nuclear being large enough to drive the group's results.

I would be more confident after seeing repeated orders, credible delivery, a disclosed segment contribution and cash collection that supports the reported profits. I would be less confident if presentations expand the opportunity every year while actual orders, receivables and cash returns fail to follow.

13. How would I organise the research?

Begin with the product, not the share-price chart. Draw the plant map and place the company on it. Record the reactor types and systems it actually serves. Separate a current supply reference from a proposed expansion.

Keep four dates apart: the announcement date, the expected delivery date, the accounting period and the cash-collection date. Revisit the original promise when a new presentation appears. Do not let a revised target erase an old delay.

The attraction of nuclear power is not a reason to lower the standard of evidence. It is a reason to improve it. If we can connect technology, qualification, commercial orders, execution and cash, we have the start of a business study. Until then, we have a theme.
