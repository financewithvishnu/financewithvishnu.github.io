Niche manufacturing: when a factory becomes more than installed capacity

By Finance With Vishnu · Class date: 16 August 2026 · Sector study

A manufacturing company buys inputs, changes them through a process and sells an output. The interesting question is why a customer should buy that output from this factory rather than the next one.

The answer may be a lower cost, a difficult process, reliable quality, an approved design, dependable delivery or years of work inside a customer's supply chain. But a large plant, an impressive machine and a fashionable end market do not establish any of those advantages on their own.

My starting point is the part being made. What does it do? What happens if it fails? Who approves it? How many suppliers can make it reliably? And how much cash must the manufacturer invest before that capability earns an acceptable return?

1. Why is manufacturing too broad to analyse as one business?

A factory making standard products for a spot market has different economics from a factory producing a safety-critical part after years of customer testing. An electronics assembler differs from a designer of a complete electronic subsystem. A speciality additive differs from a widely available bulk chemical.

Grouping them under one manufacturing theme can hide the things that determine returns: the selling contract, material content, approval process, working capital, utilisation and capital required for the next rupee of sales.

I first separate the end market from the business model. Aerospace, defence, electronics and energy describe where demand comes from. Build-to-print, build-to-specification, process manufacturing and contract assembly describe the work being done. Two suppliers serving the same aircraft programme may occupy very different positions in that value chain.

Then I ask what the customer is paying for. Is the product valued mainly by weight and material grade? Is the supplier being paid for machining time, an assembled unit, a qualified process or responsibility for design and performance? This gives us a much better starting point than the sector label.

2. What does moving up the value chain actually mean?

A supplier can begin with a basic formed part, add precision machining, combine several parts into an assembly and eventually contribute engineering or design. Each step can increase the work performed and the responsibility accepted.

But higher revenue per kilogram is not enough to prove a better business. An assembly may contain expensive bought-in components. A higher invoice value can therefore come with a lower percentage margin or a larger working-capital requirement.

My evidence test is whether the new work produces repeat orders, acceptable yields, customer retention and cash returns. A presentation announcing an adjacency is only the beginning. We need to see whether the factory can execute it at commercial scale.

3. How do build-to-print and build-to-specification differ?

In build-to-print, the customer supplies the manufacturing information and the supplier produces to it. In build-to-specification, the supplier takes a broader role in designing and developing a solution that meets the customer's requirements.

Cyient DLM's FY2024-25 annual report describes both models. Its build-to-print offering starts with a customer-provided manufacturing datapack. Its build-to-specification offering includes design and development of complex, safety-critical products and their transfer into production. [1]

The distinction does not mean build-to-print is easy. Material sourcing, process control, traceability, component obsolescence and testing can still be demanding. A supplier may remain difficult to replace because it executes that work reliably.

Build-to-specification can deepen the relationship, but it also raises questions. Who owns the design? Who funds development? Who bears the cost if the product fails testing? Is engineering paid for separately or recovered only if production orders arrive?

I would not assign a fixed margin premium to either label. Contract terms, intellectual-property rights, programme risk and capital intensity decide whether the extra responsibility is attractive.

4. Why are certification, customer qualification and revenue separate milestones?

A quality-system certificate can help demonstrate that a supplier follows recognised processes. It is not the same as approval for every product or a promise of orders.

The IAQG's 9100 standard concerns quality-management-system requirements for aviation, space and defence organisations. That is an important foundation. A customer may still need to audit the facility, qualify a process, test a specific part and approve production before ordering commercially. [2]

Azad Engineering's FY25 annual report described more than 1,700 qualified parts and over 45 specialised manufacturing processes. These are examples of the accumulated qualification work a precision supplier can build. They are company disclosures, not proof that every qualified part will generate large or recurring revenue. [3]

The useful follow-up is conversion: which qualifications are entering production, how fast volumes are rising, what capacity is required and whether the resulting cash justifies the time and money invested.

5. What does an integrated manufacturing capability look like?

Happy Forgings' FY25 annual report describes a chain that includes die design, forging, metallurgical testing, heat treatment, precision machining and quality assurance. As of that reporting period, it disclosed forging capacity of 127,000 tonnes and machining capacity of 57,000 tonnes. It also reported that machined components generated 87% of revenue. [4]

These figures answer different questions. Capacity describes what equipment can process under the stated assumptions. Revenue mix describes what customers actually bought. Neither should replace the other.

Nor should we add the two capacity figures and call the sum finished-product output. The same material may pass through more than one process. A bottleneck at machining, testing or another step can limit what the integrated factory delivers.

When a company expands, I therefore ask which bottleneck the expenditure removes. More forging capacity may be useful only if there is enough downstream processing, customer demand and qualified labour to use it.

A good manufacturing analysis follows the whole flow from raw material to accepted dispatch. Looking only at the largest machine can miss the smaller process that controls throughput.

6. When does a small component have real pricing power?

A component can be a small part of a customer's cost and still be important to the customer's product. If a cheap input causes expensive failure, the buyer has a reason to care about reliability rather than price alone.

But small cost share is only the first part of the argument. We also need to know how many alternatives exist, whether switching requires testing and whether the current supplier has maintained consistent quality. An inexpensive part with many interchangeable suppliers may still face intense price competition.

Fine Organics' FY25 annual report describes plant-based, oleochemical-derived speciality additives used in foods, polymers, cosmetics, coatings and other applications, with a portfolio of more than 600 speciality additives. That is an example of why application knowledge matters: the customer wants a particular function in a formulation, not merely a quantity of material. [5]

The word speciality is not sufficient evidence. I would investigate product performance, customer trials, repeat business, competing suppliers and the ability to recover cost changes. A high margin in one period can reflect favourable raw-material prices rather than lasting differentiation.

The same discipline applies to engineering. A difficult part is attractive only if the difficulty creates value that the supplier can retain after scrap, rework, inspection and investment costs.

7. Why can utilisation change profits so sharply?

A factory incurs some costs before it produces its first saleable unit. Salaries, maintenance, rent and other overheads may not rise in direct proportion to output. That creates operating leverage.

The right question is how much contribution each additional accepted unit provides after variable costs. Only then can we estimate how much output is needed to cover fixed costs.

This example is deliberately simple. In reality, product mix, material prices, yields, labour shifts and selling prices can all change with output. A new plant may also need time to produce acceptable units consistently.

That is why installed capacity is a ceiling under assumptions, not an earnings forecast. A factory can be physically busy and commercially weak if it produces low-value work, rejects too much output or gives customers poor payment terms.

8. Why can growing profit still consume cash?

A manufacturer may buy materials months before delivery, hold work in progress during production and wait again after invoicing. Revenue and profit can be recognised before the money reaches the bank.

The main operating balances are inventory, receivables, supplier payables and customer advances. Their timing can change the amount of cash needed to support growth.

A small collection delay can require serious funding

For a hypothetical business with ₹100 crore of annual credit sales spread evenly through the year, 60 days of receivables is about ₹16.44 crore. At 90 days it is about ₹24.66 crore.

The extra 30 days absorbs approximately ₹8.22 crore, before considering inventory, payables or advances. This is original arithmetic using a 365-day year, not a forecast for a named company.

Cyient DLM is useful for understanding metric definitions. Its FY25 annual report presented net working capital of 127 days. A CRISIL rationale dated 22 September 2025 discussed gross current assets net of cash of 236 days as of 31 March 2025 and highlighted working-capital demands. These are different measures, not directly comparable versions of the same number. [1] [6]

The rating rationale pointed to matters including customer-specific receivable delays, acquisition effects and business with longer credit periods. The lesson is to examine the components and reporting basis before deciding whether cash conversion is improving.

I read operating cash flow over several years alongside capex, borrowing and advances. One strong year can reflect collections or inventory release that will not repeat. One weak year can reflect a genuine production ramp, but that explanation needs evidence rather than patience without a limit.

9. What does an order book tell us, and what does it leave out?

An order book can improve visibility, but its usefulness depends on the contract. A framework agreement, an estimated lifetime programme value and a firm purchase order are not identical commitments.

I want to know the execution period, customer call-off conditions, cancellation rights, price-reset mechanism and remaining costs. The number can look large simply because it covers many years.

There is also a cash question. Does the customer pay an advance? Must the supplier buy long-lead materials? Who owns dedicated tooling? When is the product accepted, and what happens if a programme is delayed?

A supplier embedded in a long programme may enjoy repeat work. It may also depend heavily on one customer or platform. Customer qualification can make replacement difficult in both directions: the customer needs the supplier, but the supplier may have invested in assets with few alternative uses.

My preferred bridge is opening orders, new orders, changes or cancellations, executed revenue and closing orders. Then I connect that bridge to delivery milestones and cash collections. A growing backlog is useful only if it eventually becomes profitable, collectible work.

10. Why is return on capital more useful than margin alone?

A high margin can look attractive while the factory requires a very large asset base. A lower-margin business can earn a better return if it uses capital efficiently and collects cash quickly.

A simple analytical bridge is operating margin multiplied by capital turnover. When the measures use the same revenue and capital basis, EBIT divided by revenue multiplied by revenue divided by capital employed equals EBIT divided by capital employed.

For a growing manufacturer, the return on the next project matters as much as the return on the old asset base. A profitable legacy plant can hide a weak expansion for a while.

I would estimate the full project cost, working capital, time to ramp, sustainable output and maintenance needs. Then I would test a slower utilisation ramp and lower realisation. A project that works only at near-full capacity and peak margins has little room for error.

11. How should we analyse incentives and localisation?

Government support can help a new supply chain reach scale. It can also make reported economics look stronger than the underlying customer proposition.

The Ministry of Electronics and Information Technology's Electronics Component Manufacturing Scheme was approved in March 2025 and notified in April 2025. Its stated objective is to develop the component ecosystem, capacity and capabilities and integrate Indian companies into global value chains. The official documents are the starting point for understanding its terms. [7]

That policy objective is not the same as a guarantee that every applicant qualifies, every announced plant will be completed or every recipient will earn a good return. Eligibility, investment, production, verification and payment are separate matters.

My test has two columns: economics with support and economics after support ends. I also check when the cash is received, whether there are conditions that could delay it and whether competitors receive similar benefits.

Localisation by itself does not establish pricing power. If many suppliers install similar capacity at the same time, a promising market can become competitive quickly. The durable question is what capability remains when the incentive period is over.

12. What can break a promising manufacturing story?

The first risk is demand. A supplier can execute well and still face a slowdown in the customer's aircraft, vehicle, industrial-equipment or capital-spending programme. Diversification should be measured by underlying demand drivers, not simply the number of customer names.

The second is execution. New products may take longer to qualify. New equipment may need process stabilisation. Scrap, rework or delivery failures can turn a high invoice value into poor economics.

The third is funding. Working capital, project overruns and acquisitions can require borrowing or equity. Growth in company profit may not translate into the same growth per share if funding is repeatedly dilutive.

The fourth is capital allocation. An acquisition can buy technology or market access, but it can also bring unfamiliar labour costs, weak assets or integration work. The strategic explanation needs to be followed by returns and cash.

Finally, valuation can amplify disappointment. Even a capable manufacturer can be a poor investment if the purchase price assumes a flawless ramp, strong margins and uninterrupted growth. A large opportunity does not remove the need to ask what is already expected.

13. What would I check before forming a view?

  1. Define the product, process, customer and failure cost. Avoid stopping at the end-market label.
  2. Identify what the supplier owns: equipment, process knowledge, designs, qualifications or customer access.
  3. Separate quality-system certification, part qualification, production orders, revenue and cash.
  4. Understand the material content and value added. Do not confuse a higher invoice with a better return.
  5. Find the bottleneck in the production flow and the realistic ramp of accepted output.
  6. Reconcile orders with execution, pricing terms, customer concentration and payment milestones.
  7. Read receivables, inventory, payables, advances and multi-year operating cash flow together.
  8. Test returns on the next project, including working capital and a slower-than-planned ramp.
  9. Separate policy support from the economics that remain after it ends.
  10. Compare the price with plausible outcomes rather than assuming that a good factory must be a good stock.

The central distinction is between owning capacity and owning a capability that customers value. The first can often be bought. The second must be demonstrated repeatedly through quality, delivery, economics and cash.
