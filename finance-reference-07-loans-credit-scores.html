Chapter 7. Loans, Credit Scores and Borrowing Wisely

Chapter 7 of 85 · about 18 minutes

A home loan at 8.5% builds a family an asset over twenty years. A credit card rolling at 42% eats the same family's future in eighteen months. Both are "loans", the way a ladder and a trapdoor are both "openings". This chapter gives you the maths to tell them apart, the truth about how EMIs actually split your money, and the one three-digit number that decides what every lender in the country will charge you.

7.1What is the difference between good debt and bad debt?

From first principles: debt is money pulled from your future. Whether that is wise depends on what the money does next. Debt that buys an appreciating or income-producing asset, at a rate below what the asset earns, can make you richer: a home loan below rental-plus-growth value, an education loan that raises lifetime income, a business loan that returns more than its interest. Debt that funds consumption at a rate far above any plausible return makes you poorer with certainty: cards, personal loans for holidays, BNPL for gadgets.

The honest test is two questions. Does this loan buy something that will be worth more, or earn more, than its total cost? And if income stops for six months, does the EMI still get paid? The first sorts good from bad; the second keeps good debt from turning bad.

7.2How does an EMI actually work?

An EMI looks like one flat payment. Inside, it is two payments that change shape every month: the month's interest on the outstanding balance, plus whatever is left over going to principal. Early in a long loan, the balance is huge, so interest eats most of the EMI and the principal barely dents. Watch a ₹50,00,000 home loan at 8.5% for 20 years (EMI about ₹43,400):

Outstanding principal by year. After 10 years, half the EMIs paid, roughly ₹34,00,000 of the ₹50,00,000 is still owed. Total interest over the full term: about ₹54,00,000, more than the loan itself.

Two practical consequences. Prepaying early is worth far more than prepaying late, because early rupees kill the balance that the interest is charged on. And a 0.5% lower rate is worth real money: on this loan it saves about ₹1,700 a month and over ₹4,00,000 across the term, which is why existing borrowers should always ask about a rate reset or balance transfer when rates fall.

7.3What builds and what breaks your credit score?

Every lender you will ever meet reads your repayment past before pricing your future. In India that past is summarised by credit scores such as CIBIL's, a 300-900 number built from your actual behaviour; the American cousin is the FICO score. The recipe is boring, which is the point:

  • Pay every EMI and card bill on time, every time. Payment history is the largest ingredient, and one 30-day late mark lingers for years.
  • Keep card utilisation low. Routinely using most of your limit reads as stress even when you pay in full; staying under about a third keeps the signal clean.
  • Do not apply everywhere at once. Each application leaves a hard enquiry; a cluster of them reads as desperation.
  • Keep old accounts open and mixed. A long, clean history plus a mix of loan types builds the score slowly, the way reputation is built.

The payoff is concrete: the same home loan can price a full percentage point apart for a strong score and a weak one, over ₹8,00,000 on the loan above. Check your report once a year, free, and dispute errors in writing; wrong loans on reports are more common than they should be.

7.4How do home, vehicle and personal loans compare?

Home loans are the cheapest large loans (about 8-9.5%, secured by the house), vehicle loans cost more and fund a depreciating asset, and personal loans are the most expensive because nothing backs them.

The pattern is the collateral: the less the lender can seize, the more it charges for the risk, straight from Chapter 3's grammar. Gold loans and loans against securities sit low because the lender holds your asset; the unsecured personal loan prices the lender's fear.

7.5Is a credit card a tool or a trap?

A credit card is two products sharing a piece of plastic. As a payment tool, paid in full every month, it gives you free credit for up to about 50 days, rewards, dispute protection and a credit history. As a loan, it is the most expensive mainstream money in India at roughly 3-3.8% a month, over 42% a year before compounding. The minimum-due button is the hinge between the two products, and it is engineered to feel like relief while converting you from the first product to the second.

Worked example: the minimum-due spiral

Spend ₹1,00,000 on a card at 3.5% a month and pay only the 5% minimum. Interest is charged from day one, on the full amount, with no free period on new spends. Paying the minimum alone, the balance takes well over a decade to die and the interest paid exceeds the original ₹1,00,000. The bank's favourite customer is the one who never defaults and never escapes. The rule that keeps a card a tool: full payment, every month, no exceptions; if a month cannot be paid in full, convert it to a cheaper EMI or personal loan the same week, not the same year.

7.6Worked examples and cases

Case 1: prepay the home loan or invest the surplus?

Same ₹50,00,000 loan at 8.5%, and a ₹20,000 monthly surplus. Prepaying saves a guaranteed, tax-free 8.5% (the loan's effective cost after any tax benefit you actually use). Investing in equity might earn 11-12% over long periods, with no guarantee and full volatility. The first-principles answer: compare guaranteed with guaranteed. If the loan costs 8.5% certain, only invest the surplus if you would also borrow at 8.5% to buy equity, which is what not-prepaying quietly is. Most families do both: prepay aggressively in the early years when the interest share is fattest, then let investing take over as the balance thins.

Case 2: the true cost of a "small" card balance

A ₹60,000 card balance at 42% a year, paid at ₹3,000 a month, needs about 32 months and roughly ₹36,000 of interest, 60% on top of the spending. The same ₹3,000 a month toward a 12% personal loan clearing the card instantly costs about ₹7,000 of interest over 23 months. The swap is one phone call and saves ₹29,000. High-interest debt is the only fire in personal finance that pays you to put it out early.
