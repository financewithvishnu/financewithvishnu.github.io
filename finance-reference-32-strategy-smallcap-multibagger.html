Chapter 32. Stock Strategies: Small-Cap and Multibagger Hunting

Chapter 32 of 85 · about 21 minutes

Every Indian investor knows a story about a stock that went up a hundred times. Fewer know the base rate: for each of those, dozens of small companies went nowhere, and a meaningful number went to zero with their promoters' reputations. Small-cap hunting is the highest-variance strategy in this website, the one where skill, luck and character separate most violently. This chapter gives you the checklist of the survivors and the obituaries of the rest.

32.1Why can small-cap stocks return more, and why do most not?

The case for small-caps is arithmetic and structure. Arithmetic: a ₹500 crore company can grow tenfold without needing the universe's permission; a ₹5 lakh crore company cannot. Structure: institutions cannot own the smallest stocks in size, analysts do not cover them, and so pricing is inefficient in a way it never is at the top of the Nifty. The case against is equally structural. Small companies die: one customer, one lender, one regulator can end them. Their promoters control both the business and the information about it. Their shares trade by appointment, so prices are opinions until you try to sell. The base rate from Indian history: a minority of small-caps generate nearly all the sector's excess return, and the skill that matters most is exclusion, removing the doomed from consideration before the promising are even compared.

32.2What is the checklist for finding multibagger stocks?

A multibagger usually starts small in a large market, earns high returns on capital, is run by honest promoters with skin in the game, carries little debt, and is bought at a price that does not already assume the future.

Read the table as a funnel, not a form: each row deletes most of what survives the row above, and that is the point. The two rows investors most often skip are the two that decide outcomes. Unit economics already proven, because a small company promising future ROCE is a venture investment, not an investment; and management quality, because in a company this size the promoter can legally enrich himself at your expense in a dozen documented ways, and the only protection is a track record of not doing so.

32.3How do you handle liquidity, governance and position sizing in small-caps?

Three disciplines keep the strategy's risks survivable. Liquidity: size every position to the actual traded volumes, assuming you may need weeks to exit and that the exit price will be worse than the screen price; the investor who cannot sell does not own a stock, he owns a stake in someone's private company. Governance: apply Chapter 10's checklist without the large-cap forgiveness, then add the small-cap specials: frequent name changes, auditor resignations, auditor shopping, sudden business pivots into whatever is fashionable, and equity dilution at any price are not flags, they are eject buttons. Sizing: many small positions, not few large ones, because the payoff distribution is a lottery you improve but never fix: a basket of twenty vetted small-caps can survive its five disasters; a concentrated bet on two cannot survive its one.

32.4When should you exit a small-cap stock?

Small-cap exits are decided by the thesis, not the price, and the discipline runs both directions. Sell when the story breaks: the runway proves smaller, the ROCE slides as competition arrives, the promoter does the thing you swore he would not. Sell also, partially, when the story outruns itself: a stock that re-rates from 15x to 60x earnings has paid you five years early, and trimming is how you keep the gain from being a loan. What you may not do is hold a broken story because the price fell, the "it will come back" that turns a mistake into an obituary. And you may not fall in love: the same promoter charisma that convinced you to buy will argue, eloquently, against ever selling. Chapter 14's one-page thesis, with its written kill criteria, is the entire apparatus. In small-caps, you do not marry the stock; you audit it, repeatedly.

32.5Worked examples and cases

Case 1: anatomy of a 20-bagger

Composite of real Indian small-cap successes, numbers illustrative. Year zero: a ₹300 crore market-cap company supplying a niche component, 18% ROCE, promoter owning 62% unpledged, revenue a tenth of its addressable niche, priced at 14x earnings because nobody covers it. The decade: the niche grows with formalisation, the company takes share from the unorganised sector, ROCE holds because the niche is defensible, earnings compound 25%, the market re-rates the stock to 35x, and the multiplication, 4x on earnings times 2.5x on the multiple, makes twenty times. Note what did not happen: no genius call, no timing, no drama. The buyer's whole job was the checklist, the sizing, and the refusal to sell a working story, three decisions repeated for ten years.

Case 2: three small-caps that went to zero

Three composites, three distinct deaths. The fraud: beautiful growth, receivables growing faster, a small auditor, promoter pledges, then a confession-shaped silence and a 95% fall; every element was on Chapter 10's checklist two years early. The leverage death: a real business, genuinely growing, funded by debt that assumed the growth; one demand shock, one lender's refusal, and the equity was junior to a liquidation. The fashion death: a company that pivoted into every hot theme, announcements quarterly, revenue never; the stock gave back each pump until dilution finished what disappointment started. The shared lesson: the zeros were visible in the governance and balance-sheet rows of the checklist long before they reached the price row. Exclusion, applied early, is the cheapest insurance in this website.
