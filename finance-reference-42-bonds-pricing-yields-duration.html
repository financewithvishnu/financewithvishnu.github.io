Part VI · Fixed Income and Credit

Chapter 42. Bonds: Pricing, Yields and Duration

Chapter 42 of 85 · about 21 minutes

Most Indian investors think of bonds as the boring half of the portfolio, something that pays 7% and never makes the news. Then a year like 2022 arrives, the world's safest bonds fall 20%, and everyone learns at once that a bond is a machine with moving parts. Part VI teaches the machine. This chapter is the core of it: what a bond is, why its price moves opposite to interest rates, and how to measure exactly how much it will move.

42.1What a bond is: coupon, face value, maturity

From first principles, a bond is a standardised IOU. The issuer borrows the face value, typically ₹100 or ₹1,000 per unit, promises a fixed periodic interest payment, the coupon, and promises the face value back on the maturity date. Three numbers define the whole contract. Everything else in bondland, prices, yields, ratings, spreads, is commentary on those three promises: what they are worth today, and how sure they are. Note the deep difference from a stock: a bond's best case is known in advance, every coupon and the principal, so bond investing is the management of the downside, the rate moves and the missed payments, rather than the pursuit of the upside.

42.2Price and yield: the seesaw every investor must feel

The single mechanic that runs all of fixed income: when market interest rates rise, existing bonds, paying their old, lower coupons, are worth less, so their prices fall; when rates fall, their prices rise. Price and yield sit on a seesaw, and no bond escapes it. The arithmetic is Chapter 2's time value: the bond is a list of future payments, and the market discounts them at the current rate. A 7% bond in an 8% world must sell below face value to compensate its buyer; at exactly what discount is the next section's job. The chart shows the seesaw's other half, the part that surprises people: the longer the bond, the longer the lever, and the harder the seesaw kicks.

42.3Yield to maturity and current yield

Two yields, two questions. Current yield, the coupon divided by today's price, answers "what income does this price buy me?": a 7% coupon at a price of 93 yields 7.5% currently. Yield to maturity, YTM, answers the deeper question: "what annual return do I lock in if I buy now and hold to the end, reinvesting coupons at the same rate?". YTM is the internal rate of return of the bond's whole payment list, the single number that makes two different bonds honestly comparable. When the business channel says "the 10-year yield is 7.2%", it means the YTM of the benchmark government bond, the price of the safest money in the country, from which every other interest rate in your life, your home loan included, is ultimately marked.

42.4Duration and convexity: measuring interest-rate risk

Duration converts the seesaw into a ruler. Modified duration, roughly the weighted average wait for the bond's cash flows, tells you the percentage price change for a 1% rate move: a 10-year government bond with duration near 7 loses about 7% when yields rise one point; a 2-year bond with duration near 2 loses about 2%. One number, and the entire risk of a bond portfolio is legible. Convexity is the correction term: the seesaw is not straight but curved, slightly in the holder's favour, so gains from falling rates slightly outrun losses from rising ones. The investor's working rules: match your bond holdings' duration to when you need the money; know that "safe" long bonds are safe in default terms and volatile in price terms; and never hold long duration through a tightening cycle by accident, which is what the world's bond funds did in 2022.

42.5Embedded options: callable and puttable bonds

Some bonds carry a hidden player. A callable bond lets the issuer repay early, precisely when rates have fallen and you least want your high coupon back: the issuer holds an option against you, and you are paid for it with a higher yield. A puttable bond lets you hand it back early, your option, for which you accept a lower yield. Indian investors meet the callable species constantly, in bank perpetuals and corporate issues, and the discipline is one sentence: a callable bond's "yield to maturity" is a fiction; compute the yield to the call date, the worst case you are actually underwriting, and let that number carry the comparison.

42.6Worked examples and cases

Case 1: pricing a 10-year bond as rates move 1%

A ₹1,000 face-value bond, 7% coupon, ten years to run, bought at par when the market's rate is 7%. Rates rise to 8%: each future payment is now discounted harder, and the price falls to roughly ₹933, a 6.7% mark-to-market loss on the "safest" asset in the country. Rates fall to 6% instead, and the price rises to roughly ₹1,074. The holder to maturity, of course, receives every promised rupee either way; the price moves only matter to the seller. That sentence is the entire philosophy of bond laddering, Chapter 43's case, and of matching duration to your actual liabilities: the seesaw is only dangerous to those who must get off early.

Case 2: why long bonds fell in 2022

Entering 2022, the world's central banks had pinned rates near zero and bought bonds by the trillion; the US 10-year yielded about 1.5%. Then inflation, declared transitory, proved structural, and the fastest tightening cycle in forty years drove that yield past 4%. The arithmetic of section 42.4 executed at scale: long-duration bond indices lost 15-20%, the worst year in modern bond history, and the 60/40 portfolio, built on the assumption that bonds zig when stocks zag, lost on both legs at once. India's version was gentler but rhymed: long gilt funds posted rare negative years. The lesson the year burned in: duration is risk, real and measurable, and "I hold government bonds" is a statement about default, not about price. The investor who knew his funds' durations read 2022 as arithmetic; everyone else read it as betrayal.
