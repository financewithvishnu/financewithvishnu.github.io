Chapter 30. Stock Strategies: Momentum and Trend-Based Investing

Chapter 30 of 85 · about 20 minutes

The most embarrassing fact in academic finance is that buying what went up works. Not always, not cheaply, and not without periodic disasters, but across two centuries of data and nearly every market studied, recent winners keep winning a little more often than chance allows. This chapter takes the effect seriously: what it is, why it persists, how to harvest it in India, and, just as honestly, how it periodically takes the harvest back.

30.1What is the momentum effect, and what does the evidence show?

The finding, replicated more than almost any other in finance: stocks that outperformed over the past three to twelve months tend to outperform over the next three to twelve. It held in US data from the 1800s to today, in nearly every developed market, and in India across multiple independent studies of the NSE's history. The likely engine is behavioural and structural at once: investors under-react to news, so prices adjust in drifts rather than jumps; herding then overshoots the drift. Momentum is, in other words, the market's habit of arriving at the truth late and then running past it. Understanding this engine matters because it tells you both why the effect exists and when it will fail: anything that relies on crowds arriving late will break precisely when everyone arrives at once.

30.2What is the difference between relative strength and absolute momentum?

Two flavours, often confused. Relative strength ranks stocks against each other: buy the top decile of six-month performers, hold, re-rank monthly or quarterly. It is a stock-selection tool, always fully invested, always long whatever is strongest. Absolute momentum, also called time-series momentum, compares an asset to its own past: hold the index while it trades above its ten-month average, step aside when below. It is a risk-management tool, sometimes in cash, and its promise is not higher return but smaller disaster, sidestepping the long grinding bears, as Chapter 17's moving-average system showed on the Nifty. The two combine naturally: relative strength picks the horses, absolute momentum decides whether the track is open.

30.3What is dual momentum and tactical asset allocation?

Dual momentum applies both tests at the portfolio level: hold the strongest asset class (relative) only while it trends up (absolute), else move down a safety ladder into debt or gold. A simple Indian version, Nifty versus gold versus a debt fund, rebalanced monthly on those two rules, has historically given up little return in bulls and avoided the deep drawdowns in 2008 and 2020. The catch is not conceptual but behavioural: the system will sit in debt while your friends' portfolios rally, will whipsaw through choppy years taking small losses, and will be declared broken on social media twice a decade, usually just before it works again. Tactical rules earn their keep in decades and get judged in months; that gap is the real price of admission.

30.4What is a momentum crash, and how do you live with one?

The strategy's signature disaster has a name and a shape. After a deep bear, when the market turns violently upward, the momentum portfolio is still holding the previous regime's winners, defensive, dull, falling knives avoided, while the new leaders are yesterday's most-hated junk, which now doubles in months. The result: momentum strategies suffer sudden, deep relative losses exactly at market bottoms, 2009 and mid-2020 both showed it in India. The mitigations are known: absolute momentum as a regime filter, partial allocations rather than all-in rules, rebalancing patiently rather than chasing the new leadership immediately. The honest summary: momentum is an insurance policy you collect in trends and pay for at turning points. Size it so the premiums do not bankrupt you between payouts.

30.5Which momentum funds and ETFs are available in India?

Indian investors no longer need to build the system by hand. Momentum index funds and ETFs, tracking NSE's momentum indices, package relative-strength selection with rules-based rebalancing at index-fund costs; factor indices combining momentum with quality or low volatility soften the crashes at some cost to the upside; and several PMS and smallcase offerings run the raw version with higher turnover and higher cost. The due diligence is Chapter 38's and 39's in miniature: read the index methodology (which lookback, which rebalancing, what buffers against turnover), compare the fund's tracking, and decide whether you want the pure exposure or a blended factor. The product handles the mechanics; only you can supply the temperament the strategy demands.

Build the canonical strategy: each quarter, buy the fifty Nifty-500 stocks with the best prior twelve-month returns (skipping the most recent month, the standard tweak that avoids short-term reversal), hold one quarter, re-rank. Three honest lessons. The gross edge is real and large; the cost drag eats a third to a half of it, which is why the index-fund implementation matters more than the stock list. The drawdown profile is different, not better: you swap the market's crashes for momentum's own. And the strategy's edge net of everything, a few points a year, compounds to a fortune across decades and looks like nothing across quarters, which selects, brutally, for the investors who can hold a rule they did not invent through a year when it visibly fails.
