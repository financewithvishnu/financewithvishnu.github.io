Chapter 11. Business Models, Moats and Management Quality

Chapter 11 of 85 · about 21 minutes

Two companies sell soap in the same city. One earns 30 paise on every rupee and could raise prices tomorrow without losing a customer; the other earns 4 paise and shaves them further every time a rival sneezes. Same industry, same shops, wildly different machines underneath. This chapter is about the machine: how a business actually makes money, what protects the making, and whether the people running it deserve your capital.

11.1What are unit economics: how does a business actually make money?

Strip any business to first principles and you get one multiplication: profit = volume x contribution per unit - fixed costs. The unit is whatever the customer actually buys: one soap bar, one trade executed, one loan rupee-year, one subscriber-month. The contribution is price minus the variable cost of that one unit. Everything else, factories, head offices, brand campaigns, sits in fixed costs that the total contribution must first repay.

Unit economics: profit is a multiplication before it is an addition.

Unit economics tells you what a company is. A business with ₹8 contribution on a ₹10 price can survive price wars, fund advertising and still compound; one with ₹0.40 contribution lives one cost shock from loss. When you read a business, find its unit and do this multiplication before you read a single ratio. The ratios are shadows of this arithmetic.

11.2What is an economic moat, and which five kinds matter?

A moat is whatever lets a company earn more than its cost of capital for longer than competition should allow. Five kinds cover most of what you will meet:

  • Brand. The customer pays more, or searches less, because the name lowers perceived risk. Test: can the company raise prices 5% and keep volume? Indian paints and biscuits say yes, repeatedly.
  • Network effects. Each new user makes the service better for every other user. Exchanges, depositories and payment networks are the purest Indian examples: liquidity attracts liquidity.
  • Switching costs. Leaving is painful: data, training, integrations, habit. Enterprise software and core banking systems live here; customers grumble and stay for decades.
  • Scale economies. The biggest player's cost per unit falls as it grows, letting it price where rivals bleed. Distribution networks and manufacturing scale are the Indian versions.
  • Licences and regulation. The state limits who may play. Exchanges, credit rating, deposit-taking: a licence is a moat dug by law, which is why it can also be filled in by law.

One moat is good; two reinforcing moats, brand on scale, network on licence, is where the great Indian compounders live. And every moat claim has a test: a real moat shows up as a ROCE comfortably above the cost of capital that survives a full business cycle, not a good year.

11.3How do you judge an industry’s structure with the five forces?

Porter's five forces, rivalry, new entrants, substitutes, supplier power, buyer power, is best used as one blunt question: who in this chain has the pricing power? Airlines sell a seat that is identical across carriers (brutal rivalry), need aircraft from a duopoly and slots from airports (supplier power), and face customers who compare on price in one click (buyer power): three forces against, and decades of Indian airline history confirm the arithmetic. A pipes maker selling through dealers who stock one brand, with fragmented resin suppliers and no substitute at the same price, has three forces in its favour. Do the force count before falling for a growth story: growth in a three-forces-against industry is growth that must be bought with margin.

11.4How do you judge management: capital allocation, integrity and skin in the game?

A business is a machine for turning rupees into more rupees, and management decides where each year's rupees go: reinvest, acquire, pay down debt, dividend, buy back, or, in the bad cases, leak to related parties. That repeated decision, capital allocation, matters more over a decade than any single year's operations. Judge it with the record, not the interviews: what return did past acquisitions earn? Did they buy back shares when cheap or when fashionable? Does the dividend policy make sense for the reinvestment opportunities actually available? Integrity is read in Chapter 10's checklist. Skin in the game is read in the shareholding pattern: a promoter who owns 60% and pledges none of it eats the same cooking as you; a management that pays itself lavishly from a small holding works for a different master. Promoter salary, ESOP dilution and the trend in promoter holding are three lines worth reading every single year.

11.5What is the difference between cyclical and secular businesses?

A cyclical business's profits ride the economy: steel, cement, autos, capital goods. Its P&L is a leveraged bet on the cycle, which inverts the valuation rules: cyclicals look cheapest, high earnings, low P/E, at the cycle top, exactly when they are dearest, and expensive at the bottom. A secular business grows through cycles on a structural trend: formalisation, premiumisation, financialisation of Indian savings. The error to avoid is pricing a cyclical as secular, paying a compounding multiple for earnings that will mean-revert. Ask of every earnings stream: does this grow because the tide rises, or because this boat is genuinely faster?

11.6Worked examples and cases

Case 1: the moat of an Indian exchange or depository

Take the business of running a stock exchange or a securities depository, described generically, numbers illustrative of the breed. The unit is one trade or one account-year; the variable cost of one more trade is close to zero, so contribution is nearly the entire price. Volume is set by the whole market's activity, not by sales effort. And the moat is stacked: a network effect (brokers and investors go where the liquidity and the securities already are), a licence (very few are issued), and switching costs (every broker's systems are wired in). The result in the accounts: operating margins of 60-75%, negligible debt, and a ROCE that stays high through booms and busts alike.

Contrast the commodity producer at the bottom of the chart. Its economics are set by a global price it does not control and input costs it does not control; the spread between them is its profit, decided in Shanghai as much as in Mumbai. Mid-cycle it earns its cost of capital; top-of-cycle it earns thrice that and looks like a growth stock; bottom-of-cycle it pleads with bankers. No amount of management brilliance fixes a missing moat, and the market, over full cycles, prices exactly that.

Case 2: reading a management through one decade of capital allocation

An exercise worth doing on any company you admire: list, year by year from ten annual reports, what management did with the year's cash. One consumer franchise shows the ideal shape: steady reinvestment at 25%+ returns, a rising dividend funded from genuine surplus, zero acquisitions, promoter holding constant and unpledged, promoter salary a rounding error on profits. Another, same industry, shows the opposite: five acquisitions of unrelated businesses at rising prices, dividends cut when the acquisitions soured, promoter salary climbing as profits fell, pledges appearing in year seven. The second company's management was never dishonest in the criminal sense; they were simply, consistently, on their own side. Ten lines of public record told you so in year three.
