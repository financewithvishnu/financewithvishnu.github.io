Domestic pharma: a brand must earn the next prescription

By Finance With Vishnu · Source period: FY2021-era; exact class date unconfirmed

Two companies can sell medicines for the same condition and still have very different businesses. One may have a familiar brand, a focused field force and dependable availability. Another may have a good product but weak distribution, an expensive launch programme or a concentrated portfolio. The manufacturing plant explains only part of the difference.

Domestic branded formulations are sometimes compared with consumer franchises because brands, reach and repeat demand can support attractive cash generation. The analogy is useful until it makes us forget what is being sold. A prescription medicine is not an ordinary packaged good. Clinical judgment, appropriate use, safety, affordability and regulation remain central.

1. What is the business actually selling?

A formulation is the finished medicine in a usable dosage form, rather than just the active ingredient. In a branded domestic business, the commercial system includes the product portfolio, medical engagement, distribution and availability. It can own manufacturing, use third-party capacity or combine both. Brand value is therefore not captured by the replacement cost of a factory alone.

The boundary matters when reading consolidated accounts. A company can combine Indian formulations, international generics, ingredient sales and contract manufacturing. Their pricing, customer power, regulatory exposure and cash cycles differ. A domestic brand franchise should not receive the margin or growth rate of an unrelated export project.

Indoco's FY2025 annual report discusses domestic formulations, international formulations and API activities separately. Sun Pharma's FY2025 report likewise distinguishes India formulation sales from overseas businesses. Those dated disclosures are reminders to identify the relevant segment before judging the whole company. [2] [5]

2. What makes a brand worth studying?

A useful brand study asks what makes the product trusted, available and commercially durable. Familiarity can matter, but a name alone is not a permanent moat. Competing brands, changes in treatment, price controls, quality problems and loss of availability can affect the franchise. The analysis needs evidence of persistence rather than an adjective such as iconic.

Separate the molecule, the individual brand and the broader brand family. Also separate a narrow subsegment rank from a company's share of the whole pharmaceutical market. Being first in one product category can be meaningful without making the company dominant across all treatments.

Indoco's FY2025 report describes brands ranked in their respective subgroups. That wording matters: it does not mean the same rank across all Indian medicines. JB Pharma's FY2025 report discusses flagship brands, therapy positions and domestic concentration. These are different denominators, each useful for a different question. Neither is a substitute for product economics or an independent clinical assessment. [1] [2]

A stronger investigation records the market definition, measurement provider, period, value-versus-volume basis and the exact product perimeter. A moving annual total is not a single quarter. A brand family can include more than one product. Growth should be compared on like terms before it becomes evidence of share gain.

3. How do acute and chronic demand differ?

Acute categories generally address episodic or shorter-course needs in this business framework. Chronic categories involve longer-term treatment. The distinction can affect repeat purchases, seasonality, engagement and portfolio stability, but it is not a ranking of medical importance or a guarantee of commercial quality.

Repeat treatment can support continuity of demand. It can still be affected by diagnosis, access, adherence, affordability, competing treatments and appropriate prescribing. Acute sales can be seasonal or disrupted by changes in consultations and illness patterns. A mixed portfolio may reduce some fluctuations while increasing the complexity of field-force management.

Do not treat every chronic condition as caused by lifestyle, or infer drug demand from a personal health anecdote. Those are clinical and epidemiological questions. For a business study, use the company's actual therapy mix and observed sales rather than inventing a disease forecast.

Therapy growth and therapy share are also different. A chart of growth rates cannot be read as a market-composition pie. A small therapy can grow quickly while adding little absolute revenue; a mature therapy can add substantial sales at a lower rate.

4. How much growth is really volume?

Revenue can rise through unit demand, price, a different product mix, new launches, acquired brands or a change in distribution arrangements. These mechanisms have different durability and capital requirements. A company buying a portfolio is not generating all the resulting sales organically.

Sun Pharma's FY2025 report attributes its India performance primarily to volumes and new launches. ICRA's dated FY2025 industry note separately discusses price growth, new products and some companies' share gains. Neither should be converted into a universal explanation for every domestic pharma company. Company-specific bridges are necessary. [5] [6]

Relative performance needs the same care. If a market falls 9% and a company falls 2%, the company outperforms by seven percentage points while still shrinking. A recovery against a weak base can look spectacular without changing the long-term economics.

5. What does field-force productivity measure?

Sales per representative can help assess whether the commercial organisation is becoming more productive. It must use the same time period, sales perimeter and headcount basis. Monthly productivity cannot be compared directly with an annual number, and closing headcount can differ materially from average headcount during expansion.

A rising ratio can reflect better same-team sales, price increases, acquired products, fewer representatives or a different territory allocation. It does not automatically prove stronger relationships or more prescriptions. Understand how the numerator and denominator changed.

JB's FY2025 report supplies an explicitly monthly field-personnel productivity measure. Sun's FY2025 report describes field-force expansion and wider geographical coverage. Those are useful operational disclosures, but they should be evaluated alongside costs, sales mix, retention and time to mature. An expanding team can depress near-term ratios before it produces enough additional sales. [1] [5]

6. Why is ethical engagement an economic issue?

Medical information and commercial promotion must not be confused with permission to influence clinical decisions improperly. A business model that assumes benefits to prescribers will secure demand has omitted an important legal, ethical and reputational boundary.

The Department of Pharmaceuticals' 2024 Uniform Code for Pharmaceutical Marketing Practices addresses relationships with healthcare professionals, including restrictions on gifts for personal benefit and pecuniary advantages. The document also covers other forms of support and professional engagement. This is a dated source, not a complete current compliance opinion, but it makes clear why commercial analysis cannot treat every promotional practice as an acceptable growth tool. [4]

For research, ask how a company trains and supervises its commercial teams, whether disclosures are clear, and how patient interests and professional independence are protected. Do not infer a violation from high marketing spending, or infer good conduct from strong sales. Both require evidence beyond a ratio.

7. How much pricing power does a brand have?

A familiar brand may have some commercial resilience, but medicine pricing operates within a regulated environment. Product inclusion, formulation, applicable orders and effective dates matter. A broad claim of unrestricted pricing is not a safe forecasting assumption.

A July 2022 government explanation describes NPPA ceiling prices for scheduled formulations and limits on increases in non-scheduled formulations under the DPCO framework. Its specific lists and counts belong to that date. An actual product model needs the applicable current notification and product details, rather than an old sector rule applied mechanically. [3]

There is also a difference between a permitted increase, a published maximum retail price and what the manufacturer ultimately realises after the commercial chain. Do not apply a headline retail-price percentage directly to net company revenue without checking discounts, channel terms, mix and volumes.

A conservative business model can test a scenario with little or no pricing benefit. It then asks whether volume, mix, productivity and costs are sufficient to produce acceptable returns. If the entire thesis depends on indefinite price increases, the brand deserves more scrutiny.

8. When does a strong portfolio become a concentration risk?

A few large brands can make a commercial organisation efficient. They also make the result sensitive to those franchises. Count concentration by revenue and contribution profit, then examine therapy, molecule, manufacturing site, geography and distribution. Many brand names can still share the same underlying risk.

Suppose five fictional brands supply 75 of total domestic revenue of 100. If one brand contributes 20 and loses half its sales, the total falls to 90 before any offset. That is a 10% domestic decline, not necessarily a 10% consolidated decline if the company has other businesses. Fixed commercial costs can make the earnings effect larger.

JB's FY2025 report describes a shift from five brands representing about 75% of domestic revenue in its March 2021 comparison to eighteen representing roughly that share in March 2025. This is a dated company-reported diversification measure, not proof that all risks disappeared. Acquisitions, therapy overlap and the economics of each addition still need checking. [1]

More brands are not automatically better. A long tail can absorb promotion and inventory without earning enough. Diversification should improve the resilience of cash returns, not just increase the number of products in a presentation.

9. What turns a launch into a franchise?

A launch is the start of a commercial test. It needs appropriate product access, supply, medical engagement, distribution and repeat demand. An announced division or product count says little about its eventual contribution unless the company explains sales, costs and progress over time.

An adjacent therapy can use existing capabilities, but adjacency should be demonstrated. The same prescriber audience, complementary products or shared distribution may help. New expertise, more representatives, working capital and promotion can still be required. The model should include those costs before awarding the mature-business margin.

The same discipline applies to purchased brands. Compare the acquisition price and follow-on investment with the cash the acquired portfolio can generate. Separate underlying growth from the simple addition of purchased sales. Distribution or licensing rights are not automatically ownership of every asset, and their territory, duration and obligations matter.

For an export opportunity or complex ophthalmic product inside a domestic-focused company, build a separate contract and product case. A finished-drug market size is not the supplier's revenue; approval is not launch; launch is not sustained profit. Do not turn a screen-time anecdote into a clinical demand forecast.

10. Where can the cash go?

A mature brand franchise may require less physical expansion than a new manufacturing complex, but it is not capital-free. Receivables, inventories, expiry/return exposure, field-force costs, product development, manufacturing quality and acquired brands all use money.

Cash flow from operations divided by EBITDA is not free cash flow. It can also be lifted temporarily by working-capital release or a small earnings denominator. Compare several years, investigate the changes and deduct the spending needed to sustain and grow the franchise under a clear definition.

High return on existing capital does not establish the return on the next acquisition or launch. Good mature brands can fund poor expansion. The capital-allocation record should therefore sit beside the brand record, rather than be inferred from it.

11. Is this durable improvement or a recovery?

Recovery can come from restoring supply, reopening consultations, using existing capacity or comparing against a weak period. Those are different from a sustained gain in market position or productivity. A stock thesis should name which mechanism is doing the work.

A company with unused capacity may grow with limited additional fixed investment, but working capital and maintenance still matter. Regulatory remediation, if relevant, has its own costs and uncertainties. A management statement about resolution is not the same as checking the exact regulator, site and permitted activity.

Indoco's FY2025 report provides a useful perimeter warning: it describes domestic growth alongside a different international outcome. A positive domestic narrative is not a complete description of the consolidated result. This article does not use the old recovery thesis as proof of current plant clearance or company-wide improvement. [2]

ICRA's dated industry note also identifies remediation costs and launch delays as risks in its broader pharmaceutical sample. That is industry context, not a finding about every domestic brand business or a present warning on a named facility. [6]

12. What evidence would change the view?

I would build the study around the existing portfolio, the incremental investment and the cash result, with dates and definitions beside each claim.

  • Franchise: product and therapy boundaries, concentration, availability and like-for-like market evidence.
  • Growth: volume, realisation, mix, launches, acquisitions and recovery effects separated.
  • Commercial costs: consistent field-force productivity, territory maturity and the spending needed to retain demand.
  • Constraints: quality, ethical marketing, product pricing and supply continuity.
  • Capital: cash conversion, working capital, capex, brand purchases and returns on incremental investment.

Warning signs include weak repeat demand after launches, productivity gains caused only by shrinking the team, growth dominated by purchased sales, worsening channel collections, overreliance on price increases or one therapy, and cash absorbed by acquisitions without adequate subsequent returns.

A brand can make the next prescription more likely only within a system that earns trust and keeps the product appropriately available. The business question is whether that system produces durable cash after every cost and investment. The investment question adds the price paid and the risk of being wrong. Neither is settled by calling the company an FMCG-like franchise.
