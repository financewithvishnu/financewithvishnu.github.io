What US Investing Champions teach: the trend template, tight bases and breakouts

Imagine two people buying mangoes in a market. One grabs the first heap he sees. The other walks the lane, looks for the ripest, firmest fruit, waits until the price is fair and buys only what she can afford to lose if the box is bad. Same market, very different results over a year.

The best stock traders in the world work like the second person. This study looks at the public methods of two of them: Mark Minervini, a two-time US Investing Champion, and Kristjan Kullamagi (known online as Qullamaggie). Both share the same core idea: buy strong stocks that are resting, not weak stocks that look cheap, and cut losses fast.

Short answer: pick stocks already in a strong uptrend, wait for a quiet, tight pause, buy as the stock breaks out of that pause, keep the loss small if you are wrong and let the winners run.

1. What the championship results do and do not tell you

The United States Investing Championship is a real-money, verified contest, which makes it more believable than a screenshot on social media. In 2021, Mark Minervini won the $1,000,000+ stock division with +334.8%. The contest organiser called it record shattering. The earlier record in that division was +119.1% (2020). Minervini also won in 1997 with +155%.

Do not skip this: contests show the winners, not the many who lost. A strategy that worked in the strong 2020 and 2021 markets can struggle in sideways markets. Treat the results as a reason to study the method, not as a promise.

2. Step one: only look at stocks in a strong uptrend

Minervini calls his entry filter the Trend Template. Think of it as a gate. A stock must pass every rule before it is even allowed on your watch list. The aim is to avoid stocks that are falling or going nowhere, however cheap they look.

The commonly published version has eight rules, using simple moving averages (the average closing price over 50, 150 or 200 days):

1

Price is above the 150-day and 200-day lines.

2

The 150-day line is above the 200-day line.

3

The 200-day line has been rising for at least a month, ideally four to five.

4

The 50-day line is above both the 150-day and 200-day lines.

5

Price is above the 50-day line.

6

Price is at least 30% above its 52-week low.

7

Price is within about 25% of its 52-week high.

8

Relative strength rank is above 70, meaning the stock beat most other stocks.

Why does this help? A stock that already beat most others has big buyers behind it, such as funds that cannot buy everything in one day. They buy in pieces over weeks. That steady buying is what keeps the lines stacked.

Everyday example

Think of cricket. You would rather back the batter who has already scored fifties in the last five matches than the one who has not scored in a year, even if the second one is "cheaper" in the auction. The trend template simply asks: who is in form right now?

3. Step two: wait for the tight pause (VCP)

Passing the gate is not a buy signal. A stock that has already risen needs to rest. Minervini looks for a Volatility Contraction Pattern, or VCP. In plain words: the stock keeps pulling back, but each pullback is smaller than the one before.

A textbook example used in many guides: a drop of 25%, then 12%, then 5%, then 2%. Each time sellers run out sooner. Volume also dries up, which means few people want to sell at these prices. The stock is like a spring being pressed down a little less each time.

2 to 6 contractions is commonEach pullback about half the lastVolume fallsNo breakout, no buy

The rule that protects you is the last chip. You do not guess when the spring will release. You wait for price to move through the pivot, and you buy only then. If it never does, you lose nothing.

4. A second style: the breakout flag

Kristjan Kullamagi trades a similar idea with a shorter timeframe. In his own words, the biggest winning stocks tend to move in stair steps: a strong jump of 20% to 50% or more, a pause, then another jump. He wants to buy near the end of the pause.

He describes the setup in three parts: a big move in the last one to three months (30% to 100% or more), then an orderly pullback with higher lows and a tightening range that rides the rising 10-day and 20-day lines for about two weeks to two months, then a breakout out of that range.

The exit matters as much as the entry. Kullamagi has described selling a third to a half of the position after a few days, moving the stop on the rest to the buy price, and then trailing the stop under the 10-day or 20-day line. If price closes below that line, the trade is over. He also warns against buying breakouts when the 10-day and 20-day lines are sloping down, because that is a weak market.

Everyday example

Think of a street vendor on a busy road. On a good evening, the crowd keeps coming and he keeps stocking. When the footfall dies for two evenings in a row, he stops buying more fruit. The trailing line is the same habit: keep going while the crowd is there, step back when it leaves.

5. The part that decides survival: risk

Both traders say the same thing in different words: you will be wrong often, so make each loss small. Minervini is known for keeping losses to a small, fixed percentage, commonly quoted as around 7% to 8% at most. Kullamagi moves his stop to break even once a trade works.

Position size comes from the stop, not from how excited you feel. Decide how much of your capital you accept to lose on one trade, often about 1%, then divide by the distance to your stop.

Why this matters: if your average win is three times your average loss, you only need to be right about one trade in four to break even (before costs). If your wins and losses are the same size you need to be right more than half the time. Small losses let you survive the bad patches.

6. A simple checklist

  1. Is the stock in a clear uptrend, with price above rising 50, 150 and 200-day lines?
  2. Did it beat most other stocks over the last year?
  3. Is there a tight pause, with smaller pullbacks and falling volume?
  4. Do you know the exact price where you would buy, and the price where you admit you were wrong?
  5. Is the position small enough that a stop-out costs only about 1% of your capital?
  6. Is the overall market healthy, or are most stocks falling?

If any answer is "no", the pro answer is to wait. Waiting costs nothing. A bad entry does.

7. How to practise without risking money

Open a chart of any liquid stock you follow and add the 50, 150 and 200-day lines. Look back over the last three years and mark every time the stock passed the trend template and then formed a tight pause. Note what happened next, including the failures. Do this on 20 charts before you consider real money, and talk to a SEBI-registered adviser about your own situation.

Remember that these methods were built and tested on US stocks. Indian stocks have different liquidity, price limits and costs, so do not assume the same results.
