Power piping: when specialised tubes become a good business

By Finance With Vishnu · Class date: 12 April 2026 · Sector study

A power station needs more than a turbine and a generator. It needs systems that move steam, water and other fluids through the plant. That creates demand for pipes, tubes, fittings and engineered assemblies. But the word "pipe" is much too broad to describe the economics of every supplier.

One company may sell a repeat manufactured product. Another may engineer and fabricate a customised piping package. A third may make large line pipes for transportation infrastructure. All three can benefit from capital expenditure, but they do not carry the same production risks, payment terms or capital needs.

I would start by asking what the customer is buying and how the supplier gets paid. Only then would I connect the business to a thermal-power, nuclear-power or wider industrial-investment theme.

Study window: the power-piping class dated 12 April 2026. Company illustrations use FY2024-25 annual reports and Scoda Tubes' prospectus dated 30 May 2025. Product and standards pages support definitions, not current order-book or commissioning claims. Announced expansions in historical documents are not assumed to be operational today.

1. Why is a pipe not a complete business description?

Think of the specification as the starting point. What moves through the system? Under what conditions? What material and dimensions are required? Does the customer need a standard length, a heat-exchanger tube, a shaped assembly or a complete module?

These questions affect manufacturing, inspection, pricing and qualification. They also explain why comparing two companies simply because both report tonnes of pipe production can be misleading.

The distinction is not "ordinary pipe is bad, specialised pipe is good." A scaled standard-product business can have excellent distribution and capital efficiency. A specialised fabricator can struggle with delayed collections. We need to study the full return on the money invested.

2. What do seamless, welded and fabricated mean?

Scoda Tubes' May 2025 prospectus describes seamless products made from round bars and welded products made by forming and welding flat steel strips. Its product lines include seamless pipes, seamless tubes, seamless U-tubes, instrumentation tubes and welded tubes and U-tubes. This is a useful starting vocabulary, not a universal ranking of quality. [1]

Seamless and welded describe production routes. Material grade, dimensions, processing, testing and the applicable specification also matter. A welded product is not automatically unsuitable for demanding work, and a seamless product is not automatically suitable for every demanding application.

Fabrication adds another layer. Pipes, fittings and other parts can be cut, joined and arranged into an assembly for a specified project. The customer may be buying engineering and execution as well as metal.

DEE's shop-fabricated-piping page describes engineering and prefabrication services, welding, inspection, testing and finishing. It also lists a range of materials, including carbon steel, stainless steel and alloy steels. That is a useful correction to the assumption that all power piping must be one exotic material. [2]

For financial analysis, record both the manufacturing route and the application. Do not turn a simplified materials explanation into advice on selecting or operating a real piping system. That is engineering work.

3. Where do tubes, fittings and modules fit?

A tube can be part of equipment that transfers heat or measures and controls a process. A fitting helps form connections or changes in direction and configuration. A fabricated spool combines parts into a project-specific section. A module can bring piping and other equipment together in a frame.

Venus Pipes' FY2024-25 annual report describes a product range serving industries including power, chemicals, oil and gas, pharmaceuticals and other process applications. Its expansion discussion includes fittings and specialised welded tubes. It is therefore more useful to study the product-and-customer mix than label the entire company a single power-sector proxy. [3]

DEE describes modular skids as systems that bring machinery, piping and instrumentation together within a frame or module. Its stated applications include petrochemicals, natural gas and energy projects. This shows how a supplier can move beyond selling separate lengths of pipe, although the claimed customer benefits still need to be tested in each contract. [4]

The business opportunity may lie in reducing the customer's coordination work or shifting fabrication away from the project site. The supplier must price the extra responsibility properly. More scope can mean more revenue and more things that can go wrong.

4. Why can standards and customer approvals matter?

ASME's public description of B31.1 Power Piping covers requirements across design, materials, fabrication, erection, testing, examination, inspection, operation and maintenance for relevant piping systems. That breadth shows why a low quoted price is not the only issue in a project purchase. [5]

We should still separate several kinds of evidence. A management-system certificate is not the same thing as customer approval for a specific product. Customer approval is not a purchase order. A purchase order for one application does not establish access to all reactor designs or all export markets.

My research card would record the issuing body, product scope, location, validity period and any restrictions. If the source only says "approved vendor," I would not translate it into a fixed market share or a guaranteed stream of business.

Qualification can make entry harder because a customer needs confidence in materials, processes, inspection and documentation. But the size of the advantage depends on the number of qualified alternatives, the tender rules and the cost of switching. There is no single approval timeline or compliance cost that applies to the whole industry.

5. How does a power-capex headline reach the supplier?

Electricity demand is several steps removed from a tube manufacturer's revenue. The utility may announce a plant. The project must progress through funding, design and procurement. The equipment or construction contractor then places orders for particular packages. A supplier manufactures, tests and delivers to the agreed schedule.

Replacement and maintenance can create a different demand stream from new construction. But even that needs a defined job, approved product and commercial order. We should not assume every ageing plant is about to replace every tube at once.

A supplier's direct customer can differ from the final plant owner. That matters because it changes who accepts the goods and who owes the money. Avoid naming a customer merely because someone inferred it from a large order announcement. An unnamed order remains unnamed until a reliable disclosure establishes more.

6. Why can tonnes and revenue move differently?

Revenue is affected by volume, selling price and product mix. Selling price itself can reflect metal costs as well as the value of the manufacturing work.

Suppose a hypothetical company sells 1,000 tonnes at ₹100,000 per tonne. Revenue is ₹10 crore. If volume rises to 1,200 tonnes but the average price falls to ₹90,000, revenue becomes ₹10.8 crore. Volume grows 20%, yet revenue grows only 8%. This is arithmetic, not a forecast.

I would ask for volume, realisation and mix separately where disclosed. If management reports only value growth, we may not know how much came from more output rather than metal-price changes. EBITDA per tonne can help within a consistent product mix, but it becomes less comparable when the company adds very different products.

7. Does passing through steel prices remove commodity risk?

No. First check whether the contract actually provides for pass-through, how often prices reset, what benchmark is used and which costs are excluded. A general statement that the business is "value added" does not answer those questions.

There can be a time gap between buying material and pricing the sale. Inventory bought at a higher price can face pressure when market prices fall. A fixed-price contract can suffer when inputs rise. Freight, energy and currency movements can add other mismatches.

Scoda's prospectus explicitly warns that it may not be able to pass increased raw-material costs to customers in all cases. That is a better starting point than assuming complete insulation from commodity prices. It also warns about the inventory and funding consequences of a long production cycle. [1]

The direction of margin movement is not automatic. Lower material prices can reduce revenue while leaving conversion economics reasonably stable, or they can create inventory losses. Higher prices can lift revenue while increasing the cash needed to fund the same physical stock. Read the contract and the inventory policy before deciding.

8. When does backward integration create value?

Backward integration means bringing an input or process in-house. In seamless products, the company may produce mother hollows rather than buy all of them. Scoda's prospectus describes that role for its hot-piercing mill, while Venus' annual report discusses its own piercing-line integration. These are dated descriptions of operating strategies, not proof that every later expansion has succeeded. [1, 3]

The possible benefits include better input availability, process coordination, quality control and retention of a margin previously paid to another supplier. The costs include equipment, people, energy, maintenance and additional inventory.

I would compare the saving with the full capital employed, not merely the gross margin. A process that makes the income statement look better may still reduce cash returns if it requires too much investment or runs at low utilisation.

Scoda's prospectus links its increased FY2024 working-capital days partly to the backward-integration strategy and mother-hollow production. This is a useful real-world reminder: integration can improve control while also consuming more cash. Those two outcomes are not contradictory. [1]

9. Why is new capacity not the same as new earnings?

A factory expansion creates the ability to produce. It does not create a customer obligation to buy. Production must become qualified output, which must become sales, which must become collections.

For a new product, the company may need trials, customer acceptance, repeatable yields and an effective sales channel. Depreciation and staff costs can begin before the plant reaches a useful operating level. The ramp therefore matters as much as the nameplate capacity.

Venus' FY2024-25 annual report discusses staged investment in welded tubes, fittings, seamless products and integration. I would preserve the distinction between the report's completed milestones and its future targets. A historical target date is not current verification of commissioning. [3]

Be careful with aggregate tonnes as well. Capacities for finished products and intermediate mother hollows are not necessarily additive end-market capacity. Counting both can double-count material that passes through successive stages. Different production lines may also have different product and size limitations.

Track the evidence in order: installed, trial production, qualified output, commercial sales and stable cash returns. If management changes the date, keep the old promise in the research record.

10. Why can growth require more borrowing?

A supplier may pay for material, hold work in progress and offer customer credit before collecting the sale. Fast growth can enlarge that gap even when each order is profitable.

Scoda's prospectus explains this timing problem directly. It describes inventory across raw materials, work in progress and finished goods, together with credit to customers and payment obligations to suppliers. Its risk disclosure is company-specific, so I would not copy its historical credit periods onto every piping business. [1]

Use a separate hypothetical example. A business with ₹100 crore of annual sales and 90 days of sales-equivalent net operating working capital ties up about ₹24.7 crore. At ₹150 crore of sales with the same 90-day assumption, it needs about ₹37 crore. That is roughly ₹12.3 crore of additional funding before considering capex, tax or dividends.

This simplified sales-based calculation is not a company's reported cash-conversion cycle. In actual analysis, inventory and payables are usually related to costs, while receivables relate to revenue. The example simply makes the funding consequence visible.

Borrowing is therefore not automatically evidence of bad management. The questions are whether collections are credible, debt is affordable and the return earned on incremental capital compensates for the risk.

11. What should an order-book review include?

Start with the boundaries. Is the number consolidated or standalone? Does it include all businesses? What remains to execute, and over what period? Are there cancellation or rescheduling risks?

A large order book can support visibility while still containing low-return work. Fixed-price exposure, customer concentration, retention clauses and performance guarantees can matter more than the headline growth rate.

For an engineered package supplier, design changes can lead to extra work and disputes about who pays. For a repeat-product manufacturer, batch scheduling, mix and material procurement may be more central. Neither model removes acceptance and collection risk.

Reconcile the opening order book with new wins, execution, cancellations and the closing balance where disclosures permit. Then reconcile sales with contract assets, receivables and cash. If the company reports monthly orders, that provides useful frequency, but it does not remove the need to study terms and actual execution.

I would also check whether the business is taking work outside its demonstrated capability merely to keep the factory busy. More orders are valuable only when the company can deliver and collect at an acceptable return.

12. Is every pipe company a clean power-capex proxy?

No. Welspun Corp's FY2024-25 annual report describes a diversified group spanning line pipes, ductile-iron pipes, stainless-steel products and building-material and water-management businesses. The mix includes different end markets and geographies. That matters when interpreting a theme such as gas infrastructure or data-centre-related power demand. [6]

Even if one division supplies a project linked to rising power demand, the group's results also depend on its other businesses. A thematic label is not a disclosed revenue segment. I would not invent an "AI share of sales" from a general pipeline narrative.

The same discipline applies to nuclear opportunities. A product fit, an approval or an announced investment may create an option. It does not prove a confirmed order, and it does not tell us when cash will arrive.

Geographic diversification can spread demand exposure but adds currency, trade, logistics and local execution risks. Do not assume that several plants make a company safer in every respect, or that a single location automatically makes it unattractive. Compare the actual benefits and exposures.

13. What would make the business worth deeper study?

I would look for a supplier that can explain its product advantage without relying on a sector slogan. The advantage should show up in customer acceptance, repeat orders, stable execution and returns on capital.

Then test the downside. What happens if utilisation rises more slowly, material prices move against inventory or the customer pays later? Is the balance sheet strong enough to finish the expansion without relying on an optimistic forecast?

Finally, compare those economics with the expectations in the valuation. A genuinely capable supplier can still be an unattractive investment at the wrong price. A smaller revenue base can produce faster percentage growth without making the company better.

The useful opportunity in power piping is not that every pipe will earn a premium. It is that some suppliers may combine demanding applications with reliable execution. Our job is to establish which ones do, and whether the resulting cash return justifies the capital and the risk.
