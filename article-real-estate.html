Real estate developers: follow the project cash, not just the land bank

By Finance With Vishnu · Source period: March 2022 · Company data through: March 2026

A developer can report record bookings while accounting revenue remains weak. It can collect substantial cash without that cash being free for shareholders. It can own valuable land and still need more funding before the land produces a return.

Those are not contradictions. They are different stages of a long project cycle. I would start by separating the clocks, then ask how much of the eventual cash actually belongs to the listed company.

The key question is not how large the land bank or development pipeline looks. It is what must be spent, paid away and waited for before that pipeline becomes attributable cash.

1. What kind of property business is this?

Residential development, office leasing, retail property, hospitality, plotted development and construction contracting have different cash patterns. A company can contain several of them.

A developer selling homes monetises a project over time and must replenish its pipeline. A landlord retains an operating asset and earns rent, while funding maintenance, tenant requirements and occasional redevelopment. A contractor may carry execution and receivable risk without owning the land or unsold homes.

DLF's FY2025-26 results separately report development and rental businesses, ending the year with new sales bookings of Rs 20,143 crore and a net cash position of Rs 14,155 crore. Oberoi Realty's FY2025-26 report describes real-estate development and hospitality and separately presents its share of joint-venture and associate profit. These boundaries matter before comparing growth or applying one multiple to the whole group. [1, 2]

First identify the legal entities, consolidation method and economic interest. A project advertised under a familiar brand does not automatically belong entirely to the listed company.

2. How does the local capital cycle work?

Strong prices and returns attract land purchases, launches and competing supply. But land assembly, approvals, finance and construction take time. Much of the new supply can arrive after the original demand signal has weakened.

The reverse can happen after a downturn. Weak developers stop acquiring projects, lenders become cautious and launches decline. Surviving operators may later face less competition. That is a possible mechanism, not a guaranteed recovery date.

Track supply and demand within the relevant micro-market and price segment. A national housing shortage does not establish demand for a particular luxury tower. A successful project in one suburb does not prove that a distant land parcel will sell at the same speed or price.

Affordability also has several dimensions: home price relative to income, instalments relative to income, the down payment and total ownership costs. A lower quoted mortgage rate cannot fix a buyer's missing deposit or uncertain income.

For developer research, compare launches, absorption, cancellations, unsold stock, collections and funding. A large reported sales value can reflect higher prices or a different mix rather than more homes sold.

3. Why do bookings, collections and revenue disagree?

Bookings record an agreed sale under the company's reporting definition. Billing records a payment demand. Collections record cash received. Accounting revenue follows the relevant recognition requirements. Construction and handover add further milestones.

A fresh launch can lift bookings before significant collections or recognised revenue. A completion-heavy period can lift revenue from homes booked years earlier even if current bookings are weak.

Godrej Properties' FY2025-26 report shows booking value, collections, deliveries and revenue as separate indicators: record bookings of Rs 34,171 crore and collections of Rs 19,965 crore sit alongside recognised revenue of Rs 8,374 crore. Sunteck's FY2025-26 report likewise distinguishes pre-sales, collections, accounting results and development pipeline. That separation is a better starting model than treating every sales-related number as the same cash flow. [3, 4]

Always ask whether bookings are gross or net of cancellations, project-wide or attributable, and on which area and tax basis.

4. Is revenue always recognised only at handover?

No universal shortcut works. Ind AS115 distinguishes performance obligations satisfied over time from those satisfied at a point in time. The contract, control and enforceable rights matter.

ICAI's July 2018 clarification specifically rejects the claim that Ind AS115 rules out percentage-of-completion recognition for real estate. The standard permits over-time recognition when the relevant criteria are met. Where they are not, revenue is recognised at a point in time when control transfers. This is not permission to choose whichever method produces smoother profit. [5]

Read the developer's actual accounting policy and contracts before modelling the earnings schedule. Do not assume that two companies with the same bookings recognise the same revenue in the same year.

Costs have their own rules. Under Ind AS2, qualifying inventory costs are carried as an asset and expensed with the related revenue; inventories are measured at the lower of cost and net realisable value. Not every salary, selling cost or cash payment belongs in project inventory. Borrowing-cost capitalisation has separate conditions. [6]

Reconcile cash spent, inventory additions, recognised cost of sales, customer advances and revenue. Weak reported profit may reflect project timing, but timing should not become an excuse to ignore cost overruns or impaired inventory.

5. What does the collection ratio actually tell us?

Collections divided by current-period bookings is a useful movement indicator, not the percentage that every new buyer has already paid.

A high ratio can reflect mature projects collecting old instalments. A low ratio can reflect launch-heavy growth with payments not yet due. Neither proves good or bad credit quality alone.

Separate amounts due, collected, overdue, disputed and cancelled. Check refunds and the ageing of receivables. Then connect the collection schedule with the remaining construction and statutory payments.

Customer advances can support execution, but they come with delivery obligations. They are not equivalent to unrestricted capital that can always be moved into another project or distributed.

6. How much project cash is really left?

Start with collections, not lifetime revenue potential. Deduct the obligations that must be met and keep the timing visible.

This is not a statutory cash-flow statement. It illustrates why project profit, project operating cash flow and cash available to equity holders can be very different.

Godrej's FY2025-26 report presents operating cash flow of Rs 7,830 crore as a headline operating metric, distinct from the statutory cash-flow statement. Company-defined operating metrics need their definitions carried with them. A headline operating-cash-flow figure should not be relabelled statutory CFO or free cash flow. [3]

At group level, also map debt maturities, guarantees, partner claims and restricted balances. A project can be profitable over its life yet face a funding gap because payments fall due before collections arrive.

7. What protection does a project account provide?

RERA's separate-account mechanism is important, but it is not a delivery guarantee or an assertion that every rupee can be used freely once collected.

Section 4(2)(l)(D) of the Act provides for 70% of amounts realised from allottees to be deposited in a separate scheduled-bank account for that project's land and construction costs. Withdrawals are linked to completion and prescribed professional certification. The provision also includes an audit requirement. [7]

That is different from saying 70% is reserved only for construction, or that account segregation eliminates title, execution and financing risk. Registration exceptions and applicable state/project requirements also matter.

For a real transaction, inspect the exact project registration, disclosures, contracts and applicable rules rather than relying on this general summary. For equity research, investigate where cash sits and whether it is available to meet obligations elsewhere in the group.

8. Does a JDA remove capital risk?

A joint-development agreement can pair a landowner's rights with a developer's brand, approvals, funding, construction and sales capability. It may reduce the initial cash required to buy land outright.

The economic split could be revenue share, area share, profit share or another negotiated arrangement. Deposits, minimum guarantees, timing, cost inflation and termination rights can change the result substantially.

Sunteck's FY2026 report distinguishes outright ownership, joint ventures/joint-development arrangements and redevelopment, as well as projects at different stages. That is a map of differing rights and obligations, not a uniform asset-light profit margin. [4]

A top-line landowner entitlement can remain due while construction costs rise, leaving the developer with a smaller residual. A partner dispute can also interrupt approvals or access to cash.

Write the waterfall before estimating equity value. Lower upfront land spending can improve returns, but it does not make title, funding, execution or counterparty risk disappear.

9. How much of GDV belongs to shareholders?

Gross development value is an estimate of sales potential, not profit, current cash or equity NAV. Its area, price, stage, timing and ownership assumptions need to be explicit.

The exact waterfall depends on the agreement. The example is not a standard sector margin. Apply partner shares and costs at the correct level and avoid deducting the same obligation twice.

Unlaunched land, approved phases, booked units, near-complete inventory and finished unsold homes have different risk and timing. Acres alone do not establish saleable area, permitted use or monetisation speed.

Book inventory is also not a liquidation floor. Selling costs, discounts, taxes, encumbrances and time can reduce the cash realised.

10. Why is old land not economically free?

A low historical carrying cost can make reported project margins attractive. But the land still has an opportunity cost. Development also needs permissions, infrastructure, construction and time.

Compare using the land in the proposed project with realistic alternatives. Do not value it as a standalone sale and also add the full profit of a project that consumes the same land without reconciling the overlap.

A stronger brand may improve sales velocity or access to landowners. Equity holders benefit only if that advantage survives land pricing, partner terms and execution spending.

Likewise, more projects can increase central complexity, guarantees and cash commitments. Consolidation is useful when share gains earn good incremental returns, not simply when the headline pipeline becomes larger.

The most important register is project by project: economic interest, approvals, stage, booked value, collections, unsold stock, cost to complete, debt, launch/completion dates and restrictions.

11. How should rental assets be compared with development?

Annual rent is a flow. Property value is a stock. Converting one into the other needs assumptions about occupancy, expenses, sustainable net income, capitalisation or discount rates, reinvestment and ownership.

For a simple illustration, net annual operating income of 10 capitalised at 8% implies 125 of asset value. At 10%, it implies 100. This is pre-debt asset arithmetic, not a current property quote, promised yield or listed-equity valuation.

Do not mix gross rent with net operating income. Nor should one year's rental income be added directly to a multiyear residential profit estimate and described as NAV.

A landlord, a developer, a property-owning subsidiary and a listed REIT are different claims. A possible asset sale to an associated vehicle is not guaranteed demand, price or cash until the actual transaction is established.

Keep maintenance, occupancy, tenant concentration, fit-out obligations and future capital spending beside the attractive rental headline.

12. What can break a special-situation thesis?

A proposed merger or promoter transition can change the asset base, funding and governance. It can also fail, take longer or dilute existing holders more than expected.

Compare value per fully diluted share, not just the enlarged land bank. If an illustrative asset value doubles but the share count also doubles, per-share value is unchanged before considering debt and other effects.

Approval, legal completion, integration and monetisation are separate facts. This article deliberately does not recycle historical proposed transactions as completed catalysts.

Use original filings for exchange ratios, conditions, claims, guarantees, partner rights and related-party arrangements. A respected sponsor or famous investor's involvement is context, not protection from loss.

Delay, higher cost to complete, weaker collections, adverse partner terms and new funding needs can undermine an apparently discounted asset value.

13. What should the valuation actually discount?

Attributable future cash after remaining obligations, with realistic timing and risk. A project NAV model should not capitalise gross development value as though it were present profit.

Value development and operating rental assets consistently, include central costs and then bridge to equity using the relevant net debt, minorities and other senior claims. Reconcile cash and debt by entity so restricted cash is not automatically netted against every group obligation.

EV/pre-sales is a shortcut, not a replacement for project economics. It hides margins, landowner shares, reinvestment and time. Applying an enterprise multiple gives enterprise value, not market capitalisation without the debt-and-claims bridge.

Test weaker realisations, slower absorption, cancellations, cost inflation, delayed handover and higher finance costs. A longer schedule can reduce present value and enlarge the funding gap even if lifetime nominal profit is unchanged.

My final question would be whether the developer can deliver, collect and reinvest at attractive returns without repeatedly diluting its owners. A large land bank is a starting point for that investigation, not its conclusion.
