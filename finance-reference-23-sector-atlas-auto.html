Chapter 23. Sector Atlas: Auto, Capital Goods and Manufacturing

Chapter 23 of 85 · about 21 minutes

Manufacturing is where India's economic story stops being a slogan and becomes machine tools, assembly lines and order books. It is also the most cyclical group of sectors in this atlas: the same company can look uninvestable and irresistible three years apart, and both impressions will be reasonable. The skill this chapter teaches is reading the cycle's position in the company's own numbers, volumes, discounts, order books, utilisation, rather than in the headlines about it.

23.1What drives the auto cycle: volumes, discounts and commodity costs?

An automaker's P&L is three moving parts. Volumes: vehicle sales run in multi-year cycles driven by incomes, credit availability, fuel prices and replacement demand; the segment mix matters enormously, because a small car, a motorcycle and a truck are three different economies wearing one sector name. Discounts: in weak demand, list prices hold but discounts widen, so the real price signal is the discount trend in dealer checks, not the announced price. Commodity costs: steel, aluminium, rubber and precious metals for catalysts swing gross margin by points, and the pass-through lags by quarters. The tell-tale combination: rising volumes plus falling discounts plus falling commodity costs is the sweet spot of the cycle, and its exact mirror is the trap. Monthly sales data, published by every automaker, makes this the most current-read sector in the market.

23.2How are EVs shaking out the auto supply chain?

Electrification is a slow-moving earthquake under the auto supply chain. An electric drivetrain has a fraction of the moving parts of an internal-combustion one, so entire categories of component suppliers, engine, transmission, exhaust systems, face structural decline, while battery, motor and power-electronics suppliers face structural growth. For vehicle makers the transition is a capital-allocation tightrope: fund the EV future from the ICE present without stranding either. For investors the lesson is timing discipline: the losers decline over a decade, not a quarter, and trade cheap long before they are dead; the winners spend years investing before the profits arrive. The shake-out is real, but it will be televised in slow motion, which means the money is made by reading the pace correctly, not by being early and righteous.

23.3How do you read capital-goods companies: order books, execution and operating leverage?

Capital-goods companies, the makers of power equipment, industrial machinery, engineering projects, sell the means of production, so their demand is everyone else's capex decision: late-cycle, lumpy, and gloriously profitable at the top. The sector's own accounting: the order book is future revenue already signed, and book-to-bill above 1 is the growth signal; execution converts orders to revenue over one to three years, with fixed-price contracts carrying the risk that input costs move between signing and delivery; and operating leverage means that once revenue covers the fixed cost base, incremental orders fall to margin almost whole, which is why earnings triple on a 40% revenue rise at cycle turns. The risks rhyme with Chapter 19's credit cycles: the orders placed at the peak are the ones executed into the trough, at prices negotiated when everyone was optimistic.

23.4How do defence indigenisation and PLI drive manufacturing demand?

Two policy engines now shape Indian manufacturing demand. Defence indigenisation: import bans on listed weapons categories plus emergency procurement powers have created multi-year, government-backed order visibility for domestic defence manufacturers, a genuine structural change from the import-dependent past, though one where order announcements habitually outrun order conversion. Production-linked incentives: the PLI schemes pay manufacturers a percentage of incremental sales across electronics, pharma ingredients, auto components and more, effectively subsidising the cost curve's steepest part. Both are real demand, and both carry the same investor warning: policy-driven earnings deserve a policy-risk discount, because what a scheme gives, a revised scheme can trim.

23.5How do you read capacity utilisation and capex announcements?

Capacity utilisation is the manufacturing cycle's heartbeat. Below about 70%, nobody builds; above 80%, everyone announces plants; the announcements cluster exactly when returns on existing capacity peak, and the new supply arrives together, two years later, into the downturn. This is the capital cycle, and it explains why manufacturing fortunes reverse so reliably. The investor's use: when an industry's utilisation is low and no one is building, future pricing power is being born; when capex announcements crowd the business pages, future margins are being competed away. Read capex announcements as supply forecasts, not confidence signals. The companies that build counter-cyclically, in the trough, on balance sheets that survived it, own the next upcycle; the rest own the next glut.

23.6Worked examples and cases

Case 1: an auto major through one full cycle

Composite of the passenger-vehicle leaders, numbers illustrative. Year one, trough: volumes fall 8%, discounts widen to 4% of realisations, steel is high, operating margin halves to 5%, the stock derates to 12x trough earnings. Year two, turn: volumes recover 12% off the low base, discounts narrow, commodity costs ease, and operating leverage triples the margin to 9%; the stock doubles before the earnings arrive, because the monthly volume data told the story first. Year four, complacency: volumes peak, discounts creep, and the cycle's next down-leg begins. The investor's map through it: buy when discounts peak and volumes stop falling, sell when the story turns to capacity additions and everyone calls the cycle structural. Autos are where Chapter 11's cyclical-versus-secular warning earns its keep every few years.

Case 2: a capital-goods order book, translated into earnings

Consider an illustrative capital-goods company with a ₹30,000 crore order book against ₹10,000 crore of last year's revenue. Three years of revenue are already signed, which is why the market pays for the company before the earnings exist. But run the honesty checks: 12% EBITDA margins assume fixed-price contracts behave through a commodity cycle; a third of the book may be slow-moving government projects with stretched receivables; and the ₹13,000 crore of new orders were won at peak-cycle prices against peak-cycle competition. The translation discipline: haircut the book for execution risk, price the margins at mid-cycle, and check the receivables as hard as the orders. An order book is a promise; the P&L is where promises are audited.
