Chapter 8. The Three Financial Statements

Chapter 8 of 85 · about 20 minutes

Every company you will ever analyse, from the kirana distributor to Reliance, tells its story in the same three documents. Analysts are not smarter than you; they have simply read these three statements so often that the story reads itself. Give this chapter one honest hour and the annual report stops being a 300-page wall and becomes three short tables that mostly agree with each other, and the places where they do not agree are exactly where the story is.

8.1What is the balance sheet, and what does it show?

The balance sheet is a photograph, taken on one day (in India, 31 March), of everything the company controls and everyone who has a claim on it. It obeys one identity that never breaks: assets = liabilities + equity. Assets are what the company has: factories, inventory, receivables from customers, cash. Liabilities are what it owes: loans, supplier dues, taxes pending. Equity is the residue belonging to shareholders, the value left if everything was sold and every lender paid.

From first principles, the identity is not an accounting rule but a funding statement: every rupee of stuff the company holds was paid for by someone, either lenders (liabilities) or owners (equity). Reading top to bottom then answers three questions in order: what does it own, how was that paid for, and how much of the funding is the owners' own skin versus borrowed money. A company whose assets are mostly machinery differs from one whose assets are mostly customer IOUs, and the difference is risk.

8.2What does the profit and loss statement show?

The P&L is a film, not a photograph: everything that happened over the year, from the first rupee of sales down to the last rupee of profit. Read it as a staircase. Revenue at the top. Minus cost of goods gives gross profit, what the product itself earns before overheads. Minus salaries, rent, marketing gives operating profit (EBITDA and then EBIT), what the business earns. Minus interest gives profit before tax; minus tax gives net profit, the famous bottom line.

Each step has its own question. Gross margin asks whether the product has pricing power. Operating margin asks whether management runs a tight ship. The interest line asks how heavy the debt is. The bottom line, divided by shares, gives the EPS that every headline quotes. A serious reader spends less time on the bottom line than on the stairs above it, because each stair is where a different kind of trouble first shows.

8.3What is the cash flow statement, and why is it the hardest to fake?

Profit is an opinion shaped by accrual rules; cash is a fact. The cash flow statement reconciles the two across three lanes:

  • Operating (CFO): cash the actual business generated. This is the lane to watch. Profit rising for years while operating cash flow stagnates is the classic warning that sales are being booked faster than they are being collected.
  • Investing (CFI): cash spent on factories and investments, or recovered from selling them. Healthy companies usually show a steady outflow here; they are building.
  • Financing (CFF): cash raised from or returned to lenders and shareholders: new loans, repayments, dividends, buybacks.

A simple three-lane health read: CFO positive and growing, CFI negative (building), CFF negative or flat (paying down, paying dividends) is the shape of a self-funding business. CFO negative with CFF positive, year after year, is a business surviving on fresh borrowing, a shape that ends one way.

8.4How do the three financial statements connect?

Profit from the P&L flows into equity on the balance sheet, the cash flow statement’s closing cash becomes the balance sheet’s cash line, and every other balance-sheet change is explained by the cash flow statement’s three lanes: operating, investing and financing.

P&L: profit for the year
revenue minus every cost
Cash flow: where the cash moved
operations, investing, financing
Balance sheet: what is left
assets and claims at year end

The three statements as one machine: the P&L measures performance, the cash flow tracks the money, the balance sheet records the position. Each checks the others.

The links are mechanical. The year's profit flows into the balance sheet's equity. The cash flow statement starts from profit, strips out non-cash items like depreciation, adjusts for working capital moves, and lands on the cash line of the balance sheet. Capex leaves through CFI and arrives on the balance sheet as fixed assets, then depreciates back through the P&L over years. Once you see the plumbing, every manipulation becomes visible as a pipe that does not connect: profit without cash, assets without funding, growth without capex.

8.5Where in the annual report does the truth hide?

The statements are the skeleton; the rest of the annual report is where the honest detail lives. The notes to accounts break every big number into its parts and carry the accounting policies that decide what counts as revenue or cost. The auditor's report is supposed to be boring; any qualification, emphasis of matter or auditor resignation is a siren. The related-party transactions note lists deals with the promoter's other entities, the classic route by which value quietly leaves a listed company. And the management discussion is where the story is told in words: read it after the numbers, so you can tell where the words and the numbers disagree.

8.6Worked examples and cases

Case 1: reading a large-cap's year in five passes

Take any Indian blue chip's annual report. Pass one, balance sheet: equity ₹80,000 crore, borrowings near zero, cash large; conclusion, an owner-funded fortress. Pass two, P&L: revenue up 8%, operating margin steady near 24%; conclusion, no price war, no cost blowout. Pass three, cash flow: CFO ₹22,000 crore against net profit ₹19,000 crore; profit is turning into cash with something to spare. Pass four, notes: no strange related-party loans, auditor unqualified. Pass five, dividends and buybacks in CFF: cash genuinely returning to shareholders. Five passes, fifteen minutes, and you know this company better than most people holding it.

Case 2: the profits-without-cash company

A mid-cap reports profit growth of 25% a year for four straight years. The P&L staircase looks glorious. But open the cash flow statement: CFO has been near zero the whole time, and the balance sheet shows receivables ballooning from 40 days of sales to 140 days. The company was booking sales to customers who were not paying, on channels stuffed and terms quietly loosened. When the receivables finally soured, two years of "profit" reversed in one quarter and the stock fell 70%. The P&L told the story the promoters wanted; the cash flow statement told the truth, four years early, to anyone who read it.
