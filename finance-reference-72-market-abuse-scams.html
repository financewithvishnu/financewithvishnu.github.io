Chapter 72. Market Abuse, Frauds and Scams: Rules and Lessons

Chapter 72 of 85 · about 20 minutes

The scams change costume every decade; the skeleton underneath is centuries old. Someone manufactures a price, a promise or a pedigree, and sells it to someone who wanted to believe. This chapter is the anatomy lesson: the four great fraud templates of Indian and world markets, how each is built, how each is caught, and the pattern-recognition that costs less than the tuition most investors pay.

72.1What is insider trading: the law and its grey zones?

The rule's heart: trading while in possession of unpublished price-sensitive information, results, deals, regulatory decisions, in breach of a duty or connection, is forbidden, for the insider and for the tippee who knows the chain. The law's machinery: structured digital databases of who knew what when, trading windows that close around results, pre-clearance for designated persons, and SEBI's power to match trades against timelines, as Chapter 71's order anatomy showed. The grey zones are where careers end. The malign neglect of the group chat: information shared among analysts, promoters and fund managers that everyone treats as colour and the law treats as UPSI. Shadow trading: using inside knowledge of one company to trade its competitor or supplier, prosecuted abroad, watched here. And the "everyone does it" defence, which has the twin defects of being false and being irrelevant. The compliance rule for anyone near information is asymmetrical and therefore simple: when you could plausibly know something, you cannot trade, and the plausibility is judged by the regulator with the timestamps, not by you with your intentions.

72.2What are price rigging, pump-and-dump and circular trading?

The second template manufactures the price itself. The classic pump-and-dump: accumulate an illiquid smallcap quietly, then manufacture the appearance of life, circular trading between connected accounts to print volume, wash trades to print rising prices, research reports and tips to recruit the public, and sell into the recruited buying, leaving the chart's right edge as the bagholders' inheritance. The exchange data convicts it every time: volume concentrated in a handful of connected entities, price marching in suspiciously orderly steps, the collapse beginning the day the operators' selling starts. The defences are pattern-based: an illiquid stock rising on no news is a question, not an opportunity; "operators are active" is a confession, not a tip; and the moment you are sold urgency on a stock you cannot sell back in size, you are the exit liquidity. SEBI's enforcement here is mechanical and constant, hundreds of entities barred in the recurring sweeps, because the template is profitable until prosecuted, and the prosecution is slow, so the deterrence is yours.

72.3How do Ponzi and pyramid schemes work?

The oldest template needs no market at all. A Ponzi pays old investors with new investors' money, and the tell is structural, not numerical: returns that are high, stable and unexplained, because real returns fluctuate and only fictitious ones arrive like clockwork. The pyramid adds recruitment as the product: your payout depends on enrolling the next layer, which is why every pyramid is a Ponzi with a marketing department. India's history with both is grim and continuous: Saradha's crores from the poorest savers in the east, chit-fund fronts, "collective investment schemes" in emu farms, teak plots and goat rearing, each generation of operators discovering that agricultural imagery and monthly payout schedules still work. The mathematics is the verdict: any scheme paying more than the risk-free rate while promising stability is claiming to beat the market without the market's variance, and the claim refutes itself. The due diligence question that kills every Ponzi in one line: "show me the audited source of the yield", the assets, the strategy, the cash flows. There is always a reason that cannot happen yet.

72.4How does finfluencer and Telegram-era manipulation work?

The templates got a distribution upgrade. The modern pump-and-dump does not need boiler rooms: it needs a Telegram channel with 50,000 subscribers, a YouTube video with a price target, or a finfluencer's "conviction buy", and the enforcement record now includes exactly this, operators who accumulated first, broadcast second, dumped third, with SEBI orders mapping the trades to the posts. The new anatomy has specific tells. The coordinated hour: dozens of channels pushing the same illiquid name on the same morning. The rented credibility: a genuine expert's image or an impersonated brand lending the scheme a face. The screenshot P&L: proof-of-profit images that cost nothing to fabricate and recruit better than any prospectus. And the paid-disclosure fig leaf, a disclaimer in size-6 font that the "analyst" holds the stock, which under the rules is the confession, not the excuse. The audience defence from Chapter 61 applies with teeth here: anyone whose income scales with your action, subscriber count, referral fees, or their own holdings, is selling, and the price target is the product.

72.5What are chit fund, unregistered deposit and collective-investment traps?

India's special contribution to the taxonomy is the grey zone between community finance and fraud. The legitimate chit fund is a registered, regulated rotating savings scheme, an ancient and useful institution. The trap wears its name: unregistered "chits" and deposit schemes offering bank-beating monthly returns, banned under the Banning of Unregulated Deposit Schemes Act, surviving because the operators are neighbours and the first circles really do pay out, the Ponzi's early months disguised as community trust. The collective investment scheme variant adds an asset costume: invest in the orchard, the emu farm, the holiday plot, and the scheme manages it for you, for a yield that exists only in the brochure. The regulatory line is bright: taking deposits from the public is a licensed activity, and any entity doing it without RBI, SEBI or registrar sanction is illegal on arrival, whatever the brochure's cover. The family's defence is one question, asked at the function, before the cheque: "which regulator are you registered with, show me the registration", followed by the silence that tells you everything.

72.6Worked examples and cases

Case 1: the Harshad Mehta securities scam, the original template

1992: a Bombay broker discovers that the market for bank receipts, the paper trail of interbank government-securities deals, is a honour system, and that forged receipts can divert the banking system's money into his own account, where it becomes the buying power behind a one-man bull market. The Sensex triples in months on his bid; a journalist, Sucheta Dalal, pulls the thread; and the unravelling takes the market down by more than half, banks holding hundreds of crores of worthless paper, and a small-investor generation scarred for a decade. Every modern control is a scar of this wound: the demutualised, screen-based NSE, dematerialised settlement, the clearing corporation's guarantee, the surveillance state of Chapter 51. The template's core is eternal: find the seam between two systems, the money market and the stock market in his case, where each assumes the other is checking, and walk through it with both hands. The investor's lesson is not that markets are rigged, it is that plumbing matters, and that the integrity of the settlement system you never see is worth more than any stock tip you ever hear.

Case 2: a modern pump-and-dump, reconstructed from exchange data

A composite of real SEBI cases, numbers illustrative. Week one: connected entities, family accounts, shell firms, one introducer with fifty clients, begin accumulating an illiquid smallcap at ₹100, taking their combined holding past 15% of the free float. Weeks two to eight: the accounts trade with each other in rising ladders, 102, 105, 118, printing a chart that looks like discovery, while tipsheets and channels begin the recruitment, "promoter buying", "order book swelling", the first YouTube target of ₹250. Weeks nine to ten: the public arrives, volume explodes to fifty times normal, price touches ₹340, and the connected accounts' net position quietly crosses from long to flat to sold. Week eleven: ₹80. The reconstruction from exchange data is mechanical: client concentration reports, the circularity ratio, the perfect correlation between the operators' selling and the chart's top. The investor's extraction: the same data that convicts the operator is visible to you before the fact, illiquidity, no-news verticality, urgency, and the cheapest fraud insurance in the market is the refusal to buy anything you cannot explain in one sentence to someone who is not selling it.
