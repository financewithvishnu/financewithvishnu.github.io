Chapter 20. Sector Atlas: IT and Technology Services

Chapter 20 of 85 · about 21 minutes

India's IT services industry is one of the great business-model achievements of the last forty years: it sells Indian engineering hours to the world's largest companies, collects in dollars, pays in rupees, and converts the difference into margins most manufacturers can only dream about. It is also, for investors, a sector where the quarterly numbers are unusually honest and unusually legible, if you know which six lines to read. This chapter teaches you the machine and the six lines.

20.1How does the IT outsourcing model make money?

From first principles, an IT services company is a people-arbitrage machine with three dials. Bill rate: the price per hour or per person-month charged to clients, set by skill mix and competition. Utilisation: the share of available engineer-hours actually billed; each point of utilisation is nearly pure margin, because the salary is already being paid. Attrition: the share of staff leaving each year; high attrition forces expensive hiring, training and subcontracting, and it is the sector's most quoted operational number for good reason. Revenue = billable engineers x bill rate x utilisation; profit = revenue minus salaries minus everything else. When you can decompose a quarter into those dials, the press release stops being news and starts being arithmetic.

20.2What do deal wins and TCV tell you about revenue visibility?

The sector's forward-looking number is TCV, total contract value: the full multi-year value of deals signed this quarter. The ratio that matters is book-to-bill, TCV signed divided by revenue billed; above 1, the order book grows faster than it drains. Two cautions keep you honest. TCV is a ceiling, not a schedule: a five-year $100 million deal may bill slowly, ramp late, or be restructured. And in weak demand cycles, clients sign renewals at lower rates and smaller scopes, so headline TCV can hold while the revenue inside it shrinks. Read TCV as direction, not destiny, and always against the constant-currency growth it produces two quarters later.

20.3Which outside forces move IT earnings: the rupee, visas and US demand?

Three forces outside any management's control move this sector's earnings. The rupee: most revenue is in dollars, most cost in rupees, so a depreciating rupee is a structural tailwind and an appreciating one a headwind; companies hedge a year or so out, which smooths but never removes the effect. Visa and labour rules in the US and Europe set the cost of on-site delivery, the part of the model politics can directly tax; every tightening cycle pushes work offshore, which the industry has so far absorbed as a mix shift rather than a body blow. And US enterprise demand, the technology budgets of American banks, retailers and manufacturers, is the demand curve itself: when US clients fear recession, discretionary projects pause first, which is why Indian IT stocks trade partly as a leveraged proxy for the US corporate cycle.

20.4Where do margins live: products, services or platforms?

Services revenue is linear: more revenue needs more people, and margin per person is bounded by the bill-rate-versus-wage spread. Product and platform revenue breaks that linearity: build once, sell many times, and the marginal unit costs almost nothing, which is why software-product businesses globally command margins and multiples that services never reach. Every large Indian IT firm tells a platform story; the honest test is in the numbers: what share of revenue is genuinely non-linear, growing without matching headcount, and at what margin? In most cases the answer remains small, and the multiple the market pays should follow the revenue that exists, not the story told about it. The rare Indian companies that have built real product economics are priced like it, and usually deserve the debate.

20.5Why do IT stocks’ P/E bands shift with growth?

The sector's history is a lesson in Chapter 16's discipline. When the large caps grew 15-20% in dollars, the market paid 25-30x earnings; when dollar growth slowed to low single digits, the same companies derated toward the mid-teens, with no fraud, no failure, only arithmetic: a business's fair multiple follows its durable growth rate and the return on the capital that growth needs. IT needs almost no capital, generates free cash flow close to reported profit, and returns most of it, which is why the sector can carry premium multiples at all. The valuation question is never "is 24x expensive?" but "what growth does 24x require, and do the drivers, deals, utilisation, US budgets, deliver it?". The answer changes by cycle, which is why the P/E band moves, and why buying the derating when the cycle turns has been the sector's recurring gift.

The analyst's read: demand is recovering (constant-currency growth positive, book-to-bill above 1), the cost machine is healing (attrition down, utilisation up), and margins reflect both, helped by the rupee. The remaining question is pricing: if bill rates are flat, the margin recovery has a ceiling, and the stock's multiple is then a bet on volume growth continuing. That is a complete, defensible view formed from seven numbers in one table, no access to management required. This is what "reading a sector" means in practice.
