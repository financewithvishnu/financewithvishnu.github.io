Chapter 3. Risk and Return: The Grammar of Investing

Chapter 3 of 85 · about 20 minutes

Two neighbours each invest ₹5,00,000 in January. One buys a fixed deposit, one buys a single hot stock tip. By December one sleeps fine and one does not, and it is not the one you think, because in different years the story flips. This chapter gives you the grammar to say exactly what was different about their two bets: not "safe" versus "risky", but which risks, how much, and paid for how. Every portfolio decision you ever make is a sentence in this grammar.

3.1What is total return on an investment?

Rebuild return from the primitives: it is the money an asset pays you for the time and risk you supplied. It arrives in two envelopes:

  • Income: cash the asset pays while you hold it: dividends, interest, rent.
  • Capital gain (or loss): the change in the asset's own price.

Total return is the two added up, and it is the only honest scoreboard. A flat that rises 4% while paying 2% rent, a bond paying 7% that ends flat, and a share paying 1% dividend while rising 9% all earned roughly the same 6 to 10%, through different envelopes. Investors who watch only price or only yield read half the scoreboard.

3.2What are the three types of investment risk?

Risk is not one thing, and confusing its three forms is expensive:

  • Volatility: the price swings around on the way. Uncomfortable, survivable, and the source of the equity premium.
  • Permanent loss: the money never comes back. Satyam's shares fell 78% in a single day in January 2009 when its accounts were confessed false; that was not volatility.
  • Uncertainty: you cannot even list the outcomes. A new regulator, a new technology, a war. Uninsurable by diversification alone, managed by position size.

The practical test for any holding: is today's pain a swing that time heals, or a hole that time deepens? Everything in the accounting part of this website exists to help you tell the two apart before the market does.

3.3What is the risk-return trade-off?

Markets price risk like a ration: the more uncertainty you agree to carry, the more return you can demand. There is no high-return, low-risk seat at this table; when a product advertises one, the risk is hidden, not absent, and Chapter 1's five questions will find it.

Your own seat has three measurements, and they are different things:

  • Ability: horizon, income stability, dependents. A 28-year-old with a salary can survive a 50% drawdown; a retiree drawing monthly income cannot.
  • Willingness: what your stomach tolerates. The honest test is the last crash, not the last rally.
  • Need: the return your goals actually require. Needing 15% a year is itself a risk exposure.

The right portfolio sits where the lowest of the three puts you. Most investing disasters are people sitting where only their greed put them.

3.4Why is diversification called the only free lunch in investing?

Here is the rare genuine gift in finance. Combining assets that do not fall together cuts the swings of the whole without cutting the average return. The first few additions do the heavy lifting:

Two cautions the chart hides. The assets must genuinely differ: ten bank stocks are one bet wearing ten jerseys. And in a true panic, correlations jump toward one, so the lunch is free on ordinary days and merely cheaper on terrible ones. Real diversification mixes asset classes: equity, debt, gold, cash, the subject of the entire portfolio part later.

3.5What is liquidity, and why must it match your time horizon?

Liquidity is how fast you can turn an asset back into money at a fair price. A Nifty stock sells in seconds; a flat takes months and a discount. Neither is bad in itself. The rule is matching: pair each goal with an asset whose liquidity and horizon fit it.

  • Emergency money: same-day access, so liquid funds and savings accounts, never equity or property.
  • A goal three years away: debt instruments maturing near the goal, not shares that might be mid-crash when the fee is due.
  • Retirement twenty years out: equity's volatility is affordable because time heals swings; illiquidity here even earns a premium.

Most forced selling, the kind that turns a swing into a permanent loss, is a matching failure discovered too late.
