Chapter 26. Sector Atlas: Infrastructure, Real Estate and New-Age Businesses

Chapter 26 of 85 · about 21 minutes

This chapter covers the sectors where Indian investors have lost the most money per unit of headline growth: construction, property, telecom, and the new consumer internet. The common thread is not that these are bad businesses; it is that in all four, the exciting number, the order book, the bookings, the subscriber count, the GMV, is a promise, and the cash arrives on a schedule that has ruined many a confident reader of promises. Learn the bridges between promise and cash here, and you will read four sectors most investors never truly understand.

26.1How do you judge EPC companies and developers: order books versus balance sheets?

An EPC company, engineering, procurement, construction, is Chapter 23's order-book logic with the risk dialled up, because the projects are larger, the counterparties are often governments, and the payment terms are longer. The order book tells you the workload; the balance sheet tells you whether the company survives doing the work. The failure mode is always the same shape: win orders at thin margins, fund the working capital with debt while the client pays slowly, and discover in a downturn that the order book was a portfolio of IOUs. The reading order that protects you: receivable days and retention money before order-book growth; debt against the order book's cash conversion, not its headline size; and the promoter track record of finishing what he starts. In EPC, growth is the easy part and collection is the business.

26.2How do real estate companies make money in the RERA era?

A property developer sells apartments that do not yet exist, collects in instalments as construction advances, and books the revenue by various accounting conventions that all lag the economic truth. The metrics that matter: pre-sales (bookings), the demand signal; collections, the cash actually arriving; inventory, built and unsold, which ties up capital and ages badly; and net debt against all three. The RERA era changed the game's fairness: projects must be registered, customer money largely escrowed for construction, and delivery timelines enforceable, which transferred power from developers to buyers and rewarded the organised, well-capitalised listed players with consolidation. 26.3How do telecom economics work: ARPU, spectrum and survival?

Indian telecom is the market's cleanest case study in industry structure, Chapter 11's five forces with the volume turned up. A dozen operators fought a price war, sharpened to free by a new entrant with the deepest pockets in the country; the sector consolidated to a private duopoly-plus-one; and pricing power, in the form of tariff hikes, began returning only after the weak had exited. The vocabulary: ARPU, average revenue per user, the single number that summarises pricing power; subscriber share and its quality (paying, active users, not registered SIMs); spectrum, the licensed airwaves bought at government auction, an enormous recurring capex that technology upgrades keep fresh; and the resulting leverage, because the fixed costs are huge and the marginal user nearly free. The investment question reduces to: how many rational players remain, and have they started acting like it?

26.4How do you judge new-age platform companies on the path to profitability?

The listed consumer-internet companies, food delivery, quick commerce, marketplaces, travel platforms, share one reporting habit: a headline loss beside a polished "adjusted EBITDA" that adds back everything the management hopes is temporary. Reading them honestly is a skill with three moves. First, decompose the adjustments: stock-based compensation is a real economic cost whatever the adjustment says; marketing that buys habitual customers is investment, marketing that buys one-time orders is rent. Second, find the unit economics per order or transaction and trend them: contribution margin after variable costs must improve with scale, or scale is just a bigger loss. Third, map the path: what has to be true, order density, take rates, ad revenue, for the mature P&L to earn, and is the market paying today for that map already drawn? These companies can be wonderful businesses; the skill is refusing to let adjusted numbers grade their own homework.

26.5What are REITs and InvITs?

One antidote to this sector's promise-versus-cash problem deserves mention here and gets its own treatment in the alternatives chapters: REITs and InvITs own finished, income-producing property and infrastructure, offices, roads, transmission lines, and must distribute most of their cash. They trade the developer's growth drama for the landlord's yield, which is exactly the trade some investors should be making. The reference point for everything in this chapter: when a developer's promise and a REIT's coupon compete for the same rupee, the burden of proof sits on the promise.

Case 1: a developer's pre-sales to cash-flow bridge

Work the table above, numbers illustrative of a healthy mid-sized developer. ₹2,000 crore of bookings sound like a growth story; the bridge reveals ₹100 crore of true operating surplus, and an ambitious land purchase turns the year cash-negative, funded by debt. None of this is alarming if the pattern is disciplined: collections at 70% of bookings, debt modest against the unsold inventory, land bought within the surplus over a cycle. It is alarming when bookings double while collections lag, debt climbs, and the land bank grows in booms, because that is the precise shape of every Indian developer that broke in 2008-2014. The bridge is public information assembled into one table; build it, and the press releases lose their power over you.

Case 2: a new-age IPO's adjusted EBITDA, unpacked

Composite of recent listings, numbers illustrative: reported net loss ₹900 crore; "adjusted EBITDA" a positive ₹450 crore. The unpack: add-backs include ₹700 crore of ESOP costs (real: employees must be paid, in cash or in your dilution), ₹400 crore of marketing classed as "brand investment" (half habit-forming, half rent), and ₹250 crore of one-offs that recur every year (restructurings of a company that is always restructuring). The honest contribution trend is genuinely improving, orders up, costs per order down, which is the part worth paying for. The verdict the arithmetic supports: a real business, improving, priced at a multiple of an adjusted profit that overstates the true one by a wide margin. Both halves of that sentence matter; most commentary holds only one.
