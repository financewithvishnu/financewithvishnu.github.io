Chapter 59. Digital Money and Payments

Chapter 59 of 85 · about 18 minutes

In one decade, India went from a cash economy with a card minority to the world's largest real-time payments system, and the change is so complete that a vegetable vendor refusing digital payment is now the anomaly. Part X opens with the rails: how money actually moves on a phone tap, why India's version is studied everywhere, what a digital rupee adds, and what it still costs to move money across a border.

59.1The payments stack: cards, net banking, wallets

Every digital payment is a message that moves an obligation, and the generations stack. Cards are the oldest digital rail: a four-party system of your bank, the merchant's bank, the network between them, and the MDR, the merchant discount rate, the 1-3% baked into prices that pays for the whole apparatus, rewards points and all. Net banking moved the instruction online but kept the bank's own pipes and hours. Wallets interposed a stored balance, faster at the till but a loop that needed loading and never quite escaped being a card with extra steps. Each generation carried the same architecture: private rails, per-transaction economics, and settlement that meant "soon" rather than "now". Understanding that baseline is what makes the next section's leap legible: India did not improve the card system; it routed around it.

59.2UPI: how India's real-time system works and why the world studies it

The Unified Payments Interface, launched by the NPCI in 2016, made three decisions that look obvious only in hindsight. It was built as public infrastructure, an open switch any bank or app could join, rather than a private network with a toll booth. It made the address the innovation: a virtual payment address, your name or number with a handle, replacing account numbers and IFSC codes, so paying became as easy as texting. And it settled between banks in real time, free to users, with the MDR set to zero, the state choosing ubiquity over per-transaction profit. The result is a system carrying more transactions each month than the card networks process in some countries, from ₹10 chai to rent, studied by central banks from Singapore to Brasília. The lesson the world comes to study is not technical but institutional: payments turned out to be like roads, cheapest for everyone when built as commons, with private competition on the apps and services around the rail rather than ownership of the rail itself.

59.3Central bank digital currencies: the digital rupee and global CBDCs

If UPI moved bank money faster, the natural next question is whether money itself changes form. A central bank digital currency is a direct claim on the central bank in digital form: your UPI balance is your bank's IOU, while a digital rupee, the e₹ the RBI has piloted in retail and wholesale forms, is the RBI's own liability, cash that lives on a phone. The claimed gains: settlement without intermediaries, resilience, programmable transfers for targeted benefits, and cross-border links that skip correspondent chains. The honest problems: bank deposits could drain toward the risk-free digital form in a panic, privacy sits awkwardly with a state-issued ledger, and India's payment problem was already largely solved, which is why adoption has been a crawl rather than a UPI-style explosion. Globally, every major central bank is somewhere between studying and piloting, with China's e-CNY the largest live experiment. Watch CBDCs as infrastructure in search of its killer use; the form will matter first where trust in banks or borders is the binding constraint.

59.4Cross-border payments and remittances, old and new

One border remains expensive to cross. The old rail is the correspondent banking chain: your bank talks to a bank that talks to a bank, each taking days and fees, the SWIFT messages passing like registered post through sorting offices, with the total cost of a remittance landing anywhere from 3% to 7% for small amounts. The challengers attack from two directions. Fintech remittance firms run matched, pre-funded pools in each country, so no money actually crosses, you pay in rupees, your cousin is paid from the dollar pool, collapsing cost and time toward a percent or two and same-day. And the crypto-adjacent experiments, stablecoins and blockchain rails, promise settlement without the pools, carrying their own regulatory and volatility questions. For India, the world's largest remittance recipient, with inflows above $100 billion a year, every point off the average cost is billions of dollars kept by the families the money was always for, which is why cheap remittances may be the highest-leverage fintech problem on earth.

59.5Worked examples and cases

Case 1: the economics of a free UPI transaction

Free is a billing arrangement, not a cost of zero. A ₹500 UPI payment triggers real work: the apps' servers, the NPCI switch, the two banks' ledgers, fraud monitoring, dispute handling, all of it costing paise per transaction at scale, borne today by the banks, the NPCI's members, and ultimately the system as a whole. Why would anyone provide it free? Because the returns arrive elsewhere: payments data anchors lending and cross-selling for the apps, deposits and engagement for the banks, and for the state, a formalised, legible, tax-visible economy whose dividends dwarf the subsidy, a debate that resurfaces every year when the question of restoring some MDR returns. The user's takeaway is practical: the rail's freeness is a policy choice, not a law of nature, and the durable things to value are the design decisions underneath, open access, instant interbank settlement, addressing by handle, which would survive any future repricing of the toll.

Case 2: a remittance cost comparison

₹1,00,000 equivalent sent from the Gulf to Kerala, three ways, numbers illustrative. The traditional bank wire: a fixed fee of ₹1,500-2,500 equivalent, an exchange-rate margin of 1.5-2.5% buried in the quoted rate, and 2-4 days, total cost often ₹3,500-5,000, paid mostly in the invisible margin rather than the visible fee. The fintech route: a small transparent fee, a rate margin near zero to a fraction of a percent, same day or minutes, total ₹800-1,500. The gap, two to four thousand rupees on a lakh, is the monthly-price of not comparing, repeated twelve times a year for a decade of a migrant's working life. Two disciplines follow. Compare the amount received, not the fee quoted, because the rate margin is where the cost hides. And distrust "zero fee" until the rate is checked against the day's mid-market: in cross-border money, the exchange rate is the price tag, and the fee is often just the label.
