Internet platforms: when a growing network becomes a good business

By Finance With Vishnu · Source period: approximately August 2026 · Sector study

An internet platform brings different groups together and helps them find each other, transact or solve a problem. Buyers meet sellers. Recruiters meet jobseekers. Restaurants meet diners and delivery partners. The platform earns money from the value of that connection.

That sounds simple. The investing question is harder: does each new participant make the business more useful, or does each new participant require another discount? Both can produce impressive growth for a while. Only one gives us a reason to expect stronger economics as the network expands.

I therefore prefer to start below the headline numbers. Who uses the product? Who pays? What keeps them coming back? What costs grow with each transaction? And after everyone has been paid, what is left for an ordinary shareholder?

Study window: the internet-platform topic from approximately August 2026. The exact class date is unconfirmed. Company examples below use clearly dated FY2024-25 Indian annual reports and Airbnb's calendar-2025 filing. They are historical business-model illustrations, not a November 2026 results update, valuation call or list of stocks to buy.

1. What makes a platform different from an ordinary online business?

An online shop can buy goods, hold stock and sell them through an app. That is a retail business with a digital storefront. A platform instead helps two or more groups interact. The distinction matters because the revenue, working capital and operating risks can be quite different.

Real businesses can combine both models. A marketplace may also sell its own products, run warehouses, provide payment tools or arrange delivery. Calling the whole company asset-light can hide the physical work behind a digital order.

My first step is to draw the participants and the cash flows. The user is not always the customer. Jobseekers may use a service without paying while recruiters buy access. Sellers may pay for visibility while buyers search for free. A platform can subsidise one side because attracting that side makes the service more valuable to the other.

A listing or subscription service is not automatically a weak business, and a transaction marketplace is not automatically a strong one. These are descriptions of how money is earned. We still need to test customer value and competition.

2. What is a network effect, and what is merely scale?

A network effect exists when participation makes the product more valuable to other participants. In a direct network effect, the benefit is within the same group. A communication service becomes more useful when more of the people I want to reach use it.

In an indirect network effect, one group makes the service more attractive to another. More relevant candidates can attract recruiters. More recruiters with real jobs can attract candidates. The Competition Commission of India's e-commerce study describes this cross-side relationship, while the OECD explains why the different sides need to be examined together. [1] [2]

Scale economies are different. A larger business may spread software costs over more orders. A delivery network may fill vehicles more efficiently. These can be valuable advantages even when another user does not directly improve the product for me.

The distinction is useful because the evidence differs. Lower cost per parcel supports a scale argument. Better match quality, faster successful searches or stronger retention as relevant supply grows supports a network-effect argument. A rising download count proves neither.

There can also be negative effects. Too many irrelevant listings make search harder. Too many advertisements make the product worse. More sellers can mean tougher competition for existing sellers. The job is to improve useful participation, not simply maximise the number of accounts.

3. Why does a large network not guarantee a monopoly?

A strong feedback loop can concentrate a market. It does not establish that one company will win every category, every neighbourhood or every country. The strength of the loop depends on what users need and how easily they can use alternatives.

Multi-homing means using more than one service. A restaurant can list on several apps. A traveller can compare booking sites. A seller may keep a direct website as well as marketplace accounts. When it is cheap to use alternatives, a large network may have less pricing power than its size suggests.

We should also distinguish national scale from local liquidity. A home-service platform can have many professionals across India and still fail to provide an available professional near a particular customer at the required time. The useful network is the relevant local one.

Airbnb's 2025 filing is a practical reminder. It describes competition for both hosts and guests, including online travel agencies, hotels, search engines and other service providers. Its network does not remove the need to compete on availability, value, trust and the cost of using the service. [3]

My question is therefore not whether a company is called a modern monopoly. It is which part of the customer experience becomes difficult to copy, and what would make that difficulty disappear.

4. How does activity turn into revenue?

Gross merchandise value, gross booking value, order value and payment volume describe activity passing through a system. They are not interchangeable with revenue. Even two companies using a similar label may calculate it differently.

A transaction platform may charge a percentage of a booking. A subscription platform may charge suppliers a fixed amount for a period of service. Advertising may monetise attention rather than the transaction itself. Delivery charges may cover a costly fulfilment activity rather than represent high-margin platform income.

Take rate is usually discussed as revenue divided by a measure of transaction value. Before calculating it, check the numerator and denominator. Are taxes included? Are discounts deducted? Does revenue include advertisements, subscriptions or delivery charges? Has the business changed from agent accounting to recording product sales as a principal?

A bigger transaction base is not the same as more earnings

Suppose a marketplace processes ₹1,000 of orders and earns ₹100 of revenue. Its simple take rate is 10%. If order value rises to ₹1,200 but revenue stays at ₹100, activity grew 20% while revenue did not grow. If delivery and support costs also rise, profit can fall.

This is original arithmetic, not a company forecast. It shows why I want the bridge from activity to revenue before reading the profit story.

A higher take rate also needs interpretation. It may reflect better advertising tools or a more valuable service. It may instead reflect a price increase that encourages suppliers to search for alternatives. Monetisation is strongest when customers accept it because the service keeps delivering value.

5. What does a useful per-order calculation look like?

I start with the money the platform actually earns from an order, not the full basket value. Then I subtract the costs that arise from handling that order: delivery or service payments, payment processing, refunds, customer support and platform-funded incentives, as applicable.

The remainder is a contribution toward the wider business. It still has to pay for technology teams, offices, central marketing, management and other overheads. It is not the same as net profit or free cash flow.

At first glance a ₹20 contribution can look generous. But a small change in delivery cost or incentives can consume the ₹5 surplus. Equally, better density and a lower cost of serving repeat users can make a real difference. The direction and durability of those changes matter more than a single quarter's number.

For any reported contribution margin, I read the company's definition. Costs excluded by one company may be included by another. A neat comparison chart is not useful if its underlying measures differ.

6. What can Eternal's FY25 figures teach us about profit layers?

Eternal's FY2024-25 annual report provides a useful historical example. Its food-delivery business reported net order value of ₹32,862 crore, adjusted revenue of ₹9,418 crore, contribution of ₹3,092 crore and adjusted EBITDA of ₹1,505 crore. Those are four different measures of the same business, not four versions of revenue. [4]

Contribution was 9.4% of net order value, while adjusted EBITDA was 4.6%. The gap matters. A platform can generate a positive amount on transactions and still have substantial expenses outside that contribution calculation.

The same report showed quick-commerce contribution of ₹953 crore but adjusted EBITDA of negative ₹292 crore for FY25. Positive contribution therefore did not mean that the segment was profitable on the company's adjusted EBITDA measure.

Definitions are part of the analysis. Eternal's adjusted EBITDA adds back share-based payment expense and deducts relevant lease rentals. Its adjusted revenue also includes specified food-delivery charges beyond reported revenue. I would not compare these figures with another company's statutory operating margin without reconciling the differences.

7. When does operating leverage become real?

Operating leverage means revenue grows faster than costs that do not need to rise at the same rate. A platform can build software and support infrastructure before it has enough paying customers to cover those costs. Once revenue grows, profit may improve quickly.

But the popular version, build the app once and every extra rupee is profit, is too simple. Technology needs maintenance. Fraud controls, customer support, safety, compliance and sales teams cost money. Physical fulfilment adds further capacity requirements.

I look for evidence over several periods: revenue per customer, service quality, repeat use, cost per transaction and central costs relative to revenue. Lower costs caused by temporary cuts can look like efficiency until customer experience deteriorates.

Info Edge's FY25 annual report illustrates why segment analysis matters. Standalone recruitment revenue was ₹19,826 million, with operating profit before tax of ₹11,164 million. The broader standalone business reported revenue of ₹26,536 million and operating PBT of ₹9,733 million, including losses in other verticals and unallocated expenses. A strong mature platform can fund businesses with very different economics. [5]

That is neither automatically good nor automatically bad. The questions are how much is being invested, what milestones those investments must reach and whether management is willing to stop projects that do not work.

8. Why do subscriptions and customer advances deserve a separate analysis?

Not every platform earns a percentage of transaction value. A supplier may pay for a subscription that lasts several months or years. Cash can arrive before the company has recognised the corresponding revenue.

IndiaMART's FY25 annual report provides a historical example. It reported standalone collections of ₹15,256 million, revenue from operations of ₹13,201 million and deferred revenue of ₹16,003 million at the year-end. Collections are a flow of cash from customers. Revenue is the amount recognised for the service delivered during the period. Deferred revenue is a balance linked to service still owed. [6]

This structure can support cash generation. It does not make all advance receipts distributable profit. The business still has to provide the promised service and persuade customers to renew.

The useful checks are paying-customer additions, renewals, churn, realisation per customer and the value customers get from their enquiries or leads. Revenue can keep benefiting from past collections while newer sales weaken. That is why I read collections and customer trends alongside reported growth.

I also separate interest or investment income from the operating business. A large cash balance may be valuable, but the return on that cash is not evidence that the platform's underlying customer proposition is improving.

9. Is customer acquisition creating an asset or renting demand?

A platform can spend heavily to bring a customer onto the network. That may be sensible if the customer keeps returning and produces enough contribution to repay the acquisition cost. It is less attractive if the customer leaves as soon as discounts stop.

The cleanest way to investigate this is by cohort. A cohort is a group of users who joined during the same period. Follow their repeat use, order frequency, spending, contribution and need for incentives as they age.

Customer lifetime value is an estimate, not a cash balance. It depends on future retention, margins and servicing costs. A small change in a long-term assumption can make a weak acquisition programme look attractive.

I want to know whether repeat customers become more profitable without steadily larger incentives. Blended averages can conceal a profitable old cohort and an expensive new one. Rapid growth can therefore make the headline customer base look healthier than its newest additions really are.

10. Why do quick commerce, logistics and home services need local economics?

A software network can appear national. Fulfilment remains stubbornly local. An order must be picked, packed and delivered. A professional must reach the customer's home. A parcel must move through a physical network with enough volume to use its capacity well.

Density can help. More orders in the same area may spread rent and staff costs, improve route use and reduce idle time. But entering new areas can reverse those benefits while demand is still developing.

This is why mature-location economics and total-company economics can diverge. An established store may be profitable while newer stores lose money. Neither number should replace the other. The first tests whether the model can work; the second tells us what the expansion currently costs.

I would track opening dates, throughput, capacity, repeat demand and the time needed for a location to cover its costs. A business that adds locations faster than they mature may report strong sales growth while needing more external capital.

For logistics providers, customer concentration creates another issue. A large marketplace can supply useful volume, negotiate aggressively or bring some delivery work in-house. A growing parcel market is not the same as secure profits for every parcel carrier. We need to understand who controls the customer relationship and the price.

11. What can weaken the network after it becomes large?

The first risk is over-monetisation. Higher fees, more advertisements or worse terms may improve near-term revenue but reduce the reason users joined. A network is not a licence to ignore the participants who make it valuable.

The second is a change in access. Search, app stores, payment systems and other distribution channels can influence how users reach a platform. If customer acquisition depends on another company's rules, that dependence belongs in the risk assessment.

The third is regulation and trust. The CCI's e-commerce study examined issues including platform-business bargaining power, contract terms, transparency and exclusivity. These are reasons to examine incentives and conduct, not proof that any named company has broken a rule. The study is dated 2020 and is not a statement of the latest legal position. [1]

Safety failures, poor-quality supply or unreliable dispute handling can also damage the network. Trust is part of the product. Spending less on it may lift a margin briefly and weaken the business later.

Finally, adjacent markets can be less forgiving than the original one. A company that succeeds in one category has not proved that it can repeat the result everywhere. Each expansion needs its own demand, supply, cost and competitive logic.

12. How do we move from platform growth to shareholder value?

A company can become much bigger without producing a proportionate gain for each share. New equity, employee stock awards, acquisitions and repeated funding rounds can change how much of the business an existing shareholder owns.

Share-based compensation is a useful example. It may not require an immediate cash payment, so an adjusted profit measure may exclude it. But the ownership transferred to employees still matters. I read diluted share counts and the compensation policy alongside the adjusted margin.

Cash flow also needs care. Customer advances can help working capital. Amounts collected for other participants may not belong economically to the platform. Capital expenditure, lease payments, acquisitions and taxes can create a large gap between an operating metric and cash available to owners.

My bridge is therefore: useful activity, revenue earned, contribution, whole-business operating profit, cash after reinvestment and finally value per diluted share. A break anywhere in that bridge deserves attention.

A low current profit does not settle valuation, because a business may be investing ahead of demand. A high growth rate does not settle valuation either, because the price can already assume years of excellent execution. I prefer explicit scenarios for growth, sustainable margins, reinvestment and dilution to a slogan about the size of the opportunity.

13. What would I put on a platform-business checklist?

  1. Identify every participant and mark who pays, who receives incentives and who does the work.
  2. Describe the network effect precisely. Look for better outcomes, not just a larger registered-user number.
  3. Test multi-homing, local liquidity and the real alternatives available to each side.
  4. Reconcile transaction value with revenue. Read the treatment of fees, discounts, taxes and principal-versus-agent accounting.
  5. Rebuild contribution using the company's stated cost definition, then move to whole-business profit.
  6. Check cohorts, renewals and acquisition payback. Ask what happens when incentives fall.
  7. Separate mature operations from new categories and locations. Track the cash required to expand.
  8. Read regulatory, trust, concentration and distribution risks as part of the economics.
  9. Follow free cash flow, capital allocation and diluted shares, not only adjusted EBITDA.
  10. Ask what expectations are already in the price. A strong business and an attractive investment are different conclusions.

The central lesson is that network size is an input, not the final answer. What matters is whether useful participation improves the product, whether customers keep paying for that value and whether the resulting cash reaches shareholders after the full cost of growth.
