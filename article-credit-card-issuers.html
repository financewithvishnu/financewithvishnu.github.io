Credit-card issuers: follow the spend, the balance and the losses

By Finance With Vishnu · Source period: June 2023

A card payment looks simple to the customer. For the businesses behind it, several different activities happen together: accepting a payment, routing information, funding a receivable, rewarding a customer and collecting a debt.

I would separate those activities before analysing a listed card company. Growing payment spend is not the same as growing interest income. A high lending yield is not the same as a high return after rewards, funding, operating costs and losses.

The useful question is not how many cards have been issued. It is whether the issuer can build active, profitable customer relationships without relying on unaffordable borrowing or permanently low defaults.

1. Which part of the card business are we studying?

The issuer provides the customer's card account and credit facility. The acquirer serves the merchant side. A network helps route and process transactions under its scheme. Other service providers can support different parts of that chain.

These roles can sit in separate companies or overlap within a group. A payment brand and a lender are not interchangeable descriptions.

Visa's FY2025 filing describes the issuer-acquirer payment chain and states that Visa itself does not issue cards or extend cardholder credit. Its network economics therefore differ from an issuer earning interest and bearing borrower losses. [1]

A co-brand adds another relationship. A retailer, airline or other partner can help acquire and engage customers, but its brand on the card does not by itself tell us who funds the receivable or bears credit risk. Map the contractual roles, revenue sharing and customer-service responsibilities.

2. Is interchange the whole merchant fee?

No. The merchant discount rate, or MDR, describes the merchant-side charge. Issuer interchange is a different part of the economics. The whole merchant charge should not be treated as income retained by the issuer.

Actual terms depend on product, transaction, merchant, agreement and applicable rules. The customer's borrowing rate is a separate price from these merchant-side charges.

Visa's FY2025 filing distinguishes interchange, merchant charges and the fees Visa earns from clients. It explicitly says its own fees are not derived from interchange or MDR. That is why saying the network keeps a fixed slice of every merchant fee is too loose. [1]

3. Why are spend and receivables different?

Spend measures purchases over a period. Receivables measure amounts owed at a date, or an average balance when used in a ratio. Customers can repay and spend again, so annual purchase volume can be much larger than the outstanding book.

Fees linked to transactions and interest linked to outstanding balances have different drivers. Divide each income stream by a relevant, consistently measured base.

SBI Card's Q4 FY2025 presentation defines spend, closing receivables, active-card measures and yield separately. Its yield definition uses annualised interest income relative to average receivables, not total annual spend. [2]

Also distinguish retail and corporate spend. A shift in mix can change the economics even when total volume rises.

4. Are customers who pay in full unprofitable?

Not necessarily. A customer who pays eligible purchases in full on time may generate spend-related income, annual fees and repeat activity while costing the issuer rewards, acquisition, service and short-term funding.

A revolver carries an interest-bearing balance under the agreement. That can generate more interest income, but defaults, collection expense and conduct risk may also be higher. Gross yield alone cannot rank customer quality.

EMI balances add another economic pattern. Instalments may involve customer interest, merchant or manufacturer support and fees, depending on the product. A change from revolving credit to instalments can alter yield, duration and losses together.

SBI Card's FY2025 presentation separates transactor, revolver and EMI receivables. These are balance-mix observations, not necessarily percentages of distinct people or purchase transactions. One customer can exhibit different behaviours over time. [2]

I would ask what each customer cohort earns over its life after all relevant costs and losses. Calling full-paying customers bad and borrowers good misses both the economics and borrower welfare.

5. What does an interest-free period actually mean?

An advertised maximum is not the same number of interest-free days for every purchase. Billing dates, purchase dates, payment history and transaction type matter. Cash withdrawals and carried balances can have different treatment from eligible purchases.

Paying a minimum due should not be confused with paying the full billed amount or preserving an interest-free period. RBI's card FAQ explains that partial payment can result in loss of the interest-free period and discusses interest on the outstanding amount after relevant adjustments. [4]

HDFC Bank's Freedom card fee page uses an “up to” interest-free description and lists separate cash-advance terms. That is a product-specific illustration of why a headline needs its conditions, not a universal statement of every issuer's terms. No fee or rate from that page is quoted here. [5]

For business analysis, this matters because payment behaviour changes funding needs and income recognition. For a customer, the actual agreement and statement matter more than a classroom example. This article is not a substitute for either.

6. Where does the issuer's profit go?

Start with interest, spend-related income and other fees. Then account for funding, rewards and other operating expenses, credit impairment, other relevant items and tax.

Classification differs across reports. Do not subtract rewards twice if the relevant revenue already reflects them. Check whether partner payments, incentive arrangements or fee reversals are netted or recorded as expenses.

SBI Card's FY2025 presentation separates interest income, fee categories, finance costs, operating costs and impairment. Its cost-to-income denominator is net of finance costs. Those definitions should stay attached to the ratios. [2]

An issuer can grow spend while profit falls if rewards, customer sourcing or credit costs rise faster than retained income.

7. Why do acquisition and rewards need a lifetime view?

A new customer can cost money before becoming active. Marketing, distribution, checks, onboarding and welcome benefits arrive early. Recovery of that investment depends on retention, activity, revenue and losses over time.

A large card base can conceal inactive or low-spending accounts. Conversely, a narrower base with regular usage may support better economics. Compare cards in force, monthly activity, spend per active customer, retention and acquisition cost where disclosed.

Rewards can encourage usage but are a cost of earning that usage. A premium annual fee can help pay for benefits rather than simply becoming extra profit. Generous acquisition offers may attract customers who leave when the offer ends.

SBI Card's FY2025 annual report discusses digital sourcing, co-brands, acquisition costs and the challenge of balancing reward design with profitability. These are management descriptions, not independent proof that every new product earns an attractive return. [3]

For customers, rewards are not a gain if extra spending, fees or borrowing costs exceed the value received. A business model that depends on confusion deserves a different assessment from one built on useful service.

8. Why are credit costs so important?

Card receivables expose the issuer to borrower repayment risk. Fraud and operational losses add other risks. A short purchase cycle does not guarantee a short or fully recoverable default.

Track origination cohorts, overdue movement, write-offs, recoveries and provisions alongside the closing bad-loan ratio. Strong new-account growth can leave a young book whose eventual losses are not visible yet.

Gross credit cost and net credit cost can differ because of recoveries and the company's definitions. Neither should be replaced by a closing GNPA percentage.

SBI Card's Q4 FY2025 presentation separately shows gross write-offs, provision movements, recoveries and net credit cost. This is a useful reporting bridge, not a reason to assume recovered amounts will repeat indefinitely. [2]

Stress can arrive with lower spend, weaker fee income and higher collection costs. A model based only on average loss rates may understate the damage when these drivers move together.

9. Is funding risk negligible because balances turn quickly?

No. The issuer settles obligations before receiving every customer repayment. Borrowers who revolve or convert to instalments can keep part of the book outstanding longer than a simple purchase-and-repayment story suggests.

Map funding maturities, lender concentration, liquid resources and collection assumptions. A strong parent name is not the same as unlimited committed funding at a fixed price.

When funding rates change, the timing of repricing matters. The reported customer rate does not necessarily change immediately, and competition or conduct constraints can limit what the issuer can pass through.

Compare NIM using the issuer's own definition. SBI Card's presentation uses average receivables for its margin measure and average borrowings for cost of funds. Subtracting one rate from another without those balances is not an exact bridge to reported margin. [2]

10. Does UPI necessarily replace credit cards?

Payment interface and source of funds are different dimensions. A customer can initiate a payment through one interface while drawing on a credit facility. Competition can shift where transactions happen without removing the demand for credit.

NPCI's September 2026 RuPay consolidated circular describes RuPay credit-card linkage to UPI as an additional usage mechanism. That is a later dated cross-check of the distinction, not proof that every network, card, merchant or transaction has identical access or economics. [6]

SBI Card's FY2025 report also discusses UPI-linked RuPay card activity. The existence of the channel does not guarantee that every added transaction earns the old fee yield. [3]

I would test acceptance, eligible use, customer behaviour, rewards, retained revenue and losses. Both payment methods can grow while a particular issuer's margin weakens. Equally, a change in interface can widen the issuer's reach if its economics remain sound.

No current MDR, interchange schedule, transaction cap or product eligibility list is reproduced here. Those are scheme- and rule-specific operating facts, not stable assumptions for a long-term valuation.

11. What does a change in mix do to profit?

Two issuers with equal receivables can produce different income because their customers, spending patterns, fees and interest-bearing balances differ. Equal gross income can still produce different profit after rewards, funding and losses.

This is not a forecast that every interest-bearing customer is riskier by a fixed amount. It is a reminder that a higher revenue yield is incomplete without loss-adjusted economics.

Also distinguish a temporary festive-spend jump from durable engagement, a recovery quarter from a full year and a change in accounting classification from an improvement in the business.

12. What belongs on the research sheet?

Begin with role and scope: issuer, network, acquirer, co-brand and standalone versus group reporting. Then separate cards issued, active accounts, spend, closing receivables and average receivables.

Map transactor, revolver and EMI balances with their exact dates and definitions. Rebuild the income and expense bridge, including rewards, acquisition, funding, impairment and recoveries without double counting.

Next examine vintage loss behaviour, customer conduct, fraud controls, funding resilience and partner dependence. A portfolio should remain viable without assuming customers always spend more, borrow more and default less at the same time.

Finally connect sustainable profit, capital needs, dilution and valuation. A pure-play card issuer gives focused exposure to these economics, but less diversification when the card business itself weakens.

The conclusion is simple: follow the payment flow, then the balance sheet, then the losses. A large card count or attractive headline yield cannot do that work for us.
