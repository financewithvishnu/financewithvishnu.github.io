Chapter 38. Mutual Funds: Structure, Types and Costs

Chapter 38 of 85 · about 20 minutes

More Indian household money now reaches the stock market through mutual funds than through any other door, and most of it arrives without its owner ever learning what a mutual fund actually is. That gap costs real money: the difference between the industry's most and least honest products, compounded over a working life, is a flat in a good city. Part V begins with the machine itself: who holds your money, what the schemes are, and what they charge you.

38.1How does a mutual fund work: AMC, trustee, custodian and NAV?

A mutual fund is a trust, not a company, and the structure exists to separate your money from the people managing it. The AMC, the asset management company, manages the portfolio and earns the fee. The trustees oversee the AMC on your behalf, legally required to act in unitholders' interest. The custodian, a separate institution, actually holds the securities, so the AMC cannot touch them. And the NAV, net asset value, is the daily price of one unit: the fund's total assets minus costs, divided by units, published every evening. The practical meaning: when you invest, you buy newly created units at that evening's NAV; when you redeem, the fund buys them back at NAV. Your money is pooled, priced daily, and legally walled off from the manager's own balance sheet, which is why even a failed AMC does not take your holdings with it.

38.2What are equity, debt, hybrid and solution-oriented schemes?

The menu, in the order you will meet it. Equity funds own stocks: large-cap, mid-cap, small-cap, flexi-cap (the manager chooses the mix), sector and thematic (one industry or story, Chapter 31 in a wrapper), and ELSS, the tax-saving variant with a three-year lock-in. Debt funds own bonds and money-market paper, from overnight funds, nearly cash, to long-duration gilt funds, which are bond-price bets, Chapters 42-44 supply the grammar. Hybrid funds blend the two, from conservative, mostly debt, to aggressive, mostly equity, and include the popular balanced-advantage category that shifts with valuations. Solution-oriented schemes package retirement and children goals with lock-ins. The category label is a promise about the machine inside; read the actual portfolio before believing it, because "flexi" and "balanced" cover multitudes.

38.3What is the difference between regular and direct plans?

Every Indian mutual fund scheme comes in two flavours with identical portfolios: the regular plan, bought through a distributor, which pays that distributor a commission from your money every year, forever; and the direct plan, bought from the AMC or a direct platform, which does not. The commission, typically 0.7-1.2 percentage points a year in equity schemes, sounds small and is not: it is a perpetual annual tax on your entire growing corpus. The chart below is the arithmetic on a ₹25,000 monthly SIP over twenty years at 12% market returns: the direct plan retires with roughly ₹30 lakh more, several years of the SIP itself, donated quietly to a distribution chain many investors never knew they were paying.

Regular plans are not a scam; a good advisor can earn the fee many times over in behaviour coaching alone. But know the price, decide whether you are receiving the service, and never pay it by accident.

38.4What do expense ratios, exit loads and hidden costs take from your returns?

The total expense ratio, TER, is the all-in annual fee, deducted daily from the NAV invisibly: you never see a bill, which is precisely why it works. Equity TERs run from under 0.2% for index funds to 2%+ for small active schemes; every 0.5% of TER is roughly 10% of your twenty-five-year wealth. Exit loads penalise early redemption, commonly 1% within a year, and are disclosed, fair, and worth respecting. The quieter costs: the portfolio's own trading costs, not in the TER; cash drag in a fund holding 5% cash; and, in fund-of-funds, a second layer of fees. The habit to build: before buying any scheme, read three numbers in the factsheet, TER, exit load, and portfolio turnover, and compare them with the category's honest alternatives. Five minutes, once per fund, is among the highest-paid reading you will ever do.

38.5How are mutual funds regulated in India?

SEBI's fund regulation is one of Indian finance's quiet successes. It mandates the trust structure, caps and standardises TERs by fund size, bans entry loads outright, forces daily NAV publication, standardises the category definitions so "large-cap" means the same thing at every AMC, requires skin-in-the-game from key employees, and polices mis-selling with increasing teeth. The result: outright fund fraud is rare in India; the industry's extraction happens legally, through costs and complexity, which is why this chapter's cost literacy matters more than any fraud fear. Abroad, the SEC plays the same role for US funds. Regulation makes funds safe to own; it cannot make them cheap to own. That part is still your job.

38.6Worked example: the 20-year cost of 1% higher fees

Case: same SIP, same market, two plans

₹25,000 a month, twenty years, market returns 12% a year. The direct plan at 0.8% TER compounds at 11.2% and ends near ₹2.22 crore. The regular plan at 1.9% TER compounds at 10.1% and ends near ₹1.92 crore. The 1.1-point annual difference, invisible in every statement, consumed ₹30 lakh, about 14% of the final wealth. And the rule scales: on a ₹50,000 SIP over thirty years the same percentage gap costs over ₹3 crore. Fee awareness is not frugality; it is the single largest investment decision most mutual fund investors will ever make, disguised as a rounding error.
