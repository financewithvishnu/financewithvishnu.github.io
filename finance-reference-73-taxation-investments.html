Chapter 73. Taxation of Investments

Chapter 73 of 85 · about 19 minutes

You do not earn what an asset returns; you earn what the tax law lets you keep. India's rules tax each instrument by its own logic, equity one way, debt another, gold a third, and the differences are large enough to outrank most stock-picking skill. This chapter is the map, current as of the 2024-25 framework, with the caveat that budget day can move any number in it.

73.1How are capital gains taxed in India: short-term versus long-term?

The 2024 budget simplified the table into something memorable. Listed equity and equity funds: long-term after twelve months, taxed at 12.5% beyond a ₹1.25 lakh annual exemption; short-term at 20%. Debt funds, gold and most other assets: long-term after longer holding periods, generally at 12.5% without indexation, the indexation debate settled, for now, in simplicity's favour; specified debt funds bought after April 2023 are taxed at slab rates regardless of holding period, the old indexation advantage legislatively closed. Real estate: long-term after two years at 12.5%, with a transitional choice for older residential properties. The strategic readings: the system now taxes equity lightest and longest-term, a deliberate policy tilt toward capital formation; the slab-versus-12.5% gap makes your own tax bracket a factor in asset choice; and the holding-period boundaries, twelve months, twenty-four months, are cliffs worth circling on the calendar, because selling a week early is a permanently expensive way to be impatient.

73.2How are dividends and interest taxed?

Income is taxed more simply and more heavily than gains. Dividends arrive at your slab rate, with the company withholding tax on larger payouts, a change from the old dividend-distribution-tax world that made high-yield investing expensive for top-bracket investors. Interest, FDs, bonds, savings accounts, P2P, is slab-taxed in full, which is why the 7% FD of Chapter 45 is a 4.9% FD at the 30% slab before we discuss anything else. The structural consequence is one of the most useful facts in Indian personal finance: the tax law pays you to take risk. A debt-like return earned as interest carries slab tax every year; a similar return earned as long-term capital gain on equity carries 12.5% with an exemption and deferral until sale. The compounding arithmetic of deferral alone, paying the taxman in year five rather than every year, is worth more than most fund managers. The chart in Case 1 prices the difference.

73.3What are STT, stamp duty and the small costs that add up?

Beneath the headline taxes runs a layer of friction levies, individually trivial, structurally significant. Securities transaction tax takes a small slice of every exchange trade, fractions of a percent, both ways on some legs, and its real bite is behavioural: at Chapter 61's trading frequencies, STT and its cousins are the reliable tax on the illusion of skill. Stamp duty on trades, transfers and property adds another fraction. Exchange charges, SEBI fees, GST on brokerage complete the stack. The habit to build is the full-friction ledger: before any strategy, sum every levy per round trip and multiply by your intended frequency, because a "0.1% cost" is 2.4% a year at one round trip a month, and 24% at one a week. The patient investor's tax edge is not cleverness; it is frequency. The state has priced activity, and priced it against you.

73.4What is tax-loss harvesting, and how do you plan within the rules?

Legitimate tax planning is the year's most reliable alpha, and its main instruments are three. Tax-loss harvesting: realise losses against taxable gains before March 31, selling a loser you no longer want, or one you do and rebuying immediately, India's rules currently permitting the rebuy without a wash-sale clock, though the principle of economic substance deserves respect. Using the exemptions: the ₹1.25 lakh annual LTCG exemption is use-it-or-lose-it, so booking long-term gains up to the line each year, harvesting the exemption, re-bases your portfolio tax-free over time. Choosing the wrapper before the asset: the same return in an FD, a fund, an SGB or an NPS tier is four different tax outcomes, and the wrapper decision, made once, outperforms a decade of tinkering. The discipline boundary is equally clear: planning arranges real affairs to use the law as written; evasion invents affairs, and the difference is not the paperwork, it is whether the transactions happened. Every scheme whose first selling point is secrecy is telling you which side it is on.

73.5What are withholding tax, tax treaties and the US estate-tax trap?

Cross-border investing imports a second tax code, and three concepts carry most of the weight. Withholding tax: the source country takes its cut first, the US keeping 25% of an Indian resident's dividends under the treaty, and your home system then credits it, so the paperwork of the foreign tax credit is real money. Tax treaties are the treaties that set those rates and assign taxing rights between countries; the practical rule is that the treaty rate is a ceiling you claim, not a default you receive, which is why the broker's W-8BEN form is worth its five minutes. And the trap that surprises estates: US estate tax applies to US-situs assets, including US stocks, held by non-residents above a low exemption, $60,000, at rates that begin where the shock begins. The standard mitigations are holding through non-US-domiciled funds, Irish UCITS ETFs being the institutional answer, and sizing direct holdings with the threshold in view. The principle generalises beyond America: when you buy a foreign asset, you are marrying two tax systems, and the engagement letter is the treaty.

73.6Worked examples and cases

Case 1: after-tax return of FD vs debt fund vs equity fund

₹10 lakh, five years, an investor at the 30% slab. The chart shows the after-tax destinations. The FD at 8%: interest taxed at slab every year, the money compounding at 5.6% net, ending at ₹13.13 lakh. The debt fund at the same 8% gross: no annual tax, the whole gain taxed at slab on redemption, deferral doing quiet work, ending at ₹13.29 lakh. The equity fund at a 12% gross assumption: five years of untaxed compounding, then 12.5% on gains above the exemption, ending at ₹16.83 lakh. Three lessons. Deferral is a return source: the debt fund beat the FD on identical gross returns purely by delaying the taxman. The equity premium survived the tax code intact, roughly double the FD's terminal value. And none of the three numbers appears on any advertisement, because tax is personal, which is precisely why it is where the diligent investor's edge hides.

Case 2: an Indian's US stocks and the estate-tax threshold

Consider an investor who built, patiently over years, a direct US portfolio of $800,000, held at an American brokerage, titles in their own name. The planning gap nobody mentioned: US-situs assets of a non-resident above $60,000 sit inside the US estate-tax net, at marginal rates reaching 40%, so an unexpected death could present the family a six-figure-dollar tax bill, in a foreign system, against assets that must be valued and reported before they can be touched. The fixes are structural and cheap compared to the exposure: hold the US exposure through Irish-domiciled UCITS ETFs, which own the same American companies without creating US situs for the holder; keep direct holdings under the threshold; or accept the risk knowingly with the estate prepared. The broader reading: cross-border tax is mostly boring, a withholding rate here, a credit there, until it is catastrophically not, and the trap is always in the intersection nobody thought to check. Spend the one hour mapping which of your assets sit in which jurisdiction's nets; it is the highest-yielding hour in this chapter.
