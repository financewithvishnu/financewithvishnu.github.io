Chapter 70. Geopolitics, Sanctions and the Future of Money

Chapter 70 of 85 · about 19 minutes

For thirty years, investors could treat geopolitics as background noise; the era of one hyperpower, open trade and financial globalisation made politics somebody else's risk factor. That era is over. Wars now arrive in portfolios by the following Monday, sanctions rewrite the meaning of "reserve asset", and the question of what money itself will be has reopened. This closing chapter of Part XII maps the new terrain.

70.1How do wars, sanctions and trade blocs hit your portfolio?

Geopolitical shocks reach a portfolio through four channels, and naming them converts panic into analysis. Prices: oil, gas, grain and metals spike on supply fear, the 2022 invasion of Ukraine repricing Europe's energy and the world's breadbasket in weeks. Flows: sanctions and countersanctions sever the plumbing, assets frozen, payment systems weaponised, foreign investors in Russian securities discovering their holdings were only as good as the politics. Earnings: companies lose markets, suppliers and shipping routes, the cost arriving in margins before it arrives in headlines. And risk appetite: the indiscriminate first days, when everything risky is sold and correlations run to one, followed by differentiation as the actual exposure of each asset becomes priced. The portfolio disciplines are old ones in new dress: diversification across countries and currencies, energy and commodity awareness, and a written policy for the first scary week, because the investor who must decide during it will decide badly. The historical base rate is also worth holding: markets have absorbed every war of the modern era within quarters to years, and the investors ruined by them were the leveraged and the concentrated, not the patient.

70.2What is the dollar system, and who challenges it?

The world's money is a one-currency system: the dollar anchors trade invoicing, commodity pricing, global debt and central bank reserves, and the network effects are the moat, everyone uses dollars because everyone uses dollars, deep Treasury markets supply the safe asset, and the legal and payments infrastructure runs through New York. The challengers are real but constrained. The euro has the scale without the unified safe asset; the renminbi has the state's backing without its openness, since a reserve currency must be freely usable in ways capital controls forbid; gold is the apolitical reserve that yields nothing, which is precisely why central banks, led by emerging markets, have been buying it at record pace. The serious change is at the margin, not the throne: more trade invoiced bilaterally in local currencies, reserves diversified from three-fifths dollar toward a slightly smaller majority, and sanction-proofing as an explicit goal of reserve managers. For the investor: the dollar system's privileges, cheap US borrowing, the world's demand for Treasuries, are the quiet foundation of several asset prices you own, and its erosion would be a decades-long repricing, not a headline.

70.3What do supply chains and reshoring mean for investment themes?

The efficiency era built supply chains like clockwork: single-source, just-in-time, optimised for the cheapest stable world. The resilience era is rebuilding them for the world as it is: friend-shoring and reshoring, duplicate capacity, strategic inventories, semiconductors treated as national security. The investment consequences run in both directions, and the honest analyst holds both. The cost side: resilience is redundancy, and redundancy is expense, structurally higher costs and stickier inflation than the 2010s, with the interest rates to match. The opportunity side: the beneficiaries are identifiable, industrial automation, grid and energy investment, defence, logistics, and the countries positioned as alternative workshops, India among them, with its production-linked incentives courting exactly this migration. The investor's caution is Chapter 66's lesson in industrial form: a true macro theme is not a return forecast. Everyone knows the theme by the time it has an ETF, and the question is always the same, what is priced in, and what cash flows actually arrive?

70.4How do demographics drive the long growth story?

The slowest and most investable force of all is the age of nations. The arc is common: poor and young, then a demographic dividend as the working-age share swells, then ageing, then, in the extreme cases, shrinking. China is deep into the turn, its workforce past its peak, which recasts its growth model and the world's manufacturing map. Europe and Japan are the finished versions: growth gravity from a shrinking, ageing base, funded by debt and immigration debates. India is the world's great remaining dividend: a young, still-growing workforce with the dividend window open for another two to three decades, and the entire thesis of Indian equities rests, at bottom, on converting that demographic fact into productivity, through education, jobs, urbanisation and women's participation, none of which is automatic. The investor's use of demographics is horizon-setting, not trading: it tells you which economies have tailwinds for thirty years, it says nothing about next year's market, and it is the deepest reason this website treats India's long-run equity case as real but not guaranteed, a window, not a birthright.

70.5Worked examples and cases

Case 1: frozen Russian reserves and what they changed

After February 2022, roughly $300 billion of Russia's central bank reserves, held as securities and deposits in Western jurisdictions, were immobilised by sanctions: owned, but unusable, a distinction no reserve manager had previously needed to price. The tremor ran through every central bank on earth. The logic was inescapable: reserves held in another state's jurisdiction are an asset with a political condition attached, enforceable at that state's choice. The responses are measurable: central-bank gold purchases running at multiples of the prior decade's pace, reserve diversification toward non-traditional currencies, and bilateral payment arrangements built to route around the Western plumbing. The debate over going further, confiscating the principal rather than freezing it, matters because it defines the edge of the system: interest on the frozen assets has already been redirected, and each step changes what "safe asset" means for the rest of the world. The portfolio lesson is Chapter 69's country risk, upgraded from theory to precedent: jurisdiction is a risk factor in every cross-border holding, yours included, and it is priced, currently, at approximately zero, which is exactly what risks look like before they are priced.

Case 2: the rupee-rouble oil trade question

When sanctions cut Russia from dollar plumbing, India faced a concrete problem: it was buying large volumes of discounted Russian crude, and paying for it was suddenly a financial-engineering question. The attempted answers are a masterclass in why invoicing currencies are sticky. Rupee settlement created a pile of rupees in Indian banks that Russia had little to buy with, tens of billions accumulating with nowhere useful to go, because a currency's usefulness is the depth of what it can purchase, not the willingness of two states to print a swap line. Payment routed, awkwardly, through third currencies and third countries, each layer adding cost and compliance risk. The discounted crude itself was real money saved, billions on the import bill, but the episode's durable lesson is Section 70.2 in miniature: a reserve-and-trade currency is a network, not a logo, and networks are displaced by decades of institutional depth, not by a single crisis's workaround. Watch the margins: each such arrangement normalises the next, and the dollar system's erosion, if it comes, will look exactly like this, a thousand bilateral improvisations that slowly stop being improvised.
