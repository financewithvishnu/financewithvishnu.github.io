Water treatment: the plant, the contract and the chemical

By Finance With Vishnu · Source period: approximately September 2024 · Exact class dates unconfirmed

Nobody in this sector sells water. They sell the plants that treat it, the contracts that run those plants, and the chemicals the plants consume every day they operate. Three different businesses hide inside the phrase "water company", and they differ in the only way that matters to an investor: when the cash arrives and how often it comes back.

India's water buildout is real and policy-funded, from Namami Gange to AMRUT 2.0 and Jal Jeevan Mission. FY2024-25 shows what that means on a company P&L: strong order books and rising revenues, and right beside them, receivables, retention money and working-capital limits doing the quiet heavy lifting. Follow the plant, the contract and the chemical.

Study window: this is an original explanation of an approximately September 2024 framework; the exact class day is unconfirmed. FY2024-25 annual reports provide dated checks. They are not current data. This is education, not investment advice.

What does a water company actually sell?

Start with the chain. Raw water arrives from a river, reservoir or borewell; treatment plants make it drinkable; distribution networks deliver it; sewage networks collect what comes back; sewage and effluent plants treat it again for discharge or reuse. Companies position themselves at different points. EPC contractors design and build the plants. Equipment makers supply the membranes, pumps and resins inside them. Operators run the plants for years after commissioning. Chemical suppliers sell the consumables every plant drinks daily.

VA Tech Wabag describes its own offer as the full lifecycle: engineering, procurement, construction, commissioning and long-term operation and maintenance, on EPC, O&M, DBO, BOOT and hybrid-annuity models, across municipal and industrial customers.[1] That sentence is a menu of five different cash-flow shapes. The first task in this sector is always to ask which one a given rupee of revenue came from.

What does an EPC contract really promise?

An engineering, procurement and construction contract is a promise to deliver a working plant for a fixed price, against a design the customer approves and a timeline the monsoon and the municipality will test. Revenue is recognised as work progresses, but the risks, cost overruns, delayed approvals, site access, sit with the builder. A low bid won in a hurry becomes a loss delivered over three years, which is why experienced players talk about bid discipline more than order wins.

The contract structure decides who carries which risk. Pure EPC ends at commissioning. DBO adds years of operation. BOOT and hybrid-annuity models make the contractor part-owner or financier, paid back over time. Wabag's order book of Rs 13,667 crore spans exactly these models,[1] and reading it means asking how much is construction (lumpy, risky, faster) and how much is long-duration operation and annuity (steadier, slower, financed by the company).

How do the contract models differ?

The equipment-and-consumables end of the sector is a different business again. Ion Exchange sells water-treatment chemicals and ion-exchange resins, among the widest ranges in the industry, alongside home water solutions.[2] Chemicals and resins are consumed and reordered; a plant built once buys them for decades. This is the razor-and-blade logic of water: the installed base of plants and equipment is the razor, and the treatment inputs are the blades.

Compare the shapes side by side and the sector sorts itself. EPC is high revenue, thin margin, heavy working capital. O&M and annuity are slower, steadier and finance-hungry. Chemicals and consumables are recurring, brand-and-channel driven, and the least exposed to any single customer or project.

Who pays for a sewage plant?

Mostly the government, and mostly on a schedule it sets. Municipal water and sewage projects are funded through state missions, central schemes like AMRUT 2.0, Namami Gange and Jal Jeevan Mission, and multilateral loans routed through utilities.[1] The customer is creditworthy in the sovereign sense and slow in the operational sense: approvals, fund releases and inspections all run on administrative time.

This is why the sector's growth is policy-paced and why its companies learn to read mission budgets the way farmers read monsoon forecasts. When the schemes expand, order inflows surge across every player at once; when a mission cycle pauses between phases, the same companies live off their backlogs and their O&M books. The strongest operators plan for the pause in advance: they build the annuity layers, O&M contracts and consumables, precisely because construction orders cannot be scheduled.

How does an order become cash?

The execution cycle runs bid, award, design approval, procurement, construction, commissioning, then the long tail: defect liability, retention money, and final acceptance. Cash arrives on milestones, and a slice of every bill, retention, stays with the customer until the plant proves itself, sometimes years later. Enviro Infra Engineers' trade receivables carry credit periods of 30 to 90 days plus retention money held back by customers,[3] a disclosure you will find in some form in every EPC report in this sector.

The contractor funds the gap. Wabag expanded its working-capital limits to Rs 5,000 crore in FY2024-25 explicitly to pursue larger, more complex projects.[1] In water EPC, the working-capital line is not a footnote. It is the capacity constraint.

Why do receivables decide who survives?

When the customer is a municipality, profit is an opinion but cash is a negotiation. The companies that endure in this sector share one habit: they price the payment delay into the bid, chase milestones relentlessly, and keep enough balance-sheet room to outwait a slow quarter of government payments. The ones that fail usually did everything right on the plant and everything wrong on the invoice.

Watch the ratio, not the story. A water company growing revenue 46% while receivables grow faster is buying growth with its own balance sheet. The steady players, Wabag's profitable-growth mantra is a good example, treat working capital as a discipline, expanding limits to fund chosen growth rather than being forced by it.[1]

The arithmetic is unforgiving in both directions. Add 30 days of receivables to a company billing Rs 1,000 crore a year and roughly Rs 82 crore of cash disappears into the gap, money that must come from a bank limit or from the next equity raise. Recover those 30 days and the same sum returns to fund new bids. In a business where every project pays on the customer's calendar, the collection department quietly writes the growth plan.

What is so attractive about O&M?

Once the plant exists, someone must run it, and that someone bills every month for years. O&M revenue is smaller per year than the construction contract but arrives like an annuity: predictable, repeatable, and far less dependent on winning the next tender. It also builds the operator's case for the next plant, because nobody understands a technology better than the team paid to keep it running.

Enviro Infra Engineers shows the shape clearly: an EPC order book of about Rs 1,185 crore across 22 projects, plus an O&M order book of Rs 806 crore providing, in its own words, clear visibility into future revenues.[3] A young EPC company building its O&M book is converting project wins into annuity, one commissioned plant at a time.

Where do chemicals and resins fit?

Every treatment plant consumes resins, membranes, coagulants and speciality chemicals for as long as it runs, and the supplier of those inputs holds the most enviable position in the chain: recurring demand, hundreds of customers, no single project risk, and pricing tied to specification rather than tender. Ion Exchange's chemical segment, one of the widest ion-exchange resin ranges globally, kept growing through FY2024-25 on exactly this logic, with the company noting operational efficiency and raw-material cost management as the levers that protect its margin.[2]

The consumables business is why two companies with the same "water" label can deserve completely different valuations. One builds plants and chases receivables; the other sells what every plant must keep buying. The second is closer to a speciality-chemicals company than to a contractor.

What do order books promise here?

Every water company advertises its backlog, and the same decoding rules apply as anywhere else: duration, mix and counterparty. Wabag's Rs 13,667 crore spans construction and long-duration operation across geographies and funding models.[1] Enviro Infra's pair of books, Rs 1,185 crore of EPC and Rs 806 crore of O&M, is a young company converting projects into annuity.[3] A backlog number without those splits tells you the size of the promise and nothing about its quality.

The questions that convert headline into analysis: how much of the book is construction versus recurring operation, how much is funded by which mission or multilateral programme, what margins were bid, and how fast the book converts. A 15-month EPC book and a 10-year O&M book are different assets wearing the same label.

What do the FY2024-25 numbers actually show?

Three listed companies, three mixes of the model. Wabag, the global EPC-and-O&M specialist, runs an Rs 13,667 crore order book on disciplined working capital. Ion Exchange pairs project work with a chemicals franchise and earns a steadier margin. Enviro Infra Engineers, the young municipal EPC challenger, is growing fastest and building its annuity book. Same sector, same policy wave, three different cash engines.

Where does technology actually matter?

Water treatment sells proven reliability, but the frontier still moves: desalination, water reuse and recycling, zero-liquid-discharge for industry, and membrane technologies that keep improving cost per litre. The companies with references in these technologies win the complex, better-margin projects, while plain civil-heavy sewage plants drift toward commodity bidding. Wabag's international presence and its descriptor of choice, a pure-play water technology company, are claims about exactly this high end.[1]

Technology here is less a treadmill than a ladder: each harder problem, industrial effluent, reuse, desalination, has fewer qualified bidders. The qualification itself is the moat, earned plant by plant over decades.

The practical test is reference depth. A company that has commissioned and then operated a technology carries proof that survives a bid evaluation; a company that has only built one pilot carries a brochure. When a report lists plants by technology, capacity and years in operation, it is showing you the real product catalogue. Everything else is aspiration.

What can break a water business?

Five breaks matter. Payment delay: the municipal customer pays slowly, and a contractor that grows faster than its working capital can be profitable and insolvent at once. Bid aggression: a fixed-price plant won cheap delivers its loss over years, with retention held back at the end. Policy pauses: mission-driven demand arrives in waves, and an empty inflow quarter exposes the fixed cost of capacity. Concentration: a few large projects or one state mission can dominate a book. And execution risk on the annuity side: O&M and concession income is performance-linked, so a poorly run plant pays poorly.

Notice what is absent: demand risk. India will build water infrastructure for decades. Every break in this sector is about who finances the delay between building and being paid, which is why the balance sheet reads like the strategy.

What would make us walk away?

Walk away from order books quoted without a split between construction, O&M and annuity, because the mix is the business. Walk away from revenue growth that receivables outrun, from retention-heavy books presented as cash, and from any player that treats a mission's tender calendar as permanent. Be sceptical of technology claims without operating references, and of margins that depend on winning every future bid at today's prices.

The keepers are the mirror image: disciplined bidding visible in stable margins, a growing O&M or consumables annuity under the lumpy EPC, working capital sized by choice rather than desperation, and technology references that narrow the bidder list. Water is a forever market, and it will be built regardless of who builds it. The companies worth studying are the ones structured to be paid, on time and more than once, for serving it.
