Cement: the quarry, the kiln and the kilometre

By Finance With Vishnu · Source period: approximately July 2025 · Exact class date unconfirmed

Cement is the simplest product in construction and one of the hardest businesses. The product is a commodity powder that every buyer treats as identical, it costs more to move than to make, and the plant that makes it must sit next to a limestone quarry that took decades to assemble. Nobody wins by inventing a better cement. They win on location, logistics and fuel.

Three numbers organise the whole industry: how much clinker a company can make, how far its cement travels, and what it pays for fuel and freight. FY2024-25 illustrates all four reports at once: enormous capacity additions and acquisitions, and yet falling profits at the largest player. Follow the quarry, the kiln and the kilometre.

1. What does a cement company actually make?

Two products in sequence. First, clinker: limestone, mined from the company's own or leased quarries, crushed, blended with additives and fired in a kiln at around 1,450 degrees, emerging as hard grey nodules. Second, cement: clinker ground fine with gypsum and, usually, fly ash or slag. Clinker is where the chemistry, the quarry and most of the energy live; grinding is where the product meets the market.

The distinction runs through every financial statement in this industry. Dalmia Bharat reports its scale journey from a 250-tonnes-per-day plant in 1939 to 49.5 million tonnes per annum of cement capacity, with a target of 110 to 130 MTPA.[4] UltraTech closed FY2024-25 with 188.8 MTPA of consolidated grey cement capacity.[1] Both numbers are grinding capacity; the clinker line behind each is the binding constraint and the real capital. When a company announces cement capacity, the first question is how much clinker stands behind it.

2. Why is cement a regional business?

Because a tonne of cement is worth little and weighs a tonne. Dalmia Bharat's FY2024-25 report puts freight on finished goods at 19.9% of revenue, up from 18.8% the year before, with freight per tonne of cement sold rising from Rs 1,113 to Rs 1,120.[4] When a fifth of revenue goes to transport, every additional kilometre of selling distance comes straight out of the margin. Cement plants do not have a national market; they have a radius.

The industry measures this radius openly. UltraTech reports that its lead distance, the average distance cement travels to market, reduced to 384 km in FY2024-25.[1] Ambuja says it is working towards a 100 km reduction in lead distances by optimising plant and warehouse footprints.[2] A company reporting falling lead distance is reporting margin repair in physical units. Watch this number the way you would watch a retailer's same-store sales.

3. Why split clinker plants from grinding units?

Because the two ends of the process want different addresses. The clinker plant wants to sit on the limestone; the grinding unit wants to sit inside the market. Moving clinker is cheaper than moving cement's full weight of limestone, and grinding near the customer cuts the last and most expensive leg. Ambuja's report describes adding 11 GPWIS rakes to stabilise clinker supply and 60 BCFC rakes to move fly ash from power plants to its cement plants.[2] That is the split-model logistics machine in one sentence: rail-fed clinker flowing from quarry-side kilns to market-side grinders, with waste ash flowing back the other way as raw material.

The split also shapes what a capacity announcement means. A grinding unit is a modest, quick investment that converts clinker advantage into market share; an integrated clinker line is the heavy, slow, regulatory-laden bet. Dalmia describes its capex as directed towards integrated plants and clinker units, aligned with grinding capacity added against clinker availability.[4] Read every expansion as two questions: where does the clinker come from, and where does the cement go?

4. What drives the cost per tonne?

After freight, the big levers are fuel, power and raw materials. The kiln burns coal or pet coke; the grinders consume electricity; the quarry supplies limestone. UltraTech's report lists commodity price risk around coal and pet coke, managed through long-term supply agreements, new sources and fixed-price swaps.[1] Fuel is a traded commodity priced in dollars, so the cement margin carries an embedded energy bet that no amount of local market dominance removes.

Ambuja's FY2024-25 report shows the lever from the other side: operating EBITDA of Rs 5,971 crore resulted partly from reductions in power, fuel and freight expenses, helping net profit reach Rs 5,158 crore at a 15% margin.[2] Note the pattern: in a year when pricing was weak, cost lines, not price lines, defended the profit. Cement margins are made in the fuel contract and the dispatch schedule as much as in the market.

5. Why do fuel and freight decide the year?

Because the selling price is mostly outside the company's control while these costs are partly inside it. Cement pricing is regional, competitive and cyclical, so in a soft pricing year the P&L swings with diesel, coal, pet coke and rail tariffs. The FY2024-25 reports read this way throughout: Ambuja credits lower power, fuel and freight costs for its EBITDA,[2] while Dalmia's freight ratio climbed to 19.9% of revenue.[4] Same year, same industry, different cost outcomes, and the profits followed the costs.

The study habit is to normalise: when margins improve, ask how much came from cheaper fuel and freight that can reverse, and how much from structural gains like shorter lead distances, higher blended-cement share or waste-heat recovery that cannot. UltraTech's 384 km lead distance and Ambuja's 100 km reduction programme belong to the second kind.[1][2]

6. What does capacity cost, and what must it return?

Cement capacity is bought in lumps at thousands of crores per line, years before it earns. The test for any addition is the incremental return: the new line's EBITDA against its full cost, including the working capital and logistics to feed it, measured at realistic utilisation rather than the nameplate. A plant that reaches full utilisation in year five has a very different return profile from the same plant assumed full from day one.

Replacement and debottlenecking capex usually passes this test far more easily than greenfield, because it adds volume to an existing quarry, kiln infrastructure and market. Ambuja's 0.5 MTPA from debottlenecking and 2.4 MTPA at Farakka are the cheap tonnes; a brand-new integrated line is the expensive one.[2] When reading expansion plans, separate the two kinds and apply the incremental test to each.

7. Why is the industry consolidating?

Because scale now compounds. Dalmia's report records that the top four companies' capacity share rose from about 48% in 2021-22 to nearly 58% in 2024-25, and that 52 million tonnes of capacity changed hands in FY2024-25 alone.[4] The buyers were the giants: UltraTech added 26.3 MTPA through the India Cements and Kesoram acquisitions on top of 16.3 MTPA built organically,[1] and Ambuja completed the Orient Cement acquisition in April 2025, taking it past 100 MTPA.[2]

The logic is the framework itself: an acquired plant inside your freight map is worth more than the same plant outside it. The acquirer re-optimises lead distances, procurement, fuel contracts and brand across a bigger network. Buying capacity can beat building it when the quarry licences and land are already in place, which is why consolidation and the entry barrier are the same story told twice.

8. Does scale guarantee returns?

No, and FY2024-25 supplies the proof at the largest possible scale. UltraTech, the biggest cement company in the world outside China by single-country capacity, reported PAT declining 10% to Rs 6,193 crore even as it added 42.6 MTPA and crossed Rs 75,955 crore of net revenue.[1] Scale buys procurement power, freight optimisation and survival through downturns; it does not exempt anyone from the pricing cycle.

Scale done well is still a choice about returns. Shree Cement, at 56.4 MTPA, held EBITDA at Rs 4,414 crore on revenue of Rs 18,037 crore with PAT of Rs 1,196 crore, a steadier year built on regional strength rather than national breadth.[3] The lesson for the tracking sheet: record scale as a cost advantage to verify, not a moat to assume. The verification is in lead distance, fuel cost per tonne and capacity utilisation, not in the MTPA headline.

9. What do blended cements change?

Everything that matters to the cost line. Portland slag and pozzolana cements replace part of the clinker with fly ash or slag, cutting cost per tonne, quarry consumption and emissions at once. Shree Cement's report emphasises blended cement production across PPC, PSC and composite cements.[3] Ambuja's 60 BCFC rakes exist precisely to secure fly ash from power plants.[2] A higher blended share means less clinker per tonne of cement, which means more cement from the same kiln.

Blending also has a market side: some applications and regions accept blended cement readily, others resist, so the blended share is capped by demand as well as ash supply. When a company reports its blended ratio, read it as three statements at once: cost position, carbon position and how much headroom remains to stretch clinker capacity.

10. How does demand reach the plant?

Through housing, infrastructure and individual home builders, routed by dealers and retailers within each region. Demand is seasonal, with monsoon quarters weak, and it is local, because the buyer chooses from whichever brands the local dealer stocks. The industry's FY2024-25 volumes grew, but the consolidation data tells the deeper story: the big four grew share from 48% to 58% in three years, meaning smaller and regional players lost ground even in a growing market.[4]

For a single company, demand questions are therefore regional questions. Which states does it sell into, what is its share there, and how much unutilised competitor capacity sits within the same freight radius? A plant in a crowded region faces the pricing of everyone else's spare tonnes, whatever the national demand growth says.

11. What does pricing look like in practice?

Regional, opaque and mean-reverting. Prices differ state by state because freight isolates markets, and they move with local capacity utilisation rather than national headlines. The FY2024-25 pattern, weak pricing alongside record capacity additions, is the classic cement trap: everyone builds for the same demand, and the price clears at the marginal plant's pain point. The year's profit declines tell it in numbers: UltraTech's PAT fell 10%, and Dalmia's EBITDA fell to Rs 2,407 crore with net profit reduced from Rs 853 crore the year before.[4][1]

Since list prices are hard to verify, study the revealed price: net sales realisation per tonne, reported in the segment disclosures, and its movement against cost per tonne. The gap between those two lines is the business, compressed to two numbers.

12. What can go wrong?

Overbuilding is the industry's own risk. Every company in this article is expanding, UltraTech to over 192 MTPA, Ambuja past 100 MTPA, Dalmia targeting 110 to 130 MTPA, Shree to 62.8 MTPA.[1][2][4][3] If demand growth disappoints, the same reports that celebrate capacity will explain price weakness. FY2024-25's profit declines at the two largest acquirers are an early illustration, not a verdict.

The external risks sit in the cost lines: coal and pet coke prices priced in dollars, diesel and rail tariffs in freight, limestone royalty and mining regulation at the quarry, and power costs at the grinder.[1] Each can reverse a year's cost gains in a quarter. And acquisitions carry integration risk: UltraTech's India Cements and Kesoram tonnes must be re-logisticised to earn their price, which is a multi-year project, not a closing-date fact.

13. What should the tracking sheet explain?

My tracking sheet for a cement company would answer, with dates: How much cement and clinker capacity exists, by region, and what was added or acquired this year? What is the lead distance, and which way is it moving? What are realisation per tonne and cost per tonne, and which of fuel, freight, raw material and power moved the cost line? What share of cement is blended, and what ash or slag supply secures it? What utilisation does each region run at, and whose spare capacity competes inside the same radius? What does each announced expansion cost, when does it commission, and what incremental return does it need? What did acquisitions add, and what is the integration plan worth? And what does the balance sheet allow the company to survive?

None of this requires predicting cement prices. It records whether the business is understood well enough that a pricing downturn, a fuel spike or a slow integration would be noticed and read correctly. That record is the real output of the study.
