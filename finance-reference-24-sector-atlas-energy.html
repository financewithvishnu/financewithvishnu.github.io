Chapter 24. Sector Atlas: Energy: Oil, Gas and Power

Chapter 24 of 85 · about 21 minutes

Energy is the sector where India reads its own constraints: we import most of our crude, our power distributors lose money by design, and our largest energy companies answer to two masters, shareholders and the state. It is also where the energy transition is writing the next decade's biggest capital-allocation story. Read this sector and you stop being surprised by half the market's most violent single-stock moves.

24.1How is one oil stock three businesses: upstream, refining and marketing?

A large Indian oil company is usually three companies stapled together, and they respond to crude in opposite directions. Upstream, exploration and production, sells crude: profits rise with oil prices, minus the windfall taxes the government levies exactly when prices run. Refining buys crude and sells fuels: its profit is the margin between product and crude prices, the gross refining margin, which widens when crude is volatile and product demand is tight, and can compress to nothing in gluts. Marketing, the petrol pumps, sells at prices the government watches closely. The analytical error to avoid: treating "oil company" as a bet on oil. Depending on the mix, the same stock can be long crude, short crude, or hedged by construction. Read the segment profits first; the commodity view comes second.

24.2How do administered fuel prices affect energy companies?

India's fuel retail prices are market-linked on paper and administered in practice, especially near elections. When crude spikes and pump prices freeze, the oil marketing companies absorb the difference: the famous "under-recovery" problem, which has consumed entire years of sector profits in the past. The investor's translation: a state-controlled OMC is partly a policy instrument, and its valuation must carry a permanent governance discount for the days the government needs the pump price to be a political price. The pattern is stable enough to be a rule: OMC margins expand when crude falls and freeze when crude spikes, which makes their earnings a leveraged inverse bet on oil, and their stocks behave accordingly.

24.3Why does India’s power chain break at the DISCOMs?

India's power chain breaks at the last link. Generators produce efficiently, the national grid transmits reliably, and then the state-owned distribution companies, the DISCOMs, sell power below cost to politically sensitive consumers and lose money on every unit, accumulating losses that have required repeated bailouts. Everything in the sector prices off this fact: generators demand payment security mechanisms and letters of credit; equipment and smart-metering companies sell into bailout-conditioned reform schemes; and the private distribution franchises that actually collect their bills are valued as rare assets. When you analyse any power-sector company, the first question is always: who pays the bill, and how certain is the payment? In Indian power, revenue is an opinion; collection is the fact.

24.4How do renewable energy projects make money?

Renewable energy in India is a contracted-cash-flow business wrapped in a growth story. Projects win capacity in auctions at fixed tariffs, the winning bids having fallen from double digits to the ₹2-3 per unit range over a decade, then run for 25 years with near-zero fuel cost. The economics live in the financing: a project is mostly debt, so the equity return is the spread between the project's return and the cost of that debt, magnified by leverage; at competitive tariffs, equity IRRs land in the low-to-mid teens for disciplined developers. The risks are equally structured: aggressive bidders winning at tariffs that assume perfect execution, payment delays from the same DISCOMs, curtailment when the grid cannot absorb supply, and module-price cycles. The sector's investment question is never "is solar the future?", it obviously is, but "at this tariff, with this financing, from this developer, what is left for equity?"

24.5Why must regulated and commodity energy be valued differently?

Energy stocks divide into two valuation species. Commodity energy, upstream producers, refiners, marketers, earns volatile spreads and deserves low multiples of mid-cycle earnings: the Chapter 11 cyclical rule applies with full force, and the cheapest-looking P/E will reliably mark the commodity top. Regulated and contracted energy, transmission utilities earning a regulated return on equity, generators with fixed-tariff contracts, renewable developers with 25-year agreements, is a bond with a growth option: value it on the spread between its contracted return and its cost of capital, the durability of the regulator's promise, and the pipeline of similar projects it can add. Confusing the two species, paying a utility multiple for commodity earnings or a commodity multiple for contracted cash flows, is the sector's classic valuation error, and it is made every single cycle.

24.6Worked examples and cases

Case 1: an OMC's margins versus crude

The chart above is the relationship in miniature: crude climbs from $75 toward $98 and the marketing margin index sinks from 110 toward 78, because pump prices lag and politics resist; crude then falls and margins bloom past the starting point, because pump prices lag downward too, and nobody complains. The tradable version of this knowledge, which the market applies every cycle: OMCs are bought when crude is high and falling, sold when crude is low and spiking, the exact opposite of the naive commodity logic. The deeper lesson generalises to every administered price in the market: when the state sets your selling price, your stock is a derivative on policy, and the analysis to do is political-economy, not geology.

Case 2: a renewable developer's project-level returns

One 100 MW solar project, illustrative numbers: ₹450 crore of project cost, a ₹2.60 per unit tariff for 25 years, about 19 crore units a year, roughly ₹50 crore of revenue and ₹44 crore of EBITDA after trivial operating costs. Funded 75% by debt at 9%, the ₹112 crore of equity earns a mid-teens IRR, steady, contracted, and boring by design. Now break it, as the real world does: shave the tariff to ₹2.40 (a more aggressive bid), add a year of DISCOM payment delays carrying working-capital cost, and let modules arrive 10% over budget, and the equity IRR slides toward single digits. At the sector's scale, thousands of these projects, the difference between a 14% and a 9% equity outcome is the difference between a compounding developer and a utility wearing a growth costume. The project maths is public; the discipline is doing it before the market does.
