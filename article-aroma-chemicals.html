Aroma chemicals: the molecule, the formula and the cash

By Finance With Vishnu · Source period: April 2025

A fragrance is easy to notice and difficult to describe precisely. For a supplier, the business question is more specific: what must it do repeatedly so that a customer receives the same performance, quality and experience?

There are several businesses inside that question. One company processes raw materials. Another makes an ingredient. A third develops a formulation for a soap, detergent or perfume. The consumer brand then turns that formulation into a product and a relationship with its own customer.

I would not put all these businesses into one basket called specialty chemicals. Their selling process, assets, working capital and ability to recover input inflation can be very different.

1. What does an aroma ingredient actually do?

Aroma ingredients help create the sensory character of products. They can contribute to the smell of a perfume or household product and to flavour experiences in foods and beverages. The application and permitted use matter, so ingredients should not be treated as interchangeable across these categories.

IFRA describes fragrance creation as combining natural materials, synthetic molecules and a perfumer's formulation work. Natural materials may be extracted or distilled from flowers, woods, resins, spices or citrus peels. Synthetic materials expand the available palette. [1]

The distinction is not that natural automatically means safe or synthetic automatically means inferior. The business must deliver the right material for the right use, with suitable specifications, evidence and controls.

For an investor, the useful first question is whether the company sells a material or a finished formulation. A familiar end use does not tell us which part of the economics the listed supplier actually earns.

A perfume brand's shelf price is therefore not a sensible proxy for the price or profit of any one ingredient inside it.

2. Where does the value chain split?

The starting material may be agricultural, forestry-related or petrochemical. Ingredient manufacturers process those inputs into materials that a formulator can use. Formulators combine ingredients to meet a customer's brief, while the consumer company manages the final branded product.

Givaudan's 2024 report describes Taste & Wellbeing and Fragrance & Beauty as distinct business activities and emphasises collaboration with customers. Symrise's 2024 report maps sourcing, development, processing and consumer-product applications. [2, 3]

Those maps are more useful than a single industry growth rate. A company can supply a large end market while remaining exposed to a narrow group of molecules, customers or raw materials.

3. What can make an ingredient manufacturer different?

Process knowledge, yield, purity, consistent output and dependable supply can matter. Access to suitable feedstock and the ability to handle it efficiently may be another source of advantage.

Privi's FY2025 annual report describes its aroma-chemical portfolio, crude sulphate turpentine processing and its PRIGIV venture with Givaudan. These are different capabilities from simply buying a catalogue of ingredients and mixing them. [4]

I would ask what competitors would need to replicate: a piece of equipment, an integrated process, a supply arrangement, a customer qualification or a combination of these. Installing a similar vessel does not necessarily produce the same commercially accepted material.

But technical difficulty does not create unlimited pricing power. Customers can negotiate, alternative routes may improve and the economics of competing feedstocks can change.

The test is whether an advantage appears in reliable deliveries, cash margins and returns through different conditions. A company description that uses the word specialty is a starting point for research, not proof of an enduring moat.

4. Why distinguish gum turpentine from crude sulphate turpentine?

Gum turpentine oil, or GTO, and crude sulphate turpentine, or CST, are different feedstock streams. CST is associated with wood-pulp processing and requires suitable handling and processing to turn it into useful inputs. The US EPA's technical background describes its recovery during kraft pulping and sale as a raw material. That background is used here for the process distinction, not for a current waste-classification opinion. [8]

Privi's report describes both GTO and CST capacity in its development history and a current CST-processing capability. It also discusses a globally sourced pulp-mill network. [4]

A lower-cost starting material can be attractive, but the relevant comparison is cost per unit of accepted output. Freight, processing, impurities, yield, energy, waste treatment and capital all belong in that calculation.

Consider a hypothetical feedstock costing ₹60 that yields 0.6 units of saleable output. Its feedstock cost is ₹100 per saleable unit before processing. Another costs ₹80 and yields 0.9 units, or about ₹88.9 per saleable unit. The more expensive raw input can be cheaper on the correct basis.

I would not assume that a by-product is free or that one route always wins. Supply contracts and the full conversion process determine the result.

5. Why can formulation create a different kind of stickiness?

A formulator is trying to meet a sensory and functional brief, not merely supply a named molecule. The result must work within the customer's product, process, cost and safety requirements.

IFRA describes perfumers using a broad palette, supported by laboratory analysis, before formulas are reproduced through controlled blending. It also describes brand owners and fragrance houses working together to match scent performance to product function. [1]

If a formulation performs well, changing it can require development, testing and commercial decisions. That can create friction around switching, but it is not a promise that the customer will never move or that every formulation has identical protection.

I would examine repeat wins, retention, service quality, development productivity and the economics of maintaining customer relationships. The number of formulas in a database is not the same as the number generating revenue.

A supplier can also lose a brief before commercialisation after spending time and money on it. Development cost is part of the model, including the projects that do not win.

6. Does a small share of product cost guarantee pricing power?

No. A low-cost component may be important to the final product, which can make reliability valuable. It does not remove the purchasing department or make the supplier's price irrelevant.

The buyer may compare alternative suppliers, negotiate across a portfolio or allow price changes only at agreed intervals. Formula ownership, qualification requirements, competing products and the commercial terms all matter.

Suppose an ingredient costs ₹1 inside a hypothetical ₹100 consumer product. A 20% ingredient-price increase adds ₹0.20 if everything else is unchanged. That may be small to the final product, yet a large buyer still has a reason to negotiate it across millions of units.

The ingredient supplier also faces its own input basket. Its selling-price increase may be smaller than the cost increase or arrive later.

I would treat low cost-in-use as one source of bargaining support, then test actual recovery through gross-profit rupees, selling-price changes and customer retention. It is not evidence that tariffs, currency movements or raw-material inflation have no effect.

7. What does a pass-through delay do to profit?

A supplier may be able to recover input inflation eventually and still suffer a difficult quarter. The delay between purchasing more expensive material and receiving a higher selling price creates a cash and earnings problem.

In SH Kelkar's May 2025 call, management discussed supply constraints, elevated input costs and price increases expected to benefit margins gradually. The timing qualification is important. [5]

Profit per unit recovers in this illustration, but the intervening loss is real. The new percentage margin also differs from the original one. To assess recovery, compare both rupees and percentages and separate price, volume and mix.

8. Can sourcing security create inventory risk?

Yes. Holding more material can protect deliveries during disruption. It can also tie up cash and leave the company carrying expensive stock when replacement prices fall.

A lower market price for a raw material does not instantly mean the company is producing from cheaper inputs. Existing purchases still move through inventory according to the business's accounting and operating cycle.

I would examine raw-material days, work in progress, finished goods, receivables and supplier credit. Aggregate inventory alone can hide whether the build-up is deliberate supply protection, a new-product ramp or unsold finished output.

SH Kelkar's May 2025 call also links working capital to operating across multiple locations after the fire disruption. That is a different cause of elevated stock from simply expecting raw-material prices to rise. [5]

The useful question is how much inventory is required to serve customers reliably and what could release the cash. A management explanation should be checked against ageing, cash flow and subsequent reductions, rather than used to ignore the balance sheet.

9. Is customer approval the same as an order book?

No. Being approved can make a supplier eligible for business. It does not necessarily fix the customer's purchase volume, the start date, the price or the duration of demand.

This distinction is particularly important in SH Kelkar's May 2025 call. Management said it did not have many long-term contracts when discussing its growth outlook. It referred instead to client behaviour, existing momentum and the need for new business wins. [5]

I would separate vendor approval, a selected formulation, commercial discussions, purchase orders and invoiced sales. A strong pipeline has value, but each stage carries a different degree of certainty.

Privi's FY2025 report describes PRIGIV as a joint venture manufacturing specialised fragrance ingredients exclusively for Givaudan. That is a specific arrangement, not proof that every product sold by every aroma supplier has the same commercial protection. [4]

Even exclusivity requires reading the economics: who owns the assets, who funds working capital, how pricing works and what happens if actual demand differs from the plan.

10. How should development centres and new capacity be judged?

They solve different problems. A development centre helps the company work on briefs and customer relationships. A plant supplies output. One can exist without enough business for the other.

SH Kelkar's May 2025 call discusses development activity in Germany and planned investment in Manchester and New Jersey. Management connects these to future geographical growth, not instant full utilisation. [5]

Staff, premises and project work may cost money before the resulting revenue arrives. That does not mean those costs should disappear from an investor's assessment. Some spending may be needed repeatedly to sustain growth.

I would compare reported results with management's adjusted view, understand each exclusion and ask whether a supposedly temporary cost keeps returning. For a new plant, the test includes commissioning, qualification, yield, customer uptake and capital tied up before maturity.

A capacity announcement is not sales. A development pipeline is not profit. Both need a bridge from money spent to accepted products, orders, cash and an adequate return on capital.

11. Why are company-wide labels dangerous?

A company can combine ingredient manufacturing, formulation, camphor and other activities. A single label can hide the part that drives its reported margin or absorbs its capital.

Oriental Aromatics' FY2025 annual report discusses flavours and fragrances, aroma chemicals and camphor, as well as several manufacturing sites and chemistries. It should not be reduced to a pure camphor-price bet without examining the actual business mix. [6]

Likewise, a global formulator's group figures may include activities outside the specific aroma ingredients being compared. Symrise's 2024 report describes a broad product and application base. [3]

I would compare the relevant segment first and then reconcile it with group results. Product mix, geography, acquisitions, internal sales and accounting definitions can all distort a quick margin comparison.

Givaudan's 2024 performance report separately presents like-for-like growth and Swiss-franc sales growth. That is another reminder to distinguish operating progress from the currency and reporting effects that change the number on the first page. [2]

12. What can interrupt otherwise attractive economics?

Supply disruption, fire, environmental obligations, quality failures and customer concentration can all matter. An attractive formula does not prevent a plant from stopping or a customer from delaying a launch.

SH Kelkar's May 2025 call discusses rebuilding its Vashivali facility, operating through alternative locations and insurance receipts still being processed. An insurance claim should not be confused with immediate cash or uninterrupted operations. [5]

For safety, IFRA describes its Standards as a risk-management system that can restrict or prohibit fragrance materials where needed. It also states that final responsibility for placing safe products on the market rests with companies. These Standards are not a substitute for all applicable laws or application-specific checks. [7]

I would assess site concentration, backup production, insurance scope, customer audit history, compliance costs and the financial room to withstand disruption. Natural sourcing also brings crop and geographical risks; synthetic sourcing has its own feedstock and processing exposures.

A business deserves credit for preparedness, not an assumption that every interruption will be brief or fully reimbursed.

13. What would make the returns worth studying?

Operating margin and capital productivity need to work together. An attractive margin can be offset by slow-moving stock, a large new plant or expensive customer-development efforts.

The example uses a simplified pre-tax operating return, not a universal reported ROCE definition. It shows why working capital and new investment belong beside the profit margin.

The scent may be memorable. The investment case still needs repeatable economics. I would prefer evidence of useful capabilities and cash generation to a broad claim that every aroma supplier is a protected compounder.
