Chapter 13. IPOs and New Listings in India

Chapter 13 of 85 · about 20 minutes

Every few years India rediscovers the IPO, applies for everything in sight, celebrates listing-day pops, and then holds the leftovers for years. The pattern is old enough to have a lesson inside it: an IPO is a sale, and you are the buyer. The seller chooses the timing, the story and, within a band, the price. This chapter teaches you to stand on the right side of that counter: to read what an offer document actually says, to price the business rather than the excitement, and to know when applying is a strategy and when it is a lottery ticket.

13.1Why do companies go public, and what is the difference between primary and secondary markets?

Everything you bought in Chapter 12 was the secondary market: shares passing between investors, with the company untouched. An IPO is the primary market: new shares created, or existing shareholders selling, with the money going to the company or to those sellers. The split matters because it tells you whose interest the price serves. A fresh-issue-heavy IPO funds the company's plans; an offer-for-sale-heavy IPO is insiders cashing out, which is not automatically bad, mature businesses sell down for sound reasons, but it changes the question from "what will my money build?" to "why do the people who know this business best want less of it at this price?". Read the objects-of-the-issue section first; it is the one part of an IPO written for you rather than about the seller.

13.2How does the IPO process work from DRHP to listing?

The company files a draft red herring prospectus with SEBI, markets the issue to investors, discovers the price through book building, allots the shares, and lists on the exchanges, a journey of a few months end to end.

Three features deserve attention. The DRHP, the draft red herring prospectus, is public the moment it is filed, months before the marketing begins; the patient reader prices the company before the roadshow tells anyone what to think. Anchor investors commit a day early and their names signal institutional appetite, but they are also guests at a dinner the bankers arranged. And ASBA, applications supported by blocked amount, means your bid money stays in your own bank account, merely blocked, until allotment: applying costs you no interest and no transfer risk, which is exactly why the system can absorb the enormous oversubscriptions you read about.

13.3Which sections of an IPO offer document actually matter?

An RHP runs 400-600 pages. Read it in this order and you extract 90% of the value in an evening:

OrderSectionWhat you are hunting
1Objects of the issueWhere the money goes; fresh issue vs offer for sale
2Risk factorsThe first ten are boilerplate; the specific ones, a customer who is 40% of revenue, a pending case, are the document's confessions
3Financial statements + restated adjustmentsFive years of the Chapter 8 and 9 toolkit; restatements show what the first draft of history hid
4Related-party transactionsChapter 10's map, drawn before listing
5Management and promoter historyTrack record, other ventures, past failures and disputes
6Industry sectionUseful for structure, but remember the company paid for the report

The offer document is the one moment a company must answer, in writing, under liability, questions it will never again answer so completely. Investors who skip it are buying a product whose label they declined to read.

13.4How are IPOs priced, and why do they pop on listing day?

IPOs are sold, not discovered: bankers price to leave enough on the table for a listing pop that validates the next deal, but not more. The result is a systematic pattern visible across decades and countries: average first-day gains, weak long-run performance relative to priced-in expectations. The Indian version adds two twists. Retail allotment in oversubscribed issues is by lottery, so a sensible application is a cheap option on the pop, not a way to build a position; a ₹15,000 allotment that doubles is ₹15,000, not a retirement. And grey-market premium, the unofficial pre-listing price, is quoted everywhere and regulated nowhere; treat it as weather, not climate. The discipline that survives every IPO season: price the business with Chapters 9-11 and 15-16 before you look at the band, decide what you would pay as a five-year owner, and let listing-day noise happen to other people.

13.5What are the special risks of SME IPOs?

The SME platforms of the NSE and BSE list smaller companies with lighter requirements: smaller issue sizes, larger minimum lots, thinner disclosure, and, historically, thinner scrutiny. The segment has produced genuine multibaggers and an industrial quantity of manipulation: tiny free floats that make prices easy to paint, circular trading to manufacture the oversubscription headline, and auditor and merchant-banker ecosystems of uneven quality. SEBI has repeatedly tightened the segment, profitability track records, caps on offer-for-sale, migration rules to the main board, because the pattern kept recurring. The rules that keep you safe are the same ones, sharpened: the float is the liquidity, so check what fraction actually trades; the Chapter 10 forensic checklist applies with half the forgiveness; and a price that can be painted by a small operator is not a price you may rely on for exit.

13.6Worked examples and cases

Case 1: dissecting a mainboard IPO, the 2021-pattern

Take the shape of a large 2021-era consumer-tech IPO, composite of several real ones, numbers illustrative: a ₹9,000 crore issue, nearly all offer-for-sale, a loss-making company priced at a rich multiple of revenue, anchors filled with famous funds, and a story told in gross-merchandise-value. The disciplined read: objects of the issue show insiders exiting, not capacity being built; the financials show losses funded by successive private rounds, each pricing the next; the valuation question is not "is the app good?" but "what must margins and growth be for this price to be ordinary in five years?". Listing day delivers the pop on some, the thud on others; three years later the survivors are those where the five-year arithmetic had any chance at all. The document told you which was which, at leisure, months in advance.

Case 2: an SME listing that collapsed

The recurring script, again composite: a ₹40 crore SME issue in a fashionable niche, subscribed a hundred times on the final day after a week of grey-market excitement, a 90% listing pop, and then, within a year, a 70% fall as results fail to resemble the prospectus and the float turns out to be controlled by a handful of connected holders. Every element was visible beforehand: a two-year-old auditor, related-party sales forming a third of revenue, promoter history including a struck-off company, and a free float so small that twenty investors could set the price. None of it required genius, only the refusal to confuse oversubscription with endorsement. A crowd bidding for an allotment is evidence of a crowd bidding for an allotment.
