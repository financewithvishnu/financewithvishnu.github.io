Chapter 22. Sector Atlas: FMCG, Retail and Consumption

Chapter 22 of 85 · about 21 minutes

Somewhere in India, right now, a shopkeeper is restocking a soap he has restocked every week for thirty years. Multiply that act by ten million outlets and you have the most durable business model in the Indian market: small-ticket, high-frequency, habit-driven purchases, pulled through the deepest distribution network ever built in this country. FMCG is where moats are manufactured. It is also where investors overpay for comfort most reliably. This chapter gives you both sides.

22.1Why are distribution and brand the twin moat of FMCG companies?

Chapter 11's moat kinds reach their Indian purest form here. Distribution: the leading companies reach millions of retail outlets directly or through wholesalers, and every new product they launch rides that network at near-zero marginal distribution cost, a scale economy a challenger cannot buy at any price quickly. Brand: in low-involvement purchases, the remembered name is the product, because nobody comparison-shops soap. The two reinforce: distribution makes the brand available, brand makes the distribution productive. The test is always pricing power: can the company pass on cost inflation within two quarters without losing share? The leaders can; the rest discover their "brand" was only availability.

22.2How do you read volume versus price growth, and rural versus urban demand?

Decompose every FMCG quarter into volume and price. Volume growth is the honest engine: more units bought by more households, tracking penetration, population and incomes. Price growth is the borrowed engine: necessary in inflation years, but it mortgages volume when pushed too far, because the category's customers are price-sensitive by definition. The second decomposition is rural versus urban: rural India, the majority of the market for staples, runs on monsoon, crop prices and government transfers, and its slowdowns show up in FMCG volumes two quarters after the farm economy turns. Urban demand runs on wages and confidence and recovers differently. A company growing volume in a weak rural year is taking share; one growing only price in a strong year is spending its moat.

22.3How do input costs swing FMCG margins?

FMCG margins ride a seesaw between commodity inputs, palm oil, crude derivatives, wheat, milk, and the company's three responses: raise prices, shrink the pack at the same price (grammage, the industry's favourite invisible price hike), and time advertising spend. The pattern across cycles is remarkably stable: input inflation compresses margins for two to three quarters, the leaders then recover them through the three levers, and emerge with higher prices and intact volumes. Margin troughs in this sector have historically been buying opportunities in the leaders, precisely because the recovery is mechanical. 22.4How is quick commerce changing FMCG distribution?

The deepest change in Indian consumption in a generation is a logistics change: ten-to-thirty-minute delivery has moved a growing slice of urban FMCG from the kirana shelf to the app, and with it bargaining power, data and shelf placement. For brands, quick commerce is simultaneously a new high-growth channel, a threat to the moat (an app shows the challenger next to the leader at the same scroll), and a data source the old distribution never offered. For the platforms themselves, the economics are a public experiment in contribution-margin discipline: dark stores, delivery costs, take rates, and a race to prove the model earns before the capital runs out. Watch the unit economics, not the headline GMV; Chapter 11's multiplication applies here unchanged.

22.5Why do consumer stocks carry premium valuations?

Indian FMCG leaders have traded at 40-60x earnings for years, and the premium is not madness, it is a specific bet, four layers deep: earnings visibility (habits rarely lapse), pricing power (the moat test, passed for decades), capital-light compounding (almost all profit is free cash), and duration (the growth runway is a billion consumers premiumising for decades). Paying 50x is not automatically wrong; it is a purchase of those four certainties at a price that requires most of them to keep holding. The risk is rarely operational; it is arithmetic: when growth slows from 12% to 6%, the same certainty commands 35x, and a "safe" stock delivers a de-rating that feels like a crash. In consumption, the moat protects the business; it has never protected the entry price.

22.6Worked examples and cases

Case 1: a staple's pricing power through an inflation year

Composite of the leaders' real playbook: input costs jump 30% over two quarters. The company's response, in order: first absorb, letting margins compress visibly; then take a series of 2-4% price hikes, timed across regions so competitors follow; simultaneously reduce pack grammage on the price-point packs, the ₹5 and ₹10 units where the printed price cannot move; and cut advertising for two quarters, restoring it as margins recover. Four quarters later: volumes roughly intact, market share up because smaller rivals could not follow the hikes, margins within a point of where they began, and the revenue base permanently higher. That sequence is pricing power made visible, and it is why the moat test from Chapter 11, can it raise prices and keep volume, is answered with history in this sector rather than hope.

Case 2: a new-age retail P&L, read honestly

Composite of listed new-age retail: revenue doubling, contribution margin positive per order after discounts, and a widening net loss. The honest decomposition: the order-level economics work (each order contributes after picking and delivery); the losses are network expansion, new dark stores, new cities, new customers bought with discounts. The two questions that decide the investment: does the mature-city P&L, disclosed or estimable, show the model earning at scale, and does the cash balance fund the expansion path without dilution that changes your share of the outcome? If both answers are yes, the losses are capex in disguise; if either is no, the growth is being purchased with your ownership. The market has priced both answers at different times for the same companies; the P&L, decomposed, always knew which was true.
