Chapter 61. Digital Investing: Platforms, Robo-Advisors and Fractional Ownership

Chapter 61 of 85 · about 19 minutes

The discount broker did to investing what UPI did to payments: collapsed the friction, exploded the volumes, and moved the profit somewhere less visible. Crores of demat accounts later, the questions a digital investor must answer have changed shape. Not "can I trade?" but "what does free cost?", "who is advising me?", and "what exactly do I own?" This chapter answers them.

61.1How do discount brokers make money in the zero-commission world?

The discount broker's headline, zero brokerage on delivery, flat ₹20 on intraday and F&O, is honest and incomplete. The economics live elsewhere: the ₹20 on each side of each F&O order, multiplied by the crores of orders the model encourages; interest on the idle cash in crores of trading accounts; margin funding at 10-18%; and the cross-sell, mutual funds, loans, insurance, subscriptions. Nothing here is sinister; it is a supermarket that gives away the bread. But the incentive structure matters to you: a broker paid per trade benefits from your activity, not your returns, and every feature, one-tap F&O, price alerts, streaks, leaderboards, should be read against that incentive. The older full-service model charged visible hundreds and offered research; the discount model charges invisible twenties and offers velocity. Neither is free. The only question is whether the price buys you something you wanted.

61.2What are robo-advisors and goal-based automated portfolios?

A robo-advisor automates the quiet disciplines: a risk questionnaire maps you to a model portfolio of index funds or ETFs, contributions run on schedule, rebalancing happens by rule, and some platforms harvest tax losses along the way. The value proposition is real and specific: most investors' biggest enemy is their own behaviour, the panic sale, the performance chase, and a machine that rebalances without fear closes the gap between the market's return and the investor's. The limits are equally specific. The algorithms are not mysterious, they are Chapter 34's portfolio theory with a rules engine, and a plain target-date style fund or a two-fund portfolio plus an annual calendar reminder replicates most of the benefit at lower cost. In India, the category has converged toward goal-based advisory layered on mutual fund distribution, useful when the advice is genuinely independent and fee-based, worth suspicion when the "advice" arrives with a commission embedded in a regular plan. Ask every robo two questions: what do you earn when I follow your advice, and what would you earn if I bought the direct plan instead?

61.3What is fractional ownership of property, art and unlisted shares?

Fractional platforms slice expensive assets into ₹10-25 lakh tickets: a share of a grade-A office, a fraction of a painting, a piece of a pre-IPO company. The appeal is access; the diligence burden is the price of admission, and it is heavier than any listed security because the disclosure is thinner and the exit is a promise, not a market. For property, SEBI's small-and-medium REIT framework has begun pulling the legitimate end of the business into regulation, with trust structures, valuation and distribution rules; anything fractional outside that perimeter deserves the question "which regulator watches this?" For art and collectibles, add authenticity, custody and a resale market that may not exist when you want it. For unlisted shares, the grey market prices pre-IPO companies with no exchange, no price discovery and no guarantee the IPO arrives, and the same share often trades at different prices on different platforms on the same day, a market where the spread is the first fact. Fractional ownership is not a scam and not a free lunch; it is private-market investing offered retail-sized, and it demands private-market diligence at retail scale.

61.4What are the risks of social trading and finfluencers?

Every generation gets its stock tips; ours gets them in sixty-second videos with a Discord link. The structure of the risk is worth seeing clearly. The finfluencer's business model is attention, monetised through courses, affiliate links, brokerage referrals and sometimes the exit liquidity of their own followers, so the content optimises for engagement, not accuracy, and boldness outperforms correctness in the attention market. The track record is unverifiable: no audited statements, no benchmark, survivorship by deletion, losing calls quietly removed. And regulation has caught up in part: SEBI has acted against unregistered investment advisors and barred regulated entities from associating with unregistered finfluencers for advice, drawing the legal line at personalised recommendations for compensation. The consumer rules are old ones in new clothes. Entertainment and education are fine inputs; a buy list is not advice unless the adviser is registered and accountable. Anyone who profits when you trade is a salesperson, whatever the video's tone. And "I made 40% last year" from someone selling a course is an advertisement, not a result.

61.5How does gamification change investor behaviour?

The deepest cost of digital investing is not a fee; it is a behaviour. The app's confetti on your first trade, the streak for daily logins, the push notification that a stock you glanced at is "up 4% today", the options chain one thumb-flick from the home screen: each is a design pattern imported from gaming and social media, where the goal is engagement, and engagement in a brokerage app means trades. The evidence is Chapter 47's statistic: the overwhelming majority of retail F&O traders lose, and the median active trader's returns trail the market by the width of their own activity. The defence is architectural, not willpower. Turn off the notifications; the market will not miss you. Move long-term investing to a separate, dull app with no derivatives tab. Institute a 48-hour rule between the urge to trade and the trade. And count your own numbers annually: total return of the account against the Nifty, including every fee, because the platform that profits from your activity will never show you that comparison itself.

61.6Worked examples and cases

Case 1: what zero brokerage actually costs

Meet the "free" account of an active trader: delivery investing at zero brokerage, and a daily habit of ten F&O round trades, each side at the flat ₹20. The brokerage ledger alone: ₹20 × 2 sides × 10 trades × 20 trading days = ₹8,000 a month, nearly ₹1 lakh a year, before the statutory charges, STT, exchange fees and stamp duty that scale with turnover, and before the bid-ask spread, the largest invisible cost of frequent trading. On a ₹5 lakh trading capital, the friction alone can consume 20-30% of capital a year; the strategy must beat the market by that much merely to stand still. The comparison the broker never surfaces: the same ₹5 lakh in an index fund costs perhaps 0.2% a year and requires doing nothing, the one activity the app's entire design discourages. Zero brokerage is real; it is the price of one product, delivery, chosen because it is the one that makes you inactive. The business model is everything else you might do once you are inside.

Case 2: a fractional-property offering under the microscope

The pitch: ₹25 lakh buys 10% of a ₹2.5 crore leased office, "8% yield, monthly payouts, professional management". Diligence, line by line. The 8% is gross: subtract the platform's 1-2% entry fee and ~1% annual management, and the first-year net is closer to 5%, before tax at your slab. The lease is to one tenant in one building: vacancy is not a percentage point, it is a cliff, and a single-tenant office can go from 8% to zero on one non-renewal. The exit is a bulletin board: the platform offers a resale window, but there is no exchange, so your liquidity is the platform's marketing budget and other buyers' appetite, tested worst in a downturn. The legal wrapper matters: is this inside the SEBI small-REIT perimeter or a private LLP arrangement, and who holds the title? And the comparison is unforgiving: a listed REIT offers diversified rent-yielding property with exchange liquidity and SEBI oversight at a ₹300 ticket. The fractional offering can still deserve a slot, for an investor who has priced the illiquidity and wants the specific building, but it competes against a regulated alternative that solves most of the same problem with none of the structural frailty.
