Chapter 5. Budgeting, Emergency Funds and Financial Goals

Chapter 5 of 85 · about 15 minutes

Two colleagues earn the same ₹1,20,000 a month. Ten years later one owns a growing portfolio and sleeps well; the other has a car loan, three credit cards and a vague feeling of falling behind. The difference was never salary, luck or stock tips. It was a handful of unglamorous decisions made before the first investment was ever bought. This chapter is those decisions, in the order they must be made.

5.1Where does the surplus that funds all investing come from?

From first principles: every rupee you will ever invest comes from one number, income minus expenses. That surplus is the raw material of this website. A brilliant portfolio built on a weak surplus grows slowly; an ordinary portfolio built on a strong surplus wins.

The honest way to find your surplus is not to guess. Pull one month of bank and UPI statements and sort every outflow into three buckets: fixed (rent, EMIs, school fees), variable needs (groceries, fuel, utilities) and wants (eating out, subscriptions, impulse buys). Most people who do this once find a leak they did not know about, usually in the third bucket.

A useful starting grid is the 50/30/20 heuristic: about half of take-home pay for needs, about 30% for wants, at least 20% saved and invested. Treat it as a grid, not a law: a Bengaluru rent can break the first bucket on its own. The principle underneath it survives every city and salary: decide the saving first, on the day income arrives, and live on the rest. Saving what remains after spending reliably produces nothing, because nothing remains. Automating the transfer out on salary day is the whole trick.

5.2How much emergency fund do you need, and where should you keep it?

Before any investment comes a defence. The emergency fund exists so that a job loss, a medical bill or a broken car never forces you to sell investments at the wrong moment or borrow at 40% on a card. It is the matching principle from Chapter 3 applied to life: an unpredictable, possibly immediate need must be matched with an asset that is always there at full value.

  • How much: 3 to 6 months of essential expenses for a stable salaried job, 6 to 9 months if you are the single earner with dependents, up to 12 months for irregular freelance income. Essentials only: rent, food, EMIs, school fees, medicines. Not the comfortable life, the survivable one.
  • Where: liquidity and certainty beat return. A savings account, a sweep-in FD, or a liquid or overnight mutual fund. Never equity, never anything locked, never anything that can be down when you need it. The fund's return is not its growth; it is the crisis you never had.
  • How to keep it honest: a separate account, refilled immediately after any use. An emergency fund that slowly absorbs vacations is a vacation fund with a misleading name.

5.3What is goal-based financial planning?

Money without a deadline is money without a plan. Give every rupee a job and a date, and the choice of instrument mostly makes itself, because the horizon decides how much risk you can afford to take:

GoalHorizonWhat it needsSuitable home
Emergency fundAny dayInstant access, zero lossSavings account, sweep-in FD, liquid fund
Car, school fees, a tripUnder 3 yearsCertainty beats returnFDs, short-duration debt funds
House down payment3-7 yearsSome growth, limited swingsBlend of debt and conservative hybrid funds
Child's college, retirementOver 7 yearsReal growth after inflationEquity index and diversified equity funds

One adjustment never skipped: goals are priced in future rupees. A college seat that costs ₹10,00,000 today will cost about ₹15,90,000 in eight years at 6% inflation (10,00,000 x 1.06^8). Plan against the inflated target, or you will save diligently and still come up short.

5.4In what order should you insure, save and invest?

Insure your life and health first, build the emergency fund next, and only then invest for goals, because each step protects the one that follows it.

Personal finance has a correct sequence, and most regret comes from doing it out of order:

Earn
salary or business income lands
Protect
term life + health cover before anything else
Buffer
emergency fund of 3-12 months of expenses
Clear
kill high-interest debt first
Invest
goal SIPs, then long-term wealth

The order of operations. Each stage protects the next one; skipping a stage is how years of investing get undone by one bad month.

The logic is pure first principles. Insurance comes first because a single uninsured event can destroy every later stage at once. The emergency fund comes next because it stops a bad month from becoming a forced sale or a bad loan. High-interest debt comes before investing because clearing a 24% personal loan is a guaranteed, tax-free 24% return, and no honest investment promises that. Only then does investing get the full surplus it deserves.

5.5What is net worth, and why is it the one number to track?

Income is a flow; net worth is the stock it fills. Net worth is simply everything you own minus everything you owe, and it is the single number that tells you whether the machine is working:

Assets₹ valueLiabilities₹ value
Bank and FDs3,50,000Home loan balance32,00,000
EPF and PPF6,00,000Car loan balance3,00,000
Mutual funds and shares8,50,000Credit card dues40,000
Home (market value)55,00,000
Total assets73,00,000Total liabilities35,40,000
Net worth: assets minus liabilities37,60,000

Compute it once a quarter, on one page, with honest values. It grows only three ways: you save, your investments compound, or your debts shrink. In the first decade the saving does almost all the work, which is why the early chapters of wealth feel slow and why quitting early is the most expensive mistake.

5.6Worked examples and cases

Case 1: a 28-year-old salaried planner in Bengaluru

Take-home ₹1,20,000 a month, expenses ₹75,000 of which ₹50,000 are essentials, surplus ₹45,000. The plan, in order: a family health cover of ₹10,00,000 at roughly ₹18,000 a year; a term cover of ₹1,50,00,000 at roughly ₹15,000 a year (Chapter 6 shows the sizing); an emergency fund of 6 months of essentials, ₹3,00,000, built over the first 7 months into a sweep-in FD. From month 8, the full ₹45,000 splits into goals: ₹15,000 a month into a short-duration debt fund for a car in 4 years, ₹20,000 into an equity index fund for the long goals, ₹10,000 into PPF for the safe layer. No product here is clever. The order is the cleverness.

Case 2: a freelancer with irregular income

A designer averages ₹90,000 a month but the months swing from ₹30,000 to ₹2,00,000. A fixed ₹30,000 SIP would bounce in the thin months, so the rules change shape. Live on a fixed salary paid to yourself of ₹60,000, set at the level of a weak quarter. Everything above it flows into the buffer until it holds 12 months of expenses, ₹6,00,000. Saving is a percentage, not an amount: 30% of every receipt moves out the day it lands. SIPs run only from the buffer, never from the current month's invoice. Same principles as Case 1, re-fitted to lumpy cash flows: protection first, buffer sized to the risk, investing downstream of both.
