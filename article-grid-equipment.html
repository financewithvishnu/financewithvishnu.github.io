Grid equipment: the transformer, the smart meter and the tender

By Finance With Vishnu · Source period: 2024 value-chain studies · Exact class dates unconfirmed

Everyone studies where electricity is generated. Fewer study how it reaches anyone. Between the power plant and the plug point sits the grid: transformers that step voltage up and down, switchgear that protects the network, and now smart meters that count every unit at the consumer's end. Each is a different business wearing the same "power sector" label.

The distinction that organises the whole space is not the product but the contract. Selling a transformer is equipment supply: qualify, build, deliver, collect. Smart metering under government schemes is an AMISP concession: build, own, operate and get paid monthly for a decade. FY2024-25 gave both models a record year, and the annual reports show exactly where the money, and the risk, sits in each. Follow the transformer, the meter and the tender.

Study window: this is an original explanation of a 2024 value-chain framework; the exact class days are unconfirmed. FY2024-25 annual reports provide dated checks. They are not current data. This is education, not investment advice.

What does a grid equipment maker actually sell?

Start with what the grid physically is. Power leaves a plant at one voltage, travels far at a much higher voltage, and steps down in stages before it reaches a home. Every step needs a transformer; every node needs switchgear to isolate faults; long bulk transfers need HVDC links and FACTS devices to keep the system stable. The companies selling this hardware are really selling reliability: a transformer that fails takes a substation down with it, so buyers pay for proven design, testing and track record rather than the cheapest tonne of steel and copper.

GE Vernova T&D India describes FY2024-25 demand as customers "modernizing and investing in critical grid components", and it is investing more than Rs 2,200 million to expand manufacturing of exactly these technologies, including HVDC and FACTS.[1] That vocabulary matters: these are engineered systems with long qualification cycles, not catalogue products.

Why is a transformer not a commodity box?

A distribution transformer for a local feeder and a 765 kV autotransformer for the national grid are different businesses sharing a name. As voltage class rises, the number of qualified makers shrinks, design and testing dominate cost, and a single field failure can end a supplier's empanelment. TARIL manufactures power transformers up to 500 MVA and the 1,200 kV class, the top of India's voltage ladder.[2] The voltage class a company can credibly build is its real product range.

This is why grid equipment margins can widen in an upcycle instead of being competed away instantly. Capacity at the high-voltage end takes years to qualify, so when utilities order record volumes, the qualified few price the scarcity. TARIL's EBITDA margin reached 17.51% on a consolidated basis in FY2024-25, up from single digits in the downcycle years.[2] Commodity products do not behave that way; qualified ones do.

Where does the smart meter fit?

A smart meter looks like a small device but is sold as a large system: the meter itself, a communication network, head-end software, and integration with the utility's billing. Under the Revamped Distribution Sector Scheme, India is paying utilities to replace hundreds of millions of electromechanical meters, and it structured the rollout so that private players finance it. The meter is the visible tip; the business model underneath is a services concession.

Genus Power, an early and large player, describes itself as offering end-to-end smart metering: in-house meters, communication systems, and operation as an Advanced Metering Infrastructure Service Provider, an AMISP.[3] Reading "AMISP" as "meter manufacturer" is the most common mistake in this sector, and the next section is the correction.

How does equipment supply differ from an AMISP concession?

In an equipment sale, the maker delivers hardware, invoices on milestones, and moves on; the contract ends at commissioning and the customer owns the asset from day one. In an AMISP concession, the provider finances and installs the meters, owns or operates the system, and is paid a monthly service charge per meter over eight to ten years, conditional on the meters actually working: availability, accuracy and service levels decide the payment. One is a product business with a receivable; the other is an infrastructure annuity with an uptime obligation.

Genus's disclosures make the shape explicit: its order book, including SPVs and its platform with an investor, exceeds Rs 30,110 crore net of taxes, structured as concessions of eight to ten years.[3] That is not a sales pipeline. It is a decade of contracted service revenue whose quality depends on execution and on the utility paying its monthly bill.

Who pays, and when?

Every rupee in this sector ultimately comes from an electricity utility, and most Indian utilities are state-owned distribution companies with a history of weak finances. That single fact explains the sector's two recurring themes: equipment suppliers carry receivables and fight for advance and milestone terms, while the smart-metering programme was deliberately designed with payment-security mechanisms and direct debit structures so that AMISP annuities rank ahead of the utility's other cash demands.

The cycle matters as much as the credit. Record electricity demand, renewable integration and transmission investment drove FY2024-25 ordering across the sector.[1] When the grid investment cycle turns, it lifts equipment makers, meter concessionaires and their order books together, and it tests their working capital together too.

How does an equipment order become revenue?

An equipment award starts a long production cycle: engineering and design approval, type testing where required, procurement of core, copper and insulation, manufacturing, factory acceptance testing, delivery, erection and commissioning. Revenue is recognised along the way, but cash arrives on milestones, and retention money lingers until the equipment proves itself in service. A profitable order can still consume cash for most of its life.

The order book therefore has a tempo. TARIL's unexecuted orders of Rs 5,132 crore as of March 2025 will be executed over roughly 15 months, and its largest single order ever, Rs 740 crore from GETCO, arrived in March 2025.[2] One number tells you the stock of work; the other tells you the flow is still arriving. A healthy grid business needs both.

What does an order book really promise?

Grid companies advertise order books the way solar companies do, and the same decoding applies. GE Vernova T&D India's order backlog reached Rs 12,660 crore, up 102% in the year, with order inflows of Rs 10,780 crore.[1] TARIL's Rs 5,132 crore converts to roughly 15 months of work.[2] Genus's Rs 30,110 crore stretches across 8 to 10 years of meter-service concessions.[3] Same label, three different durations, margins and risks.

The questions that convert a headline into analysis: how much is domestic versus export, equipment versus service annuity, and government versus private? What margin was bid, and how much has already been won at peak pricing? And whose promise to pay stands behind each rupee: a strong utility, a weak one, or a payment-security mechanism?

What changed with the smart-metering scheme?

RDSS turned meter replacement from a utility purchase into a financed national programme, and the numbers arrived fast. Genus Power's revenue grew 103% to Rs 2,442 crore in FY2024-25, EBITDA excluding other income rose about 250% to roughly Rs 470 crore, and PAT tripled to about Rs 298 crore, driven by scaled execution of AMISP projects.[3] The concession model converts installation pace into revenue and installed meters into annuity.

The same report carries the honest caveat: new tender activity moderated during the year, which the company reads as a pause while utilities absorb earlier orders.[3] Scheme-driven sectors move in policy waves. The concession book smooths revenue, but the growth rate still rides the tender calendar.

Who carries the collection risk?

In equipment, the supplier does, through receivables, retention and the discipline of its own bid terms. In AMISP concessions, the structure tries to move it: meter-service charges are routed through dedicated mechanisms precisely because the payer is a distribution utility. Neither model escapes the underlying truth that the Indian grid's customer is financially fragile, so the strongest players underwrite collection as carefully as they engineer the product.

GE Vernova T&D India ended FY2024-25 not just profitable but debt-free, with cash and bank balances of Rs 1,051 crore, which it credits to working-capital discipline alongside earnings.[1] In a sector where everyone sells to the same strained buyer, the balance sheet is the shock absorber, and a net cash position is a competitive weapon.

The discipline runs both ways. A supplier with cash can afford to refuse a badly structured tender, walk away from a utility with a poor payment record, and keep investing through the pause between ordering waves. A stretched one must take whatever the cycle offers, on the buyer's terms. Collection risk, in the end, is allocated by negotiation, and negotiation is won by whoever needs the deal less.

What do the FY2024-25 numbers actually show?

Three listed businesses, three positions in the same grid. GE Vernova T&D India, the high-voltage equipment veteran, rode record transmission ordering to a debt-free balance sheet. TARIL, the transformer specialist, shows the qualified-capacity margin story at work. Genus Power shows the AMISP annuity machine scaling. All three grew; what differs is the duration and the risk inside each order book.

Where does the technology risk sit?

In grid equipment, technology risk is gentler than in solar cells but it is not absent. HVDC links, FACTS devices, STATCOMs and digital substations raise the engineering bar, and suppliers without those references watch the high-margin end of the cycle pass to those who have them. GE Vernova T&D India's decision to invest in exactly these technologies, backed by its global parent's grid portfolio, is the moat being maintained in public.[1]

In metering, the risk runs the other way: the technology is mature, so the moat is execution and payment structure, not invention. The meter maker's version of obsolescence is a communication standard or a scheme design that changes mid-rollout. Both models reward the incumbent who has already proven the current generation and is quietly qualifying the next.

What can break a grid business?

Five breaks matter. A stalled ordering cycle: transmission and RDSS investment are policy-paced, and a pause empties the inflow pipeline while capacity costs stay. Price aggression at the bid: a tender won cheap during the boom becomes a loss delivered slowly over 15 months. Working-capital strain: receivables and retention against financially weak utilities turn profit into a funding gap. Execution failure: in AMISP, meters that fail availability tests do not get paid for, however many are installed. And concession concentration: a service annuity is only as good as the utility and the payment-security mechanism behind it.

Notice the pattern: none of these is about whether India needs a grid. It does, urgently. The breaks live in bidding discipline, counterparty quality and contract structure, which is why the analysis of this sector is read off the order book's terms, not its size.

What would make us walk away?

Walk away from order books quoted without inflow, duration or margin, because a number without a tempo is decoration. Walk away from equipment makers whose receivable days climb while they celebrate record orders, and from metering stories where installation pace outruns utility payment security. Walk away when high-voltage qualification is claimed but the product list stops at distribution class. And walk away from any pitch that assumes the tender wave never pauses; the companies themselves disclose that it does.

The keepers are the mirror image: qualified high-voltage capacity with pricing discipline, order inflows that refresh the backlog faster than it burns, annuity books backed by payment security rather than promises, and a balance sheet that can fund the cycle's pauses from its own cash. The grid will be built either way. The question each company must answer is whether it is paid for building it.
