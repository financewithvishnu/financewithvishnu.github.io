Chapter 39. Index Funds and ETFs

Chapter 39 of 85 · about 20 minutes

The most disruptive idea in the history of investing required no genius, no model, no edge: buy the whole market, hold it forever, and let costs, not brilliance, decide the winner. Half a century after the first index fund, the idea has absorbed trillions of dollars and humiliated generations of professionals. This chapter explains why it won, how to use it in India, and, honestly, the cases where paying a human is still worth it.

39.1The index idea and why it won

Chapter 12 defined an index as a portfolio with published rules. The index fund simply buys that portfolio, mechanically, at near-zero cost. Its victory rests on one iron piece of arithmetic: before costs, the average investor earns the market's return; after costs, the average investor earns the market's return minus costs. Active managers as a group cannot beat the market, because they are the market, and after their 1.5-2% fees, they must trail it as a group. Some managers do beat the index, genuinely and skilfully; the problem is identifying them in advance, because past winners regress and the survivors of any league table are partly luck wearing skill's clothes. The index fund accepts the market's return, keeps the costs, and lets the maths of averages do what brilliance promises and rarely delivers.

39.2Index funds vs ETFs: structure, pricing, tracking error

Both hold the index; the wrapper differs. An index fund is a mutual fund: you buy and redeem at the day's closing NAV, no demat needed, SIP-friendly, the default choice for most Indians. An ETF trades on the exchange intraday like a stock: you need a demat account, you pay the spread and brokerage, and the price can drift slightly from NAV, though authorised participants arbitrage the gap closed. The quality measure for both is tracking error, how faithfully the fund reproduces the index's return, and its quieter sibling tracking difference, the actual annual shortfall. Before buying, compare the candidates on TER, tracking difference over several years, and, for ETFs, liquidity and spread. The cheapest fund is not the best fund; the cheapest fund that tracks tightly is.

39.3Choosing an index: broad, sector, factor and smart beta

The menu has widened far beyond the Nifty 50, and the choice of index is the investment decision. Broad: Nifty 50 for the large profitable core, Nifty Next 50 for the bench, Nifty 500 or total-market variants for everything at once; most investors need one or two of these and nothing else. Sector and thematic: bank, IT, pharma, defence indices, which are Chapter 31's themes in fund form, with the same sizing rules. Factor and smart beta: momentum, low-volatility, quality, value, equal-weight, each a bet that one documented anomaly (Chapters 27-30) keeps paying; they diversify the plain index's cap-weighted concentration in the biggest names, at slightly higher cost and with their own long droughts. The discipline: the more exotic the index, the more it is a strategy you must understand and hold through its bad years, not a passive default you can forget.

39.4Gold ETFs, international ETFs and fund-of-funds

The index wrapper extends beyond Indian equity. Gold ETFs hold physical gold at the exchange, giving you the metal's hedge without the jeweller's making charges and purity doubt; a 5-10% portfolio allocation is the standard hedge sizing, Part I's diversification made tradable. International ETFs and feeder funds open the US and global markets: the S&P 500 and Nasdaq-100 in rupee form, subject to the industry's overseas investment limits, which periodically close and reopen the tap, and to currency, which adds the rupee's long depreciation as a quiet tailwind. Fund-of-funds package these when direct ETFs are unavailable or limited, at the price of a second fee layer (Chapter 38's warning). The principle: each wrapper is a tool for an allocation decision you have made; none of them is a strategy by itself.

39.5When active still earns its fee

The honest counterweight, because this book is not a pamphlet. Active management has genuine hunting grounds: small and mid-caps, where coverage is thin and the index is a dumping ground for the deteriorating; credit, where an index buys the most indebted issuers by construction; and categories where the index itself is flawed. India's record shows persistent active success exactly in those inefficient corners, and persistent active failure in the large-cap core. The decision framework: default to index in efficient markets; consider active where inefficiency is structural; demand from any active fund a specific, articulable reason it should win, a process, a niche, a cost advantage, not a star manager's highlight reel; and hold it to a simple review: three to five years of failing its benchmark is the answer, whatever the story. Paying for active is a bet on a process. Make sure you can state the process.

39.6Worked example: index fund vs the average active large-cap fund, fifteen years

Case: the race nobody was supposed to win

The shape of the Indian evidence, consistent with SPIVA's long-running scorecards:

The table's last row is the one that decides real lives. Some active funds beat the index over those fifteen years, a minority, and their identities were essentially unknowable in advance; the investor who simply bought the index beat most of the professionals, paid almost nothing, and made exactly one decision. The active route is not wrong; it is hard: you must pick the winner in advance, hold it through its underperforming years, and re-pick when the manager leaves or the style drifts. The index route's entire requirement is that you keep buying through the crashes. Both demand character. Only one demands being right.
