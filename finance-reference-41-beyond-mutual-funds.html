Chapter 41. Beyond Mutual Funds: PMS, AIFs, REITs and InvITs

Chapter 41 of 85 · about 19 minutes

Above the mutual fund shelf sits a smaller, costlier, more exclusive layer of Indian investment products: portfolio management services, alternative investment funds, and the listed income vehicles, REITs and InvITs. Some are genuinely useful; all are sold with more glamour than their economics usually justify. This chapter tells you what each one is, what it really charges, and, most importantly, which investor each is actually for.

41.1Portfolio management services: minimums, fees, fit

A PMS is a separately managed account: your own demat holds the stocks, a manager runs them to a strategy, and the regulatory minimum is ₹50 lakh. The appeal is real: concentration beyond what mutual funds allow, direct ownership, and a manager you can call. The costs are also real and frequently underestimated: management fees of 1-2.5%, performance fees of 10-20% over a hurdle, plus full brokerage and the tax friction of the manager's turnover landing in your account, a drag mutual funds structurally avoid. The fit test is therefore strict: a PMS must beat the equivalent mutual fund after its heavier fees and taxes, using the same strategy. Some specialist managers genuinely clear that bar, particularly in small-caps and special situations where mutual funds cannot concentrate. Most of the shelf does not, and the ₹50 lakh investor is usually better served by Chapter 40's architecture plus a good advisor.

41.2Alternative investment funds: Categories I, II and III

AIFs are privately pooled funds for sophisticated investors, ₹1 crore minimum, in three regulatory flavours. Category I backs the real economy's young: venture capital, angel, social and infrastructure funds, illiquid by design, with returns that follow a J-curve, fees first, harvest years later. Category II is the broad middle: private equity, real estate funds, private credit, the Indian private-credit boom of recent years lives here, lending where banks will not at yields that reflect exactly that. Category III trades: long-short equity, derivatives, leverage permitted, the local hedge-fund habitat. The investor's frame: AIFs sell access, access to private deals, to strategies funds cannot run, and the access is priced accordingly, 2-and-20 style fees plus multi-year lock-ups. The due diligence must therefore be heavier than the product is glamorous: the manager's realised, not paper, track record; the fee waterfall in the documents; and your own liquidity, because a crore locked for five years is a lifestyle decision, not a trade.

41.3REITs: owning property through the market

A REIT owns finished, income-producing commercial property, office parks, increasingly malls and warehouses, collects the rent, and must distribute at least 90% of its cash distributable to unitholders. India's listed REITs have quietly built the track record the structure promised: mid-to-high single-digit distribution yields plus modest rental growth, with unit prices that fluctuate like stocks because they are stocks. The reading toolkit: distribution yield, occupancy, the weighted-average lease length, the sponsor's pipeline, and the borrowings against the assets. The honest comparison, against the Indian retail investor's favourite asset, direct property, is the table below, and it surprises most people who run it properly.

41.4InvITs: infrastructure as an income asset

The InvIT is the REIT's infrastructure cousin: it owns operating roads, transmission lines, pipelines, and pays out the tolls and tariffs as distributions. The economics differ in one crucial way: many InvIT assets are concessions, finite contracts that eventually hand the asset back, so part of every distribution is effectively your own capital returning, and the yield must be read against the concession's remaining life, not as a perpetual coupon. The good ones are transparent about this and price accordingly; the sector's history already contains a cautionary tale of an over-leveraged InvIT whose yield was borrowed from its own balance sheet. The checks: concession lives, leverage against the regulatory caps, counterparty quality (who pays the tolls), and distribution coverage from genuine cash flow. An InvIT is a bond with traffic risk; price it like one.

41.5Global parallels

Every product in this chapter has an older foreign twin, and the twins' histories are free education. US REITs have fifty years of record: they compounded competitively with stocks, with higher income and higher rate-sensitivity, and they spent long stretches trading below the value of their own buildings, a reminder that listed property discounts and premiums are a cycle of their own. UCITS funds are Europe's mutual funds, the template for cross-border fund regulation, and the wrapper through which Indian investors meet many international feeders. The lesson generalises: none of these structures is exotic anymore; each is a known machine with a known failure history. When an Indian product's salesman says "new", the honest translation is usually "new to you".

41.6Worked example: a listed Indian REIT vs direct property

Case: the same ₹50 lakh, two doors

Through door one, the investor buys a listed office REIT: 7% distribution yield, rents growing 4-5%, a unit price that dips when rates rise and recovers as distributions compound, total return in the low double digits across a full rate cycle, sellable any trading day. Through door two, the same ₹50 lakh plus a loan buys a small commercial shop: gross yield 7%, minus a quarter vacant year one, minus maintenance, property tax, and a broker at both ends; net yield under 5%, with the entire return's upside riding on one locality's fate and the exit priced by whoever shows up to buy. Direct property still wins where the buyer has genuine local edge, development skill, or leverage working for them; as a passive income asset for a salaried investor, the listed version wins on every line of the table except one: it shows you its price every day, and many investors would rather not look. That preference is a feeling, not a return.
