Industrial gases: selling reliability, not just molecules

By Finance With Vishnu · Class date: 26 July 2026 · Sector study

An industrial-gas company supplies gases used inside another business's process. A steel plant may need oxygen. A food processor may use nitrogen. A hospital needs dependable medical-gas supply. The molecule matters, but the customer is also buying the right quality, the right quantity and dependable delivery.

This makes industrial gases a useful business to study. Air is available everywhere, yet supplying a specified gas continuously can require expensive plants, power, storage, transport, trained people and long relationships. The value is in turning an available input into a service the customer can depend on.

My starting question is therefore not whether demand for oxygen will grow. It is who owns the plant, how the customer is supplied, which costs can be recovered and what happens when the customer's demand falls. Those answers explain far more than a broad sector-growth forecast.

1. What are industrial gases, and do they all come from air?

Industrial gases are gases supplied for use in manufacturing, healthcare, food, chemicals, electronics and other applications. The category includes oxygen, nitrogen, argon, hydrogen, helium, carbon dioxide and many specialised mixtures. They do not all share the same raw material or production process.

Atmospheric gases such as oxygen, nitrogen and argon can be separated from air. Other gases need different feedstocks or recovery methods. Linde's 2025 filing describes hydrogen production from several feedstocks, carbon dioxide recovered from industrial or natural sources, and helium sourced from helium-rich natural-gas streams. Calling the entire industry an air-separation business would therefore be misleading. [1]

Even within atmospheric gases, the commercial product is more than a chemical name. The required purity, pressure, physical form, quantity and continuity of supply determine the equipment and service needed. A customer requiring a continuous pipeline supply has a different problem from a workshop buying occasional cylinders.

2. If the air is free, where do the costs come from?

Atmospheric air may not carry a purchase price, but separating and supplying its components is not free. Plants need energy, maintenance, skilled staff and capital. Liquid products require additional processing and suitable storage and distribution. A tanker journey and a cylinder fleet remain real costs even when the original feedstock is air.

Linde calls energy the single largest cost item in producing and distributing industrial gases. It identifies electricity, natural gas and diesel among its main energy needs. Air Products also describes electricity as its largest cost in atmospheric-gas production. Those statements alone should stop us from assuming a near-100% margin because air is available without charge. [1] [2]

The accounting label matters too. A low reported cost of materials does not mean the business has almost no variable cost. Power can appear in a different expense line. Transport, purchased gases, plant depreciation and service costs may sit elsewhere. I would read the full expense structure before drawing a conclusion from a materials-to-sales ratio.

The right bridge is from the delivered selling price to cash left after all the work needed to serve that customer. Feedstock cost is only one part of that bridge.

3. Why does the supply mode change the business?

The basic supply modes are on-site or pipeline supply, merchant bulk supply and packaged gases. Linde describes all three and notes that the same plant can support more than one mode. Supply is selected around the customer's volume, usage pattern, purity and required form. [1]

On-site supply usually serves a customer needing substantial, relatively steady volumes. A supplier builds or operates production on or near the customer's facility and supplies through a pipeline. The relationship may be long, but the supplier has committed capital to a particular location and customer requirement.

Merchant supply takes product from a plant to customers, often by tanker to storage at their premises. Here, production efficiency, the delivery network, customer density and the amount sold into the surrounding market become especially important.

Packaged supply serves smaller or more varied requirements through cylinders and other containers. The delivery route, container fleet, filling network, customer service and collection of empty containers are part of the business. It is not simply bulk supply at a higher selling price.

Three questions before comparing margins

Is the company earning from a large contracted pipeline customer, a network of bulk customers, or many packaged-gas deliveries? Does it own the supply equipment? Which costs and assets are included in the reported segment?

Without that context, a higher margin may reflect a different service model rather than a better operator.

4. What does a long contract actually protect?

A long contract can improve revenue visibility, but its protection depends on the words. Minimum purchase obligations, fixed facility charges, energy adjustments, inflation escalation, performance requirements, termination rights and the customer's ability to pay all matter.

Linde's 2025 filing describes on-site contracts typically lasting 10 to 20 years, with minimum purchase requirements and price escalation. Air Products distinguishes large on-site arrangements generally lasting 15 to 20 years from smaller on-site plants generally under 10- to 15-year contracts. Its on-site contracts commonly include fixed monthly charges and/or minimum purchases. [1] [2]

For an Indian operating example, INOX Air Products describes its on-site supply as supported by 15- to 20-year take-or-pay contracts indexed to energy and inflation. That is evidence of its stated model, not proof that every Indian gas supplier has the same protection. [4]

Take-or-pay broadly means the contract requires payment for an agreed minimum even if the buyer takes less. It does not mean unlimited profit, no customer risk or payment regardless of the supplier's performance. The actual contract determines the conditions and remedies.

Before treating contracted revenue as secure, I would ask who bears construction overruns, when charging starts, what happens during customer shutdowns and whether payment depends on meeting availability or quality standards. A strong customer relationship is useful. A clear allocation of risk is more useful.

5. Are merchant contracts just shorter versions of on-site contracts?

Not necessarily. The volume commitment can differ as much as the duration. Linde describes merchant agreements as usually three- to seven-year requirement contracts. Air Products says its merchant sales do not include minimum purchase requirements and are governed by contracts or purchase orders generally lasting five years or less. [1] [2]

These disclosures should not be forced into a single industry rule. A requirement contract, a minimum purchase commitment and a fixed monthly payment are not interchangeable descriptions. We need the company's own disclosure and, where available, the relevant contract terms.

Merchant supply may spread customer risk across many buyers, but that does not remove demand risk. A weak industrial region can affect several customers at once. A supplier can also face competition from a nearby plant with spare capacity.

My preference is to separate committed payments from volume-dependent sales. Then I can test how much cash remains if deliveries weaken, rather than assuming the word contract makes every rupee equally predictable.

6. How does energy pass-through affect revenue and margins?

A pass-through arrangement allows specified cost changes to be reflected in what the customer pays. Its usefulness depends on the covered costs, adjustment formula, time lag, caps and the customer's payment behaviour. Linde explicitly warns that its contractual attempts to limit energy-cost variability may not fully protect results. [1]

It also changes how we read growth. If higher electricity costs are passed through, revenue can rise without an equivalent increase in gas volume or underlying profit. The percentage margin can fall even when the amount of operating profit is unchanged.

The reverse can also happen when energy costs fall. That is why I would separate price, volume, energy recovery and mix before judging a quarter from reported revenue growth or margin alone.

7. Where can a local distribution advantage come from?

For bulk products, the delivered cost includes the journey from the plant. A supplier with an efficient plant near a dense group of customers may have an advantage over a distant competitor. More useful deliveries from the same network can improve the economics of transport and service.

But there is no universal delivery radius for every gas. Linde says merchant oxygen and nitrogen generally have a relatively small distribution radius because of distribution cost, while merchant argon, hydrogen and helium can travel much longer distances. A single kilometre rule for the whole sector would hide important differences. [1]

The existence of a nearby plant is not enough either. We need to know its available product mix, customer commitments, transport capacity and competitive alternatives. A plant may be close to an expanding industrial cluster but unable to capture that demand on attractive terms.

I would map plants, major customer areas and competing supply before calling local presence a moat. Then I would test whether the advantage survives lower utilisation, a new entrant or a change in the customer's own production.

8. Why can utilisation and product mix matter together?

A plant has fixed costs and a finite ability to produce and deliver its products. Selling more can spread some costs over a larger base. Yet the value of added output depends on which gas is sold, to whom, through which supply mode and at what delivered contribution.

Air separation can produce several products from a shared production system. The customer mix therefore matters alongside headline capacity. A useful analysis asks whether the supplier can monetise the products available from that system, not merely whether one demand category is growing.

The same issue appears in expansion announcements. Tonnes per day describe physical capacity, not annual revenue, operating profit or free cash flow. Commissioning, customer ramp-up, product mix, energy consumption and delivery requirements sit between installed capacity and returns.

I would look for the point at which the asset starts earning, the time needed to reach a useful level of sales and the incremental capital still required. A newly commissioned plant can strengthen a long-term position while depressing near-term cash generation. Both can be true.

9. What does Ellenbarrie's FY25 cost structure show?

Ellenbarrie's prospectus dated 26 June 2025 provides a clear historical illustration of why free air does not mean a costless product. For the year ended 31 March 2025, it reported revenue from operations of ₹3,124.83 million and power and fuel expenses of ₹749.15 million. Power and fuel were 23.97% of operating revenue. [3]

In crore terms, those amounts are approximately ₹312.48 crore and ₹74.92 crore. For FY24 and FY23, the disclosed power-and-fuel ratios were 28.82% and 36.07%. The falling ratio is an observation, not proof of a permanent improvement or a forecast of future margins.

The denominator needs attention. The FY25 revenue note includes manufactured products, traded products, services including facility fees and operation-and-maintenance charges, construction contracts and finance-lease interest. It would be wrong to describe every rupee as recurring sales of gases produced in the company's own plants.

That mix also limits what we can infer from a changing expense ratio. I would investigate power prices, production volumes, contract recoveries and revenue mix before attributing the entire change to operating efficiency. A useful financial example should create better questions, not a shortcut to a stock conclusion.

10. Why separate gas supply from plant and equipment projects?

A gas supplier can also design or build plants, operate equipment for a customer or sell related equipment. These activities may sit near each other technically while producing different financial results. Linde reports an Engineering business, and Air Products separately describes its industrial-gases equipment activities. [1] [2]

Building and selling a plant is different from owning a plant and selling gases for years. A project has milestones, construction costs, acceptance conditions and collection timing. Recurring supply has ongoing production, service, maintenance and customer-volume exposure. Neither is automatically better; they require different checks.

The same applies to an equipment manufacturer supplying cryogenic tanks or components. It may benefit from gas-industry investment without earning gas-supply revenue or holding long-term take-or-pay contracts. Industry association does not transfer another company's business model.

I would split the order book and revenue by activity before applying a single growth assumption or valuation argument. A large project order can be important while still being a poor guide to next year's recurring gas earnings.

11. What can turn reported profit into weak cash returns?

Industrial gases require physical assets. Production plants, storage, tankers, pipelines and cylinders can absorb cash before revenue arrives. Accounting profit therefore needs to be read alongside maintenance spending, expansion spending, receivables, financing and the returns on newly commissioned capacity.

A high operating margin does not settle the investment question. A business that needs much more capital to generate each rupee of sales can earn a lower capital return than a lower-margin business. The relevant test is profit relative to the capital needed to earn it, using a consistent definition.

I would also distinguish a company that owns the productive assets from one operating customer-owned assets for a fee. Similar revenue growth can require very different investment. Read the balance sheet and cash-flow statement, not only the income statement.

12. Why are safety and qualification part of the economics?

Dependable supply requires more than having spare molecules. Product quality, suitable equipment, safe handling and trained staff are part of the service. EIGA's air-separation safety guidance discusses hazards involving pressure, low temperatures, oxygen enrichment and oxygen deficiency. This article is not a guide to operating or handling gas equipment. [5]

In India, PESO's published guidance covers approvals and licensing under the Gas Cylinders Rules, 2016, including cylinder manufacture, filling, possession and testing-station matters. The exact requirements depend on the activity and applicable rules. For an investor, the point is that compliance and equipment integrity are ongoing obligations, not optional overhead. [6]

Specialty and electronics applications also need care in the investment story. A statement that a company can supply a gas is not the same as proof of customer qualification, a supply contract, meaningful revenue or attractive returns. Avoid assigning an electronics premium to an entire gas portfolio without those steps.

When a customer cannot easily tolerate interruption or contamination, a dependable supplier can be valuable. That is a reason to investigate quality systems, redundancy and service history, not to assume every incumbent has unlimited pricing power.

13. What would I check before forming a view?

I would begin with the gas, customer and supply mode. Then I would separate the recurring supply business from projects, equipment and other services. Only after that would I compare growth, margins and capital returns.

  • Product: which gases are sold, how are they sourced and what specifications do customers require?
  • Customer and location: who uses the gas, how concentrated is demand and which alternative suppliers can serve the same area?
  • Contract: what is the duration, minimum payment, energy adjustment and exposure to customer shutdowns or default?
  • Plant and delivery: when does capacity start earning, which products can be sold and what distribution assets are needed?
  • Financial bridge: how much growth comes from volume, price, energy recovery, projects or a change in mix?
  • Cash and capital: what must be spent to maintain and expand the system, and what return does that investment earn?
  • Evidence: do qualification, orders, revenue and cash support the claimed opportunity?

The attraction of industrial gases is easy to describe: customers need them, supply can be difficult to replace and some relationships last for years. The harder question is whether a particular company converts those features into dependable cash returns after paying for the plant, power and service network.

That is where I would spend the research time. Selling an essential product can be a good starting point. Selling it on terms that reward the capital and risk taken is what makes the business worth studying further.
