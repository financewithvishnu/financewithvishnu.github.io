Packaging: the film, the converter and the customer

By Finance With Vishnu · Source period: approximately October 2024 · Exact class dates unconfirmed

Nobody in this sector sells packaging. They sell the film that keeps food fresh, the carton that carries a brand to the shelf, and the tube that a consumer squeezes every morning without thinking. Three different businesses hide inside the phrase "packaging company", and they differ in the only way that matters to an investor: whether the margin comes from a commodity spread, a conversion service, or a customer who cannot easily leave.

FY2024-25 shows all three at work in India: substrate makers running commodity-scale film lines, converters printing and forming other people's materials, and specialists so deep inside a customer's product that the packaging is part of the brand. Follow the film, the converter and the customer.

Study window: this is an original explanation of an approximately October 2024 framework; the exact class day is unconfirmed. FY2024-25 annual reports provide dated checks. They are not current data. This is education, not investment advice.

What does a packaging company actually sell?

Start with the layers. At the bottom is the substrate: polymer films like BOPP, BOPET and CPP, or paperboard, or glass, made in capital-hungry plants that run best at full tilt. Above that sits conversion: printing, laminating, coating, forming, the steps that turn a roll of film or a sheet of board into a pouch, a carton or a tube. At the top sits the customer relationship: the design that was qualified into a brand's product, sometimes years ago, and reordered ever since.

Every packaging company earns some mix of these. A film plant is a spread business. A converter is a service business. A qualified supplier is a franchise. The label on the gate tells you less than the layer where the margin actually lives.

Why does substrate capacity behave like a commodity?

A BOPP or BOPET film line is a serious piece of capital. It runs continuously, it produces a product that buyers treat as interchangeable, and its profitability rides the spread between polymer resin prices and film prices. When the industry adds lines faster than demand grows, every line's spread compresses at once.

Uflex illustrates the scale: a global capacity above 1.3 million tonnes a year, including about 6.4 lakh tonnes of base films and 4.3 lakh tonnes of resins, across nine manufacturing locations and customers in over 150 countries. At that scale the company is a materials business first: volumes of 6,47,499 tonnes in FY2024-25, and profits that rise and fall with the film spread and plant utilisation more than with any single customer.

Read a substrate maker the way you read a refinery: utilisation, the spread, and the cost position. Revenue growth means little if the industry added a line at the same time.

What does the converter earn over the substrate?

The converter buys the film or the board and sells a service: print quality, registration, lamination, delivery on the customer's filling schedule. The margin is the difference between what the customer pays for a finished pack and what the material plus conversion costs. That margin is steadier than the substrate spread, because the converter usually passes material cost through with a lag.

But the conversion margin is contested. The customer is often a large FMCG or pharma company with a procurement department, and the converter down the road owns similar machines. What protects the margin is not the press; it is qualification, consistency and the cost a customer would pay to switch. A converter without those is a job shop with a lease.

The year's reports separate the three models cleanly.

The pattern is visible: EPL's customer-qualified tube business runs a 19.9% EBITDA margin, TCPL's carton franchise runs near 16.6%, and Uflex's integrated commodity-to-converter chain runs at a lower margin on a far larger base, with profit swinging more sharply between years.

Why does customer intensity decide the margin?

The most valuable thing a packaging company can own is a place inside the customer's product. A tube qualified into a global toothpaste brand, or a carton qualified into a pharma line, is reordered for years. The customer's switching cost is not the price of a new supplier's sample; it is the risk of a failure on a filling line that runs at thousands of units a minute, plus a fresh qualification cycle.

This is why the specialist converters carry the sector's best margins. The customer is not buying film or board; it is buying the certainty that the pack will run, seal and look identical in every market. That certainty is worth a margin, and it compounds as the relationship deepens into more SKUs and more geographies.

Customer intensity also shows up quietly in the working capital: a qualified supplier often holds customer-approved inventory and receives forecasts instead of purchase orders, which smooths the plant's week. None of this appears as a line item, but it is the difference between selling a product and being part of one.

What makes a tube or a carton sticky?

EPL's FY2024-25 is the cleanest demonstration. Revenue grew 7.6% to about Rs 4,213 crore while the EBITDA margin reached 19.9%, the eleventh consecutive quarter of margin expansion on management's count, and adjusted PAT rose 44.6% to about Rs 363 crore. A company that makes one in every three oral care tubes in the world (its own claim) does not win each order on price; it wins on being already inside.

TCPL shows the same logic in paper. Revenue grew 15% to about Rs 1,770 crore with EBITDA up 16.6% to about Rs 293 crore, and the company describes itself as the country's largest standalone paperboard converter, serving FMCG, food and beverage, liquor, pharmaceuticals and tobacco. The carton is customised to the brand, printed to its artwork, and locked into its supply chain; the converter that delivers without drama keeps the account.

Why do cartons sit between paper and brand?

A folding carton is a quiet hybrid. Its main input is paperboard, a material with its own price cycle, but its value is created in printing, cutting, creasing and gluing to a brand's exact specification. The carton maker therefore lives partly in the substrate world, watching board prices, and partly in the service world, where the brand cares about how the box looks on the shelf and runs on the line.

The better carton companies manage both sides: board procurement and inventory for the material cycle, and design support and consistency for the brand cycle. TCPL's decade of roughly 13.5% revenue CAGR from FY2015 to FY2025, funded largely from internal accruals, is what that balance looks like over time.

Does integration help or hide the cycle?

Uflex runs the whole chain: resins, base films, inks, adhesives, coatings, printing cylinders and finished pouches. Integration captures margin at every step and guarantees supply to the conversion business. But it also concentrates the cycle: when film spreads compress, the integrated chain holds more of the commodity exposure, and when spreads widen, it keeps more of the upside.

FY2024-25 showed the upside: operational EBITDA rose 18.1% to about Rs 1,902 crore. Yet consolidated PAT of about Rs 142 crore on a total income of about Rs 15,184 crore reminds you how much of the chain's earning is consumed by depreciation and interest on its asset base. Integration is a leverage on the spread, in both directions. Judge it over a full cycle, never a single year.

How do polymer and paper costs move through the P&L, and when does capacity hurt?

Resin tracks crude and naphtha; paperboard tracks pulp and waste paper. Neither asks the packaging company before moving. The pass-through mechanism decides who feels the move: converters with price-adjustment contracts see margin wobble for a quarter and recover; companies that carry fixed-price commitments or slow contract resets absorb the shock in the margin line.

The inventory policy matters as much as the contract. A converter that stocks up before a price rise earns a one-off gain; the same stock before a fall writes the next quarter's miss. When you read a packaging result, separate the volume story from the cost-timing story before you credit management with a good year.

Capacity timing rides on the same cycle. In the substrate layer, one company's new line is every company's problem: capacity added ahead of demand compresses the industry spread until demand catches up. In the conversion layer, capacity follows the customer: a new carton line justified by a signed brand expansion is a different risk from a speculative film line. Watch who pays for the capacity and why. TCPL reports funding its investments largely from internal accruals, with most of its FY2024-25 capital expenditure aligned to ESG-focused technologies, a sign of demand-led, conservatively funded growth. Uflex's capacity additions sit on a global commodity curve and must be read against industry supply, not company ambition.

What do exports change?

Packaging is heavy and bulky relative to its value, so long exports are rare except where the product is specialised or the customer is global. EPL is the exception by design: a plant network across AMESA, EAP, the Americas and Europe that follows its multinational customers into each region, making exports a factory-location question rather than a freight question. TCPL's FY2024-25 commentary flags increased exports alongside portfolio diversification as growth drivers.

The test is the same as everywhere in this sector: does the export business carry the qualification moat with it, or is it commodity volume sold into someone else's home market? A carton or tube sold alongside a global brand's regional expansion keeps the franchise's pricing. A film sold on a spot tender does not, no matter how impressive the export tonnage looks in the chairman's letter.

How should you read a packaging annual report?

What can break the economics?

Three risks recur. In the substrate layer, overcapacity: spreads can stay compressed for years when the industry overbuilds. In the conversion layer, customer concentration: the same intensity that protects the margin makes losing one large account expensive. Across all layers, the material cycle: resin, pulp and energy move faster than contracts.

The mitigants are structural. A qualified customer base, a conversion-heavy mix, conservative funding and honest pass-through contracts do not remove the cycle, but they decide who earns through it.

Watch also the quiet risk of success: a specialist whose best customer keeps growing can drift into dependence, where the account that built the margin starts to set it. The healthiest converters keep qualifying new brands and new categories even while the flagship account hums, because customer intensity is an asset only while it is diversified.

How do the three companies differ?

What should you remember?

A packaging company is three businesses wearing one name. The substrate plant is a commodity spread with a smokestack. The converter is a service business earning over the material. The qualified supplier is a franchise living inside the customer's product. The margin tells you which one you are looking at: near 20% for the tube specialist, mid-teens for the carton converter, and a thinner, cyclical spread for the integrated film chain.

So when a packaging company reports, ask which layer grew, whether the margin came from the moat or the material, and who paid for the new capacity. Check that volume growth and margin growth arrived together, and that the year's PAT is not just a kind quarter of the resin cycle. In this sector, the customer relationship is the asset; everything else is a machine.
