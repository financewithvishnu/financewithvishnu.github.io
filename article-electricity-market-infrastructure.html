Electricity-market infrastructure: the operator, the exchange and the trader

By Finance With Vishnu · Source period: March 2021

India buys and sells electricity on screens now, and it is tempting to think of that market as a smaller NSE with electrons instead of shares. The analogy fails in the first minute. A share trade settles in a day or two and the buyer can hold it forever; an electricity trade is a promise to deliver power at a specific time into a grid that must balance supply and demand every second, and nobody can store the product in a demat account. That physical fact designs the whole market: who may trade, what the exchange actually does, and why the system operator sits above everyone.

Three institutions carry the Indian power market, and they earn in completely different ways. The system operator runs the grid and the schedule. The exchange runs an anonymous auction and takes a fee per unit traded, with platform margins a software company would envy. The trader signs bilateral contracts, carries credit risk, and earns a thin spread on an enormous turnover. FY2024-25 shows all three clearly.

Source period: March 2021. FY2024-25 annual reports and dated official releases provide checks. They are not current data. This is education, not investment advice.

What does an electricity market actually sell?

Start with the product. Electricity is delivered, not shipped: when you buy a megawatt-hour for tomorrow evening, you are buying an instruction that some generator will inject power and your distribution company will draw it, balanced in real time by the grid operator. Because the product cannot be stored economically at scale, every contract is ultimately a scheduling promise, and every promise has to be honoured physically by the grid the moment it comes due.

This is why the power market has a structure no securities market needs. A stock exchange matches buyers and sellers and its work is done. An electricity market must also tell the grid what is coming: who generates, who draws, when, and what happens when reality deviates from the schedule. Market design and grid operation are the same subject wearing two coats.

Three roles follow from that: the operator who keeps the grid balanced, the exchange where anonymous price discovery happens, and the trader who stitches together the bilateral contracts that still carry most of India's power.

Why does the system operator sit above the market?

The Grid Controller of India, the government enterprise that runs the National and Regional Load Despatch Centres, does not buy or sell a single unit of power. Its job is the grid itself: frequency, security, scheduling and settlement of deviations. Every trade, whether struck on an exchange or bilaterally, becomes a schedule the operator must implement, and every deviation from schedule is priced through its mechanism.

Regulators treat the operator's independence as the foundation of the whole market. Tariff orders and consultation papers return repeatedly to the same phrase: the functional and financial autonomy of load despatch centres is the key to grid reliability and an efficient electricity market. A market whose referee reported to one of the players would not stay a market for long.

For investors the operator matters less as a company, it is unlisted and runs on regulated cost recovery, than as the rule-setter that decides what everyone else's product means. Market rules, deviation prices and open-access procedures written in the operator's world shape the revenues earned in the exchange and trading worlds.

What does the exchange actually do?

Indian Energy Exchange is the dominant electricity exchange: a nationwide, automated platform, regulated by the Central Electricity Regulatory Commission, operating since 2008. What it sells is trust at scale. Generators and buyers quote anonymously; the exchange clears the auction, discovers the price, stands between the two sides on settlement, and takes a small fee on every unit traded. Its product is not electricity but liquidity: the assurance that you can buy or sell power today, at a visible price, without knowing or trusting the counterparty.

The business economics follow the platform pattern. FY2024-25 standalone total revenue was Rs 654 crore, up 19%, against total expenses of about Rs 104 crore: an operating margin above 80%, earned on transaction and subscription fees. Profit after tax was Rs 414.65 crore, against Rs 341.44 crore the year before. Traded electricity volume reached 121 billion units, up 19%, the highest in its history, with renewable energy certificates traded up 136% to 178 lakh certificates.

An exchange with 80%-plus margins looks like a money machine, and structurally it is: each additional unit traded costs almost nothing to clear. The risk is not cost; it is that the volume is not the exchange's to command. It belongs to the market design.

How does the day-ahead auction actually clear?

The design choice that matters most is the uniform clearing price. Because every accepted seller receives the market-clearing price rather than its own bid, the auction rewards low-cost generation and reveals one honest price per time block. That price, Rs 4.47 a unit on average in FY2024-25, becomes the reference for the whole sector: bilateral contracts price off it, regulators read it, and the deviation mechanism anchors to it.

Notice also what the auction does not do. It does not decide which plants exist, which fuel they burn, or what they earn on capital; it decides which of the plants that exist run tomorrow, and at what price. The market optimises dispatch, not investment. Confusing those two jobs is the most common analytical error in this sector.

Why does liquidity concentrate?

Liquidity in a power exchange works like gravity: buyers go where sellers are, sellers go where buyers are, and the venue with the deepest order book clears the most reliable price. India's day-ahead volume concentrates on one exchange for the same reason most equity volume concentrates on one or two exchanges worldwide. The practical result is that the leading platform's economics reinforce themselves, and the meaningful competition happens at the edges, new products, new segments, rather than in the core auction.

The edges are real, though. The same group's gas exchange traded a record 60 million MMBtu in FY2024-25, up 47%, reaching about a sixth of the country's natural gas spot market, and the green market grew 171% in the year. New liquidity pools are where the exchange model gets replayed from the beginning, with the same gravity pulling volume toward whoever establishes depth first.

What does the trader earn on the same electron?

Now look at the other channel. PTC India is a power trader: it signs purchase agreements with generators and sale agreements with distribution companies and industrial buyers, and it earns the spread between them. Its FY2024-25 numbers show what that model looks like next to an exchange. Standalone trading volume was 82,751 million units, up 11%. Turnover was Rs 15,644 crore. And profit after tax was Rs 854.78 crore, of which Rs 457.39 crore was a one-off, post-tax gain from divesting a subsidiary. Strip the one-off and the ongoing business earned roughly Rs 400 crore on Rs 15,600 crore of turnover, a margin of about 2.5%.

Compare the two P&Ls honestly. The exchange earned Rs 415 crore on Rs 654 crore of revenue; the trader earned roughly Rs 400 crore ongoing on Rs 15,644 crore. The trader's turnover is twenty-four times the exchange's revenue because it books the full value of the power it resells, while the exchange books only its fee. Same market, same electrons, completely different business: one is a toll booth, the other is a merchant.

The merchant's risks are the mirror image of the toll booth's. The trader carries credit risk on every distribution company it supplies, delivery obligations on every contract it signs, and price risk wherever its purchase and sale terms do not match. The spread looks thin precisely because the risks are real.

Why is the market a regulatory artifact?

No electricity market exists by nature. Every feature that makes India's market work, open access to the wires, the day-ahead auction, deviation pricing, renewable certificates, the licence a trader operates under, was written into being by the Electricity Act and by Central Electricity Regulatory Commission regulations. Change the rules and you change the volumes: a new market segment appears when a regulation creates it, and an old one can shrink the same way.

This makes regulatory design the sector's true demand driver. The day-ahead market's average price of Rs 4.47 a unit in FY2024-25, the surge in certificate trading, the growth of the gas exchange, each traces back to rules about who must buy what and through which channel. An investor reading this sector reads consultation papers as seriously as results, because the consultation paper of one year is the revenue line of three years later.

The corollary is sober: regulatory reversal is the sector's deepest risk. A market created by rules can be reshaped by rules, and the entities in it have influence over the process but not control.

How do new segments get born?

Watch how the product list grows. The core day-ahead auction came first; then term-ahead contracts, then green markets for renewable power, then renewable energy certificates, then a gas exchange, then contracts for energy storage and other emerging products. Each segment starts as a regulatory design decision plus a platform build, and each follows the liquidity gravity pattern: early volume is thin and fragile, then depth attracts depth.

For the exchange this is the growth algorithm: not stealing share in the core market but launching the next market before anyone else has depth in it. The cost of a new segment is technology and stakeholder work; the payoff, if the segment matures, is another fee stream with the same platform margins. The FY2024-25 numbers show the algorithm mid-flight: record electricity volume, certificates up 136%, the gas exchange up 47%.

For the trader the equivalent expansion is along the contract: longer-tenor agreements, cross-border trade, round-the-clock renewable supply stitched from multiple sources. The skills are portfolio and credit skills, and the margin stays thin and risk-priced whatever the product.

What do renewable certificates trade?

Some products on the exchange trade electricity's attributes rather than electricity itself. A renewable energy certificate represents the green attribute of renewable generation: a solar or wind generator sells the power as ordinary electricity and the greenness separately as a certificate, which an obligated buyer purchases to meet its renewable purchase obligation. The product exists because the regulation says distribution companies and large consumers must source a share of their power from renewables, and certificates let the obligation travel independently of the electron.

Certificate markets are the purest example of the sector's central truth: regulation writes the product into being, and the exchange gives it liquidity. When compliance tightens or enforcement strengthens, certificate volumes jump, as they did in FY2024-25 when trades rose 136% to 178 lakh certificates. When the obligation weakens, the same market can go quiet for years. There is no natural demand floor, only the rule book.

How do the three roles read differently?

The comparison disciplines the analysis. When the exchange reports a strong year, the question is whether volumes grew or merely prices; fees ride units, not rupees. When the trader reports a strong year, the question is whether the spread held or a one-off flattered the line, as the divestment gain did in FY2024-25. And when the operator's rules change, both of their next years change with them.

What can break the economics?

For the exchange: volume risk in all its forms. A market-design change that routes flow elsewhere, a competing platform that gains depth in a new segment, a policy shift that shrinks the short-term market it dominates. For the trader: counterparty risk, because its customers include financially stressed distribution companies, and spread compression as bilateral markets mature. For everyone: regulatory reversal, since the whole structure is written in rules.

Physical risk binds all three too. The market clears on screens but settles in copper and steel: a grid disturbance, a transmission constraint or a fuel shock moves prices and volumes in ways no platform design can smooth away. The operator exists precisely because that physical layer can never be contracted away.

The mitigants are structural. The exchange's defence is liquidity gravity and early depth in new segments. The trader's defence is diversification of counterparties and tenor. The market's defence is that electricity demand keeps growing, and every electron consumed must pass through one of these channels.

How should you read an electricity-market annual report?

Two habits sharpen the reading. First, always convert turnover into economics: the trader's giant revenue is mostly pass-through power value, and only the spread belongs to shareholders. Second, track the rule book: a CERC consultation on market design is a leading indicator of this sector's revenues in a way that no demand forecast can be.

What should you remember?

Electricity-market infrastructure is three businesses behind one set of screens. The system operator keeps the grid honest and earns regulated cost recovery. The exchange sells liquidity and earns platform margins above 80% on a fee per unit. The trader sells contract performance and earns a thin spread on a giant, pass-through turnover, with credit risk as its constant companion. FY2024-25 priced the roles cleanly: Rs 415 crore of profit on Rs 654 crore of revenue at the exchange, about Rs 400 crore of ongoing profit on Rs 15,644 crore of turnover at the trader.

So when a power-market company reports, place it in the trio first. Then ask the role's own question: for the exchange, did units traded grow; for the trader, did the spread and the credit book hold; for everyone, what did the regulator just change? The electrons are the same. The economics never are.
