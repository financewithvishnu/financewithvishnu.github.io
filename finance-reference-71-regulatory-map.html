Chapter 71. The Regulatory Map: India and the World

71.1Which regulator guards what in India: RBI, SEBI, IRDAI, PFRDA and MCA?

The five gatekeepers, and their fences. The RBI regulates banks, NBFCs, payment systems and the currency, and sets monetary policy: your savings account, home loan, UPI rail and the NBFC behind an app all sit in its perimeter. SEBI guards the securities markets: stock exchanges, brokers, mutual funds, listed companies, AIFs, investment advisers, and since 2015 the commodity derivatives markets too. IRDAI licenses and supervises insurers, which is why the missold ULIP and the rejected health claim go to a different door than the missold mutual fund. PFRDA runs the pension architecture, the NPS and the Atal Pension Yojana. And the MCA administers company law itself, incorporation, governance, the SFIO's serious-fraud investigations. The practical art is routing: a failed broker is SEBI and the exchange; a frozen bank account is the RBI's ombudsman; an insurance dispute is IRDAI's Bima Bharosa; and a product wearing the wrong regulator's clothes, an unregistered deposit scheme sold like a bank product, is the oldest scam signal there is.

71.2Who regulates markets abroad: the SEC, Fed, CFTC and FCA?

Abroad, the same functions split along historical accident. The United States separates securities (the SEC, disclosure and markets), banking (the Fed, the OCC and the FDIC sharing the turf), and derivatives (the CFTC, futures and swaps), a fragmentation that leaves seams, as crypto's decade in the SEC-CFTC gap demonstrated. Britain runs the twin-peak model: the FCA for conduct, the Prudential Regulation Authority inside the Bank of England for solvency, a design born of the 2008 lesson that one supervisor cannot watch both behaviour and safety. Above the national level sits the soft architecture: IOSCO for securities standards, the Basel Committee for bank capital, the FSB for systemic risk, bodies with no power to command and enormous power to standardise, whose accords are why capital rules look alike from Mumbai to Munich. For the cross-border investor, the operative fact is extraterritoriality's limits: your protections generally run with the jurisdiction where the product is sold to you, and a foreign broker's failure is a foreign court's problem.

71.3How is a securities law made, from paper to enforcement?

Rules have a lifecycle, and watching one cycle teaches more than a statute book. It begins with a problem: a scandal, a market failure, a gap the last crisis exposed. The regulator publishes a consultation paper, problem, options, cost-benefit, and invites comment; industry, investors and the public respond, and the responses genuinely change the drafts, which is why the comment box is the retail investor's cheapest lobby. The board approves a regulation; the gazette notifies it; a transition period lets the market adjust. Then the machinery that gives it teeth: supervision and inspection, investigation powers, adjudication and penalties, appeals to the Securities Appellate Tribunal and beyond, and settlement by consent, with or without admission of guilt, for the cases everyone prefers to end quietly. The investor's readings: the gap between paper and enforcement is measured in years, so today's loophole is real until notified; the enforcement orders themselves are public and readable, the best free education in how the rules actually bite; and a regulation's first big case is the true test, because law untested is law unpriced.

71.4What are listing obligations and disclosure rules?

The public market's entire information order rests on one bargain: a company that takes the public's money must keep the public informed. India's framework, SEBI's listing obligations and disclosure requirements, runs on two clocks. Periodic disclosure: quarterly and annual results on a fixed schedule, shareholding patterns, related-party transactions, auditor reports, the rhythm that lets Chapter 10's reading happen at all. Event disclosure: anything material, an acquisition, a default, a regulator's raid, a key resignation, must reach the exchanges promptly, because the price cannot be fair if the facts are private. Around the disclosure duty sit the conduct rules: insider-trading prohibitions guarding unpublished price-sensitive information, takeover regulations forcing an open offer when control changes hands, so the minority exits on the acquirer's terms, and governance norms on boards and auditors. The bargain's enforcement is what separates markets: where disclosure is prompt, audited and punished when false, capital is cheap; where it is theatre, the discount never lifts.

71.5How do regulators cooperate across borders, and where does it stop?

Money crosses borders in seconds; warrants do not. Regulators cooperate through memoranda of understanding, information-sharing gateways and mutual legal assistance treaties, and the machinery works, slowly, for the cases both sides care about. The limits define the scammer's business plan: a fraud run from a jurisdiction with weak cooperation, shell companies layered through secrecy havens, assets converted to crypto and moved, presents each national regulator with a slice of the crime and none with the whole. The honest reading for the investor: recovery across borders is the exception that proves the rule, so the protection must be front-loaded, deal with entities regulated somewhere serious, because "somewhere serious" is the entire substance of your recourse. When a platform boasts registration in a jurisdiction chosen for its lenience, it is answering the question "who can hold me accountable?" with "no one you can reach", and the marketing is merely the sound of that answer being disguised.

71.6Worked examples and cases

Case 1: a SEBI enforcement order, read end to end

SEBI's orders are public, and reading one is the cheapest masterclass in market law. The anatomy of a typical insider-trading or manipulation order: the facts, a trading pattern flagged by surveillance, call records, WhatsApp chats, bank trails linking the tipper to the trader; the charges, the specific regulations invoked, framed with lawyerly precision; the defence, coincidence, public information, family relationship, each tested against the timeline; the analysis, where the order does its real work, matching trades to the information's existence with timestamps; and the directions, disgorgement of unlawful gains, penalties, market-access bans for years. What strikes the first-time reader: the evidence is circumstantial and cumulative, no smoking gun, just a hundred timestamps pointing one way; the definition of "connected person" reaches through families and firms; and the profits that seemed clever are recovered with interest and a ban attached. The order is the statute with a pulse, and an hour with two or three of them teaches the insider-trading rules better than any summary, including this one.

Case 2: how Sarbanes-Oxley followed Enron

In 2001, Enron, America's seventh-largest company and its "most innovative" for six consecutive years, revealed itself as an accounting structure: debts parked in off-balance-sheet vehicles, profits manufactured by mark-to-model, the auditor complicit, the board ornamental. It went bankrupt in weeks; WorldCom followed; Arthur Andersen, one of the five great audit firms, died of the association. The legislative response, Sarbanes-Oxley 2002, rebuilt the plumbing of trust: CEOs and CFOs personally certify the financial statements, on pain of prison; audit committees must be independent; auditors may not consult for the companies they audit, and rotate; internal controls over financial reporting are audited, not asserted. The costs were real, compliance budgets that punished the small, and the debate about them is legitimate, but the template is Section 71.3 in its purest form: a scandal names a failure, the law prices it, and the market's information order improves one notch. India ran the same loop, Satyam's 2009 confession, a billion-dollar hole announced by the founder's own letter, produced tighter governance codes and auditor accountability. Regulation is scar tissue, and the scars map exactly where the knives went in.
