Chapter 80. The Great Crises: What Each Crash Taught

Chapter 80 of 85 · about 21 minutes

Every crash is different and every crash is the same: leverage, euphoria and a story that made the price feel safe. This chapter walks the five defining crises of modern markets, not as history but as equipment, because the investor who has studied past panics owns the only vaccine the next one recognises.

80.11929 and the Great Depression

The template. The 1920s fused genuine progress, electrification, radio, the automobile, with margin debt at 10% down and an investment-trust structure that leveraged leverage itself. The Dow fell 89% from the 1929 peak to the 1932 trough, and the market's failure became the economy's: nine thousand bank failures, a quarter of the workforce idle, nominal prices not recovered for a generation. The lessons that never expire: leverage converts a decline into a liquidation, the forced seller sets the price; investment trusts and funds can hide gearing inside gearing, always ask what the vehicle owns and owes; and the policy lesson that built modern finance, deposit insurance, securities law and disclosure were born in this wreck. The investor with no debt, diversified income and a long horizon was eventually made whole and then prosperous; the leveraged one was carried out in 1932.

80.2Black Monday 1987 and the portfolio-insurance feedback loop

October 19, 1987: the Dow fell 22.6% in one day, still the largest one-day percentage fall, on no discernible news. The mechanism was the lesson: portfolio insurance, a strategy that sold index futures as prices fell to mimic a protective put, was sold to institutions as risk elimination. It was actually a crowded mechanical seller whose instructions said sell more the further prices fell, a machine that manufactured the very crash it insured against. Markets recovered within about two years, astonishingly fast, because the economy underneath was fine and the crash was plumbing, not fundamentals. The teachings: beware strategies that promise protection while requiring the same exit door as everyone else; liquidity is an illusion precisely when the crowd needs it; and a crash without a recession heals, which is why buying quality into mechanical panics has been one of history's reliable trades.

80.3The dot-com bubble and the 2000 crash

The story was true and the prices were false: the internet did change everything, and the Nasdaq still lost 78% peak to trough. India's version ran hotter and smaller, technology stocks, Himachal Futuristic, Satyam, Pentamedia at three-figure multiples, and the Nifty fell 53% from February 2000 to September 2001. The mechanisms to recognise: valuation abandoned for narrative, eyeballs and clicks replacing earnings; the catalyst of easy money, late-1990s liquidity and the IPO machine; and insider selling into retail buying, the permanent signature of tops. The durable lesson is two-sided, which is what makes it hard: the sceptics who avoided the bubble were right about prices and wrong about the technology, and the survivors, bought at sane prices later, compounded magnificently. Bubbles are paid for by the people who confuse being right about the future with being right about the price.

80.42008: the global financial crisis, full anatomy

The deepest crisis since 1929, and the most instructive because its machinery was financial. Layer one: assets, US mortgages to weak borrowers, packaged into securities stamped AAA by models that assumed house prices could not fall nationally. Layer two: leverage, investment banks at thirty-to-one, funded overnight, holding the toxic tranches they could not sell. Layer three: derivatives, AIG's credit-default swaps concentrating the risk of the whole system on one unregulated balance sheet. When prices fell, the chain ran backwards: margin calls, fire sales, counterparty panic, Lehman, and a global credit freeze that only state guarantees stopped. India, insulated by boring banks and capital controls, still saw the Sensex fall 61%. The anatomy teaches in order: model risk, the AAA lie; funding risk, long assets on short money; counterparty risk, your safety depends on others' solvency; and the meta-lesson, every "this time the risk is engineered away" is the crisis rehearsing.

80.52020: the pandemic crash and the fastest recovery

The first crisis in this hall that arrived from outside finance entirely. A virus shut the world economy in weeks; the Sensex fell 38% in five weeks, the fastest major fall in its history; and then the recovery: fiscal transfers and central-bank liquidity at a scale 2008 only imagined, vaccines within a year, and the index doubled in eighteen months. The investor who sold at the bottom, Chapter 77's case study, locked in the only permanent loss of the episode. The lessons: exogenous shocks without financial rot heal fastest, there was no leverage mountain to unwind; policy response is now a first-order market force, the liquidity flood repriced everything, including the mania in small caps and F&O that followed; and the plan must survive the unfathomable, because no one's scenario list in January 2020 said "the world stops by March". Diversification, cash buffers and no leverage are not return strategies; they are survival strategies, and survival is the prerequisite for everything else.

80.6Worked example: one portfolio carried through each crisis

The chart carries the same ₹10 lakh of equities to the peak of each crisis and marks the worst point.

The numbers, and what a surviving investor experienced

1929-32: ₹10 lakh becomes ₹1.1 lakh, an 89% loss, and the index does not set a new nominal high for about twenty-five years, the one crisis where "wait" was a multi-decade sentence, the argument for rebalancing into bonds and never holding a pure leveraged equity book. 1987: ₹10 lakh becomes ₹6.65 lakh, recovered in about two years, mechanical panic, quick healing. 2000-02 (Nifty): ₹4.7 lakh, roughly three years to recover, the valuation crash, and the new-economy leaders of 1999 never led again. 2008 (Sensex): ₹3.9 lakh, back to the old peak in about two and three-quarter years; the investor who kept the SIP running through 2008-09 bought the recovery at half price. 2020 (Sensex): ₹6.15 lakh, recovered in about seven months, then doubled. Three readings. One: depth and duration are different risks, 1929 was survivable in price and brutal in time. Two: recovery has always come, but the waiting period is the price of the equity premium, paid in years. Three: the variable that decided every outcome was the investor, the leveraged or panicked one was ruined in crashes that the patient one barely remembers.
