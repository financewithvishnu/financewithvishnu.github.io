Agro-processing and basmati: the paddy, the warehouse and the brand

By Finance With Vishnu · Source period: September-October 2024

Nobody in this sector sells rice. They sell the ability to buy a harvest that arrives once a year, the patience to store a grain that improves with time, and the brand that makes a shopper reach past twenty identical bags. Three different businesses hide inside the phrase "rice company", and they differ in the only way that matters to an investor: how much working capital the model eats, and what the extra rupee of inventory earns.

India's basmati trade is a real export franchise with policy risk attached. FY2024-25 shows what that means on a company P&L: steady revenues, margins squeezed by paddy costs and freight, and balance sheets where the warehouse matters more than the factory. Follow the paddy, the warehouse and the brand.

Source period: September-October 2024. FY2024-25 annual reports provide dated checks. They are not current data. This is education, not investment advice.

What does a basmati company actually sell?

Start with the grain. Basmati paddy arrives in one harvest, roughly October to December. In those weeks a company must buy a large part of its year's raw material, in mandis, with cash or short-term credit. From that single decision flow three different businesses.

The first is commodity rice: buy paddy, mill it, sell it fast, earn the spread between the paddy price and the rice price. The second is aged rice: hold the grain for a year or two while its aroma and cooking quality improve, and earn a premium for patience. The third is the brand: put the rice in a bag a consumer recognises, in a shop she trusts, and earn a margin that has little to do with the paddy market.

Every basmati company runs some mix of these five steps. The mix, not the sector label, decides the economics.

Why does procurement decide the year?

In most manufacturing, a bad quarter of buying can be fixed in the next quarter. In basmati, the raw material window opens once. Buy too dear in November and the cost sits in inventory for a year or more. Buy too little and the mills run below capacity for the rest of the year.

This is why the balance sheet of a rice company is really a procurement diary. KRBL, the India Gate company, closed FY2024-25 with inventories of about Rs 3,885 crore against revenue of about Rs 5,594 crore. Nearly seventy paise of inventory for every rupee of annual sales is not inefficiency; it is the business model. The company buys in the harvest, holds, and sells through the year and beyond.

Procurement skill shows up years later. The paddy bought in the winter of one year becomes the aged rice sold at a premium two years on. A company that consistently buys good paddy at fair prices builds a compounding advantage that a competitor cannot copy in one season, no matter how much it spends. Scale deepens the edge: a large buyer with cash and storage can wait for the right mandi and the right moisture, while a small buyer must take what the market offers on the day.

What does the paddy-to-rice spread pay for?

The commodity end of the business lives on the spread between paddy and rice. Out of a quintal of paddy, a mill recovers a share as saleable rice; the rest is husk, bran and broken grains that sell for much less. Milling recovery and the share of broken grains decide whether the spread covers conversion cost.

This spread is thin and cyclical. When the harvest is short, paddy prices rise faster than rice prices and the miller's margin compresses. When the harvest is ample, the spread widens. A pure commodity player is a spread trader with a factory attached, and its profits will swing with the crop even when volumes look stable.

Read a rice company's gross margin trend before its revenue growth. KRBL's FY2024-25 gross margin fell to about 25.5% from about 27.0% the year before, on higher material costs, even as revenue grew. That one line tells you more about the year than the topline did.

Why would anyone store rice for two years?

Freshly milled basmati is good; aged basmati is better. Time in the warehouse reduces moisture, firms the grain and deepens the aroma, and buyers pay more for it. Ageing is the sector's quietest and most misunderstood business: the warehouse is a slow factory that runs on interest cost instead of electricity.

The economics are a trade. Inventory carries a funding cost, a storage cost and a risk of damage or price decline. Against that stands the price premium aged rice commands. When the premium exceeds the carrying cost, the warehouse compounds value. When paddy prices fall after the company has stocked up, the same inventory writes the year's profit warning.

KRBL's numbers show both sides. Inventories fell from about Rs 4,451 crore to about Rs 3,885 crore during FY2024-25, partly because the per-unit carrying cost of paddy was lower, while EBITDA margin compressed from about 16.4% to about 13.0%. The warehouse had helped in earlier years when the cycle ran the other way.

What does a brand buy that a mill cannot?

A mill sells rice; a brand sells the confidence that the next bag will cook like the last one. That confidence is expensive to build and cheap to lose, and it is the only durable answer to the spread cycle.

LT Foods is the clearest case. Its FY2024-25 consolidated revenue of about Rs 8,681 crore, up 11.7%, came with the company crossing the USD 1 billion revenue mark, built on Daawat in India and Royal in the United States, backed by sustained advertising, including a Shah Rukh Khan campaign for Daawat, and steady distribution expansion. Net profit of Rs 612 crore on that base tells you the brand earns a margin the commodity mill never sees.

The test of a rice brand is not the advertisement. It is whether the company can raise prices when paddy costs rise and keep the customer. A brand that passes on cost with a lag earns a steadier margin through the cycle; a brand that cannot is a commodity business with a marketing budget. LT Foods spoke openly about strategic pricing actions during FY2024-25 while profits held; that is what pricing power looks like in a cost-up year.

Why do exports pay differently?

Basmati's natural home market is the Middle East, where the grain is a staple of celebration cooking. KRBL's exports grew 10.7% in FY2024-25 to about Rs 1,473 crore, and GRM ranks among the top five global basmati exporters, with about Rs 783 crore of international revenue, a MENA stronghold and a growing US and UK presence.

Export economics differ from domestic in three ways. Realisations can be better, but receivables run longer, so the exporter lends to its buyer for months. Freight and insurance sit between the mill and the buyer, and a freight spike lands directly in the margin, as KRBL's management noted for FY2024-25. And policy is a live variable: minimum export prices and shipment rules have changed before, and each change reprices the trade overnight.

The healthiest exporters treat the Middle East as a distribution franchise built over decades, not a tender to be won at any price. Watch export receivable days alongside export growth; growth bought by stretching credit is the commodity spread wearing a nicer label.

How does working capital decide who survives?

Add the pieces together: a harvest that must be bought in one window, a warehouse that holds value for one to two years, and export customers who pay late. The basmati business is a working-capital machine, and the companies that survive are the ones that fund it conservatively.

KRBL runs a debt-equity ratio of about 0.08 and a current ratio above 6, with interest cover near 45 times. That is a balance sheet built to carry the warehouse through a bad spread year without flinching. The cost of that caution is a lower return on equity in good years; the benefit is that the company is still standing, still buying the harvest, when leveraged rivals are selling inventory into weakness.

When you read a rice company, read the funding before the growth. Growth financed by short-term debt against slow inventory is the classic way this sector produces a blow-up.

Why keep both loose rice and branded rice?

Most large players run both models side by side, and that is not indecision. Loose and private-label rice keeps the mills full and the procurement machine fed; the brand earns the margin on top of that base. Volume buys relevance with farmers and traders, and the brand converts a slice of that volume into pricing power.

The danger is confusion inside the P&L. A company can show rising revenue while quietly shifting the mix back towards commodity volume, which flatters the topline and taxes the margin. The reverse shift, from bulk to branded, does the opposite. When a basmati company reports, the first question is not how much it sold, but which bag it sold.

Where do the new consumer bets fit?

The large players are pushing beyond plain rice. LT Foods describes a consumer-brands strategy across geographies, and GRM is explicit about travelling from a leading basmati exporter towards a consumer-focused FMCG player, with its international business already contributing about 59% of revenue.

The logic is sound: the mill and the warehouse are spread businesses, while the brand compounds. But adjacency has a cost curve. New categories, new countries and new shelves all spend money before they earn it, and the payoff depends on distribution muscle that takes years to build. Judge these bets on whether branded revenue and branded margin rise together, not on the announcement.

What can break the economics?

Three risks sit on top of this sector. First, the crop: a short or expensive paddy harvest squeezes every downstream margin at once. Second, policy: export rules and minimum prices have changed abruptly, and no brand premium fully protects an exporter from a closed window. Third, the warehouse itself: inventory bought high must eventually be sold, and a falling paddy market turns the ageing bet into a write-down.

The mitigants are the same three businesses in better proportions. A strong brand passes on cost. Conservative funding survives the squeeze. A disciplined buyer earns the next cycle. The companies that treat basmati as a trading business get trading results; the ones that treat it as a slow consumer franchise get something rarer.

How should you read the annual report?

Four dated checks carry most of the information. Gross margin tells you what the paddy spread did this year. Inventory, set against revenue, tells you how large the ageing bet is and how it is moving. Export receivable days tell you whether export growth is being bought with credit. And the split between branded and bulk revenue tells you which of the three businesses is actually growing.

Where does the wider agro-processing frame fit?

Basmati is the cleanest case study, but the same lens travels across agro-processing. Any business that buys a seasonal harvest, stores or transforms it, and sells to a fickle consumer faces the same three questions: how well does it buy, what does it earn for holding, and does a brand let it escape the spread? Sugar, edible oils, spices and packaged staples are all variations on that frame, with different holding periods and different policy shadows.

The seasoning changes; the arithmetic does not. Wherever inventory is large relative to sales, the warehouse is a business decision, not a storage detail. Wherever the raw material arrives in a window, procurement is a year's profit decided in a quarter. And wherever the product is hard to tell apart at the shelf, the brand is the only exit from the commodity spread.

What should you remember?

A basmati company is three businesses in one bag. The commodity mill earns a thin spread and takes crop risk. The warehouse earns a premium for patience and takes funding risk. The brand earns pricing power and takes years to build. The sector's winners blend all three deliberately.

So when a rice company reports, do not ask only how much it sold. Ask what the paddy cost, what the warehouse earned, and which bag the customer bought. In this sector, the quiet disciplines of buying well, storing patiently and funding conservatively decide the decade long before the year is reported.
