Jewellery retail: the gold, the making charge and the trust

By Finance With Vishnu · Source period: July 2025

A jewellery shop looks like a retail business and behaves like a bank wearing a showroom. The shelves hold the most expensive inventory in Indian retail, most of it borrowed, all of it priced twice a day off a global metal quote the jeweller does not control. The product is not really the ornament. The product is the trust that lets a family hand over its old bangles and walk out with new ones.

Three numbers organise the whole business: how the gold on the shelf is funded, how much of the bill is the jeweller's own value add, and how fast the metal converts back into cash. FY2024-25 tested all three at once. Gold rose roughly 25% year on year in every quarter, the Budget cut the import duty from 15% to 6% mid-year, and the listed players still grew revenue between 16% and 35%. Follow the gold, the making charge and the trust.

Source period: July 2025. FY2024-25 annual reports provide dated checks. They are not current data. This is education, not investment advice.

What does a jeweller actually sell?

Strip a jewellery bill down and three things are being sold at the same counter. The first is metal, priced at the day's rate per gram, which the jeweller buys and resells almost at pass-through. The second is workmanship: the making charge, the part of the bill that is actually the jeweller's product. The third is assurance: certified purity, a buy-back promise and a name the family already trusts with its savings.

This mix explains why jewellery retail has such strange economics. Revenue per store is enormous because most of the bill is metal. Margin on that revenue is thin because the metal earns the jeweller almost nothing. And the balance sheet is heavy because the metal must be bought before it is sold. Titan's Jewellery Division reported revenue of Rs 46,571 crore in FY2024-25, up 21%, with same-store growth of 14%.[1] Very few retail formats anywhere grow a Rs 46,000 crore base at 21%.

Why is jewellery inventory unlike any other retail inventory?

In most retail, inventory falls in value every day it sits unsold. In jewellery, the inventory is the commodity itself. A necklace that sits in the vault for a year is still gold, and in FY2024-25 it was worth a great deal more at the end than at the start. This inverts the usual retail worry: the risk is not obsolescence, it is price. The same inventory that gains 25% in a good year can fall sharply, and when it falls the jeweller still owes the same money to the bank.

That is why the inventory line on a jeweller's balance sheet must always be read together with the borrowing line. Senco's inventory rose 34% to Rs 32,992 million in FY2024-25 while total borrowings rose 18% to Rs 17,690 million, most of it to stock new stores and to deepen existing ones.[3] Growth in this business is literally bought by the gram.

How does the price of a necklace come apart?

A jewellery bill is transparent by law and by custom, and every line on it teaches something. The metal is charged at the day's rate multiplied by the weight, adjusted for purity. The making charge is added on top, either per gram or as a percentage of metal value. Stones, if any, are charged separately. GST of 3% applies on the whole. The jeweller's economics live almost entirely in the making charge and in the small spread between the buying and selling rate of the metal itself.

This is why a jeweller can post Rs 25,045 crore of revenue, as Kalyan did in FY2024-25, and keep Rs 714 crore as profit.[2] The metal is a pass-through. The business is the few rupees in every hundred that pay for design, manufacturing, the showroom and the trust.

Who pays for the gold sitting in the showroom?

A mid-sized showroom can carry crores of inventory, and somebody's money has to sit inside it. Three sources share that load. The cheapest is the customer's: old gold exchanged and advance schemes put metal or money in before a sale. The second is the bank's: working-capital limits and gold metal loans fund the bulk. The third, the most expensive and the smallest, is the promoter's own equity, which absorbs the shocks the other two will not.

Watch how deliberately the listed players push toward borrowed metal. Senco ended FY2024-25 with gold metal loans at 67% of total borrowings and a stated target of 75%.[3] Kalyan reported GML of Rs 10,911 million within total debt of Rs 24,152 million, and tracks net debt with and without GML separately, 2.18 times equity with it and 0.02 times without.[2] When a company reports leverage two ways, it is telling you that metal borrowing is not ordinary debt.

What exactly is a gold metal loan?

A gold metal loan is a loan of gold, not of money. A bank hands the jeweller physical gold, the jeweller works it into inventory, and repays the same quantity of gold at the end of the term. The jeweller never carries the metal's price risk on that portion: if gold rises, the inventory is worth more but so is the repayment; if it falls, both sides of the ledger fall together. The jeweller pays a fee and interest, and the quantity of gold is the hedge.

Because repayment is in kind, GML is as much a risk-management tool as a funding tool. It matches the asset (gold inventory) to the liability (gold owed) in the same unit. That match is exactly what an ordinary rupee working-capital loan cannot do, and it is why jewellers want GML to be as large a share of their funding as banks will allow.

How do jewellers protect themselves from the gold price?

What GML does not cover must be hedged another way. The standard tool is the MCX gold future: sell futures against the inventory, so a fall in the metal price earns on the hedge what it loses on the shelf. Titan disclosed a total gold commodity exposure of Rs 9,392.85 crore as of 31 March 2025, hedged under a Board-approved Bullion Risk Management Policy with a Bullion Risk Management Committee reviewing the position every quarter.[1] Senco runs the same pairing of GML and MCX cover, and was named MCX's best hedger in the bullion segment for the second time.[3]

The discipline matters more than the instrument. Hedging removes the bet on the gold price so that the business earns the making charge and nothing else. An unhedged jeweller is a gold speculator with a showroom attached; sometimes a lucky one.

What happened when the Budget cut the customs duty?

The July 2024 Budget cut the import duty on gold from 15% to 6%, a nine-percentage-point drop in the tax embedded in every gram of landed metal. Domestic gold prices fell by roughly the duty difference almost overnight. Every jeweller holding inventory bought under the old duty regime was suddenly holding metal that the market repriced lower, and FY2024-25 annual reports carry the scar tissue line by line.

Senco quantified a one-time loss of Rs 574.2 million on existing inventory backed by GML, absorbed into cost of goods sold across two quarters, while its MCX-hedged inventory escaped without loss. Kalyan disclosed a one-time inventory impact of about Rs 1,240 million and still grew consolidated revenue 35%. Titan's net profit declined 6% to Rs 3,335 crore, with the company naming the duty reduction as a factor.[1][2][3] One regulatory line item moved the profit of the entire listed industry in a single quarter. That is what metal pass-through with thin margins means in practice.

Why do making charges decide the profit pool?

If the metal is a pass-through, the industry's entire profit pool sits in the making charge and in the design premium on studded and branded pieces. Plain gold bangles carry single-digit making charges; intricate, studded and branded collections carry several times that. This is why every large jeweller talks about its studded ratio, its wedding collections and its light-weight modern lines. Each is an attempt to sell more workmanship per gram of metal.

The same logic explains the discounting wars. Making charges are the only lever a competitor can cut without touching the metal rate, so they are the first casualty of competitive intensity. Titan noted muted studded growth of 12% in FY2024-25 as high-value solitaires weakened, while Senco saw gross margin slip from 15.3% to 14.8%, citing the duty impact and mix changes amid heavy discounting across the industry.[1][3] When making charges compress, nothing else in the model can rescue the margin.

Why does the old-gold exchange counter matter so much?

A striking share of Indian jewellery demand is not new money at all. Kalyan's annual report describes gold exchange as contributing nearly a third of industry revenue: the customer brings old ornaments, the jeweller tests and values them on the spot, and the exchange funds most of a new purchase.[2] The jeweller melts the old metal back into inventory. It is the cheapest working capital in the business, and it doubles as a demand engine: high gold prices make the old bangle in the locker worth more, which brings the customer to the counter.

The exchange counter is also where trust is tested in public. Titan describes Karatmeters installed in showrooms to verify purity in real time, the exchange valuation conducted in front of the customer, and every piece BIS hallmarked.[1] A jeweller who handles the exchange honestly earns the family for a generation. One who cheats it loses the neighbourhood.

Why does trust still beat scale in this business?

For decades the local family jeweller held the market because buying gold is an act of faith: the buyer cannot test purity and must believe the seller. Mandatory BIS hallmarking, now with a unique identification code on every piece, turned that faith into a verifiable standard, and the listed chains built their expansion on making the verification visible. Transparent billing, karatmeter checks and exchange-in-front-of-customer are not service features. They are the product.

Yet scale arrives slowly. Titan reached 503 Tanishq stores; Kalyan operates 388 showrooms plus 1,037 grassroots My Kalyan stores; Senco runs 175.[1][2][3] The unorganised sector still dominates nationally because trust is local, wedding-driven and built over decades. The formalisation wave is real, but it converts trust into market share one community at a time, which is why the chains spend below 2% of sales on marketing and still grow on word of mouth and wedding referrals.

What do the FY2024-25 numbers actually show?

Put the three listed players side by side and the model's logic appears in the figures. Revenue growth was strong everywhere, powered by the rising gold price and new stores. Profit moved the other way at the largest player, because a duty cut and volatile prices squeeze the thin value-added slice even while the metal inflates the top line. And every one of them runs the same funding machine: heavy inventory, heavy metal-backed borrowing, and hedging discipline around it.

What can break a jewellery retailer's economics?

Four breaks matter. The first is an unhedged metal position, which converts a retail margin into a commodity bet and can erase a year of making-charge profit in one quarter. The second is the funding itself: metal loans and working capital are short-dated, so a credit squeeze hits the inventory directly. Senco's finance costs rose as average borrowings climbed from Rs 13,388 million to Rs 16,337 million, and its interest cover stood at 3.5 times.[3] The third is dilution of the trust asset: a purity scandal or a cheating exchange counter destroys the one moat the business has. The fourth is dilution of returns through expansion: Senco's ROE fell from 15.7% to 9.6% as fresh QIP equity and new-store inventory outran earnings.[3]

Notice what is not on the list: the gold price itself. A disciplined, fully hedged jeweller can grow through a 25% rally or a duty-cut crash. What it cannot survive is running the showroom as a metal bet or the balance sheet as if equity were free.

What would make us walk away?

The walkaway test writes itself from the model. Walk away when management cannot say how much of the inventory is hedged, or reports leverage one way when it suits the story. Walk away when growth comes from discounting making charges, because the company is selling its only real product cheaper. Walk away when inventory balloons without a matching metal-backed funding line, when receivables appear in a cash business, or when hallmarking and exchange practices are described vaguely. And walk away from any pitch that rests on the gold price rising; that is a view on the metal, not a case for the retailer.

The keepers are the mirror image: hedging disclosed and disciplined, making-charge economics defended by design and brand, exchange and hallmarking run as visible trust machines, and new stores funded so that returns survive the build-out. In jewellery retail the metal is borrowed, the margins are thin and the customer is trusting you with her family's savings. The business that respects all three facts is the one worth studying further.
