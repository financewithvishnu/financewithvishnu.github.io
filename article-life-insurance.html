Life insurance: separate the promise, the model and the cash

By Finance With Vishnu · Source period: September 2021; exact class date unconfirmed

A life insurer can sell more policies, report a higher new-business margin and still need additional capital. Its accounting profit can lag the value it estimates it has created. That estimated value can rise without the same amount becoming available for dividends.

These are different views of a business making long promises. The challenge is to connect them, rather than choose whichever measure tells the most attractive story.

I would begin with the product and customer, follow the distribution and renewals, and only then read the actuarial value and the price paid for it. A favourable demographic story cannot substitute for that work.

1. What promise does the product make?

Death protection, savings and retirement income solve different customer problems. Term protection pays on specified insured events during the cover period. Savings products combine benefits and life cover in different ways. Annuities can protect against outliving assets by paying income under the contract.

Participating products allow policyholders to participate in declared surplus or bonuses on the relevant basis. Non-participating savings products can include defined guarantees. Unit-linked products connect fund value to underlying investments, but remain insurance contracts with charges, cover and product-specific conditions.

Do not assume every endowment is non-participating or every linked product is free of guarantee risk. HDFC Life's FY2025 report separately describes participating, non-participating, linked, protection and retirement offerings, including linked variants with capital guarantees. The exact product terms matter more than a loose category label. [1]

The premium payer, insured life and benefit recipient can be different people or entities. Before comparing economics, identify the event, benefit, duration, guarantee, surrender terms and who bears investment risk. Premium alone does not tell us how much financial protection was sold.

2. Is premium growth the same as better protection?

No. Premium divided by GDP measures insurance penetration, but savings intensity and product mix strongly influence it. Policy counts are not unique insured lives because one person can hold several contracts.

A large savings premium can produce less death cover than a much smaller term premium. More premium can therefore reflect ticket size, savings demand or single-premium business rather than equivalent growth in protection.

For sector demand, distinguish income, household liabilities, protection adequacy, retirement needs and affordability. For a company, follow new and renewal premium, sum assured, product mix, customer cohorts and distribution.

A long opportunity can still be cyclical. Equity markets affect demand for linked savings; interest rates affect competing savings choices and guarantee economics; household income affects renewals. Regulation can alter incentives and product design.

The opportunity is to write useful, appropriately priced promises at attractive returns on capital. A large protection gap does not tell us which insurer earns the value, how quickly customers can pay or what multiple investors should pay today.

3. Why use APE instead of raw new premium?

Annualised premium equivalent, or APE, makes a convention-based adjustment between regular and single-premium new business. In the referenced ICICI Prudential glossary, it is annualised first-year regular premiums plus 10% of single premiums, covering the stated individual and group scope. It is not total premium including old renewals. [2]

Retail APE, total APE, new-business premium and total premium answer different questions. Total weighted received premium can include renewal premiums while APE concerns new business. State the exact definition before comparing growth or costs.

A large single-premium sale can lift cash premium much more than APE. A product-mix shift can do the reverse. Reconcile the bridge instead of describing either growth rate as inherently more truthful.

4. What is value of new business?

Value of new business, or VNB, estimates the additional shareholder value created by contracts written during the period, using projected future cash flows and the disclosed risk and cost allowances. It is not accounting revenue or cash profit already earned.

The actuarial principles include expected renewals of those new contracts in their value. That differs from including future customers who have not yet bought policies. The latter belong to the future franchise, not the existing new-business cohort. [3]

VNB margin is VNB divided by the stated APE base. Product design, acquisition costs, distribution, mortality, reinsurance, persistency, guarantees and capital all affect it. A higher margin is not automatically a safer product or a better customer outcome.

Suppose APE falls from 100 to 80 while margin rises from 25% to 30%. VNB falls from 25 to 24. An attractive margin chart can coexist with lower absolute value creation.

Ask how much of a change came from business mix, pricing, channel economics, actual productivity or revised assumptions. Model improvements and realised operating improvements should not be given the same evidential weight.

5. Why can sales growth depress accounting profit?

Acquisition expenses, commissions and reserve requirements can create new-business strain before renewal margins emerge. Economic value may be modelled over many years while accounting recognition follows the applicable reporting framework.

This does not mean every expense for a long policy is paid in year one. Future service, claims and other costs still need inclusion. Nor does it make weak PAT irrelevant: accounting earnings, cash and capital offer checks on the actuarial story.

A stripped-down timing sketch might collect premium of 100, incur acquisition and administration of 40, claims of 10 and a reserve addition of 65, giving a result of minus 15 before omitted items. That is not a complete actuarial model and establishes no particular VNB.

To decide whether strain is productive, inspect the policies generating it, expected renewal economics, capital consumption and later actual experience. Aggressive growth can consume capital even when management reports positive VNB.

Compare statutory accounts, cash flows and actuarial disclosures without forcing them into one number. Each measures a different part of the same obligations.

6. What does embedded value include?

Embedded value, or EV, estimates shareholder interests in covered existing business and its allocated assets after the relevant risk allowances. The useful teaching equation is adjusted net worth plus value of in-force business.

Adjusted net worth includes free surplus and required capital. Value of in-force includes projected shareholder profits from the covered book after appropriate deductions, including the disclosed treatment of options, guarantees, capital friction and non-hedgeable risks. SBI Life's FY2025 EV report explains these components explicitly. [4]

The Institute of Actuaries of India's referenced principles explicitly exclude future new business from EV. They also require the covered business and risk allowances to be understood. [3]

EV is neither statutory book equity nor the value of all future franchise activity. A multiple above one may reflect expectations for future sales, but also disagreement with existing-book assumptions, capital efficiency and other factors.

7. Why read the EV movement before the growth headline?

The bridge separates expected return or unwind, VNB, operating experience, operating assumption changes, economic effects and shareholder capital movements. Closing EV growth mixes these sources.

A contemporary example makes the distinction tangible. ICICI Prudential's FY2021 presentation reports opening EV of ₹230.30 billion, EV operating profit of ₹35.05 billion, positive economic/investment variance of ₹25.67 billion and net capital injection of ₹0.04 billion, producing closing EV of ₹291.06 billion. Operating return was about 15.2%, while closing EV grew about 26.4%. These are historical company figures, not forecasts. [2]

Favourable markets should not be relabelled superior recurring operating performance. Equally, capital raised from shareholders should not be presented as value created for them. Read dividends and dilution beside the absolute EV increase.

8. Which assumptions deserve the most attention?

Mortality, morbidity, persistency, expenses, economic inputs, taxes, guarantees and capital determine projected shareholder cash flows. The importance and direction vary by product.

Lower mortality can reduce death-protection claims while longer survival increases lifetime annuity payments. More persistency can preserve profitable fees or keep a costly guarantee in force. Surrender can release reserves while creating a liquidity payment.

Experience variance measures actual results against prior expectations for the elapsed period. An assumption change revises the future model. Repeated favourable assumption changes without supporting experience deserve investigation.

SBI Life's FY2025 report presents one-factor sensitivities and explains its expense and capital assumptions. Its independent actuarial opinion has a stated review scope. Such review is useful, but not a promise that future experience will match the model. [4]

Sensitivity rows are not additive forecasts. Several adverse events can interact, and different books react differently. The question is what causes the sensitivity and how management can respond, not simply which company prints the smallest percentage.

9. Is a lower discount rate always more conservative?

No. First identify what is being discounted: a policy liability or shareholder profit. The same present-value arithmetic has different implications for the two.

Reserve-valuation interest rates are not interchangeable with EV reference curves. Guarantees, bonus assumptions, expenses, mortality and backing assets also differ. A single rate cannot establish that one insurer is universally more conservative.

The actuarial principles require risk allowances to work together, including options, guarantees, capital and discounting. Counting only an attractive assumption while ignoring offsetting choices can give false reassurance. [3]

Long guarantees need asset-liability analysis. Reinvestment at lower yields, credit losses, duration mismatch or large surrenders can matter even when the original policy looked profitable.

10. Who owns the distribution relationship?

Agents, bank partners, direct channels and brokers connect insurers to customers. Distribution can create scale and trust, but can also create commission costs and dependence on a powerful partner.

HDFC Life's FY2025 report describes both proprietary agency/direct channels and bank and other financial partnerships. That is a business architecture to investigate, not proof that a bank-backed insurer faces no acquisition or concentration risk. [1]

Measure active-agent productivity, conversion, branch output, acquisition cost, renewals and product suitability. A large registered agent count or branch network is capacity, not necessarily productive sales.

Partner access can be shared, repriced or withdrawn under the actual arrangements. Read current contracts and rules for a current decision; this historical framework does not assert today's partnership limits or ownership stakes.

A broker earns distribution and service economics rather than the insurer's mortality and investment spread. Premium passing through a platform is not its revenue or underwriting float. Do not apply an insurer's EV multiple to a distributor merely because both sell the same policy.

11. What do renewals and service reveal?

Persistency follows how much of an issued cohort remains in force or renews at specified durations. It can be weighted by premium or measured by policy count. The basis, exclusions, product mix and cohorts must accompany the number.

A 13th-month figure broadly tests early renewal, while a 61st-month figure looks much further into a policy's life. A table of both durations in one reporting year usually concerns different issue cohorts. It is not a single cohort losing customers within that one year.

Premium-weighted persistency can improve because larger policies renew better, even if the number of customers retained does not improve. The referenced ICICI glossary and presentation distinguish premium measures and persistency bases. [2]

Claims settlement by count also differs from settlement by value and timing. Complaints per new policy differ from complaints per total in-force policies. Customer outcomes need matched definitions and context rather than a single headline ranking.

Good service can support renewals and trust, but the economic effect must be tested. A temporarily low expense ratio achieved by neglecting claims or servicing may damage future value.

12. What can prevent modelled value becoming cash?

Capital requirements, adverse experience, guarantee costs, asset losses, poor renewals and expensive growth can all reduce or delay distributions. Much of the investment portfolio backs policyholder obligations, not freely distributable shareholder assets.

Solvency, liquidity and duration matching answer different questions. A strong point-in-time solvency ratio does not guarantee that a book can fund a surrender spike without selling assets at an unattractive price.

The 2024 regulatory circular separately addresses actuarial reports, valuation, asset-liability management and financial-condition reporting. That separation is a useful reminder that one capital ratio cannot replace the whole risk assessment. This is a later framework reference, not a complete current compliance opinion. [5]

Reinsurance can transfer specified mortality or other risks and alter capital needs. It introduces treaty, counterparty and recovery-timing questions. Swiss Re's life-and-health material describes biometric and structured reinsurance capabilities; the particular contract determines what is actually transferred. No reinsurer's scale removes the need to inspect terms. [6]

Follow free-surplus generation, required-capital movement, dividends and new equity together. Value that repeatedly requires fresh shareholder funding must be assessed per diluted share.

13. What does the price assume?

Price/EV is a starting comparison, not a complete valuation. It combines views on the existing book, future sales, margins, capital efficiency, risk and the discount rate investors demand.

Align the listed security with the EV actually owned. A holding-company interest in an insurer is not the whole subsidiary: parent debt, minorities, other assets, tax, capital calls and distribution rights remain relevant.

Multiple risk can overpower operating growth. If EV per share grows 15% annually for three years but price/EV falls from 5x to 3x, the price factor is 1.15³ × 3/5, or about 0.913 before dividends. That is roughly an 8.7% cumulative loss despite strong EV growth. It is an illustration, not a market forecast.

Build the research sheet around the promise, distribution, APE and absolute VNB, persistency, EV movement, assumptions, assets and capital. Test weaker growth, higher expenses, adverse mortality, guarantee pressure and a lower valuation multiple.

The strongest conclusion is not that life insurance is automatically a compounder. It is that a durable franchise must turn well-priced long promises into shareholder cash, with enough resilience to survive the assumptions being wrong.
