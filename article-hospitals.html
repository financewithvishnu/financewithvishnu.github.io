Hospitals: beds create capacity, care creates the business

By Finance With Vishnu · Source period: 2022-era; exact class date unconfirmed

A hospital can add beds, increase revenue and report a higher operating margin while still earning a disappointing return on new capital. The missing pieces are often the age of those beds, the money invested around them and the time needed to build a clinical franchise.

I would begin with the hospital, not the sector multiple. Who brings patients? Which services can the team safely deliver? Who pays the bill, how long does collection take and what capital must remain in the business?

Healthcare demand is a need. Profitable hospital demand also requires access, affordability, staff, equipment and a reliable care system. Those are related ideas, not identical ones.

1. What exactly does the listed business operate?

A hospital group may combine inpatient hospitals, outpatient clinics, diagnostics, pharmacies, day-care centres, training and digital services. It may also operate across countries with different currencies, payers and regulations.

These activities do not deserve one assumed margin or capital requirement. A pharmacy can have substantial revenue but a different operating margin from a hospital. A digital service can be growing while still consuming the cash generated by the established network.

Apollo's FY2025 report separates healthcare services and HealthCo in its business discussion and warns through its historical metric notes that reporting scope has changed across years. Its operating-bed chart includes owned and managed hospitals. A group total therefore needs a scope note before it becomes a peer comparison. [1]

First draw the business boundary. Identify ownership, consolidation, geography and minority interests. Then ask which segment generates cash and which segment needs it. App registrations, hospital count and pharmacy sales are not substitutes for attributable earnings.

2. Why are all beds not the same denominator?

Capacity beds describe potential physical capacity under the company's definition. Operational or staffed beds describe a narrower service capability. Occupied bed-days measure actual use over time. A bed added late in a year is not available for the whole year.

Aster's nine-month FY2025 presentation is a useful warning against mixing these numbers. It separately reports capacity, operational census beds, operational non-census beds and available capacity. Its occupancy note uses operational census beds. Dividing occupied beds by the biggest capacity total would answer a different question. [2]

Day-care and non-bed services can complicate the bridge further. A lower overnight bed count does not necessarily mean fewer procedures or less care. Read the definitions before building a productivity model.

3. How do occupancy, ARPOB and ALOS connect?

Period occupancy is occupied bed-days divided by available operational bed-days on the same basis. Average revenue per occupied bed, usually called ARPOB, divides a specified revenue measure by occupied bed-days. Average length of stay, or ALOS, relates patient days to a matched patient population, commonly discharged inpatients.

The revenue scope is essential. ARPOB is not necessarily just the room charge or even a pure inpatient-revenue measure. Max's FY2025 presentation defines its measure using gross revenue divided by total occupied bed-days, with Max Lab revenue excluded. Do not silently apply a narrower formula to that disclosed number. [3]

Real admissions cross reporting cut-offs, and day-care, packages and revenue recognition need reconciliation. The identities organise a model; they do not eliminate the need to read the footnotes.

Also check the time unit. A full-year revenue-per-operating-bed measure expressed in millions cannot be compared directly with rupees per occupied bed-day.

4. Does a shorter stay automatically raise revenue?

No. In the example, reducing average stay from four days to three at the same 1,800 occupied bed-days increases the approximate number of completed stays from 450 to 600. Revenue remains ₹5.4 crore if the assumed revenue per bed-day is unchanged.

Revenue can change if procedure mix, package prices, outpatient activity, occupied days or other factors change. More throughput is a possibility, not an extra revenue line that can be added without considering those mechanics.

Capacity release is useful only when there is appropriate demand, staffing and support for the next patient. A shorter stay can also reflect different cases rather than operational improvement. A longer stay may be medically necessary.

I would never use ALOS as a standalone care target. Readmissions, infections, outcomes, patient experience and clinical appropriateness need their own evidence. NABH's hospital programme and sixth-edition standards focus on safety, patient-centred care and quality improvement, not merely financial utilisation. [6]

Full occupancy is not automatically ideal either. Aggregate averages can conceal congestion in one specialty and idle capacity in another.

5. What is behind a rising ARPOB?

It could be pricing, but it could also be a shift toward more complex procedures, a different payer mix, day-care revenue, geography or a changed denominator. Higher reported realisation does not prove a higher price for identical treatment.

Complex services can require scarce clinicians, expensive equipment, intensive support and higher maintenance. Revenue and cost must be examined together. The most expensive service is not automatically the best capital-return opportunity.

Payer mix matters too. Self-pay, insured, corporate, international and government-scheme categories can differ in negotiated rates, approvals, deductions and collection time. A category's share of patients is not its share of revenue or occupied bed-days.

Narayana's FY2025 presentation separately labels revenue-based payer and specialty profiles and defines the composition of insured patients and schemes. Those definitions should travel with any comparison. Its India operating metrics and Cayman business also need to remain separate before consolidation. [4]

Medical travel adds another dependency: funding, connectivity, access and follow-up. A historical cost comparison is neither a present package quote nor a guarantee that a patient can or should travel.

6. Why separate mature hospitals from new hospitals?

An established hospital has had time to develop referrals, recruit teams and spread fixed costs. A new hospital starts with capital already committed but only part of the eventual activity. Combining them can hide improvement in one cohort and deterioration in another.

Aster's nine-month FY2025 presentation breaks hospitals into maturity cohorts. KIMS' FY2024 report describes high initial fixed costs at new hospitals until volumes increase and discusses balancing those costs with mature facilities. These are useful analytical structures, not promises that every new site will converge to a mature margin. [2, 5]

Track each cohort's actual opening, operating beds, utilisation, losses and cumulative cash investment. EBITDA break-even is not recovery of the original investment or a return above the cost of capital.

7. Which expansion route uses capital best?

Brownfield expansion adds capacity around an existing operation. It can use established infrastructure and referrals, but still needs approvals, clinicians, equipment and cash. Construction can disrupt the current hospital.

A new building in a familiar city is still greenfield. Local brand awareness may reduce one risk without removing construction risk. An unfamiliar city adds the challenge of building a referral and staffing network from scratch.

An acquisition brings operating assets sooner, but purchase price, liabilities, doctor retention and integration determine the return. Buying a minority stake, acquiring control and purchasing the whole enterprise are different transactions.

I would compare capital committed, realistic commissioning, ramp-up losses, funding, working capital and sustainable cash returns for every route. A cheap-looking EBITDA multiple does not reveal the additional money needed after acquisition.

Count net additions. New beds that replace demolished or relocated beds should not all be treated as incremental capacity. A plan, an agreement, commissioning and cash-positive operation are different milestones.

8. Does asset-light mean free of fixed commitments?

No. Owned property, leased buildings, revenue-sharing agreements and management contracts distribute capital and risk differently. The operator may still fund equipment, interiors, staff, working capital or minimum payments.

KIMS' FY2024 report describes long leases on land and buildings with capital concentrated in medical equipment. Narayana's FY2025 presentation distinguishes owned hospitals from hospitals operated on rent or revenue-sharing terms with profit-and-loss responsibility. Neither description means the operating business has no capital at risk. [5, 4]

Read tenure, escalation, renewal, termination, minimum guarantees and who pays for improvements. A building that cannot economically be relocated creates dependence on the property agreement.

Lease accounting also changes presentation. EBITDA, EBITDAR and pre-lease-accounting profit are not interchangeable. Choose an earnings measure and debt/lease treatment that agree, then retain that basis across the peer comparison.

Long tenure can improve continuity, but the word leased does not establish perpetual rights. Nor does a managed hospital's bed count prove the listed company owns the full economics of those beds.

9. Why can margins rise while capital returns fall?

Margin compares profit with revenue. Return on capital employed compares an operating profit measure with the capital needed to earn it. New construction and equipment can enlarge the denominator before the hospital contributes meaningful earnings.

For actual research, use a consistent average-capital basis, entity scope and lease treatment. Distinguish goodwill, construction in progress and non-operating assets. Old depreciated assets may make returns look better than the replacement economics.

Max's FY2025 presentation explains exclusions and normalisation in its ROCE calculation, including purchase-price-allocation effects and the impact of new units and construction in progress. The lesson is to read the calculation, not treat the reported percentage as automatically identical to another company's ROCE. [3]

High group returns on yesterday's asset base do not establish attractive returns on the next hospital.

10. How much EBITDA becomes cash?

Doctors, staff, equipment and facilities must be paid before shareholders receive a distribution. Receivables, maintenance, interest, tax, leases and growth commitments can absorb operating earnings.

This is not a company cash-flow statement. Using a post-rent starting measure avoids deducting rent again; actual lease principal and interest require a reconciled reporting bridge.

Payer receivables deserve attention. Billing is not collection, and ageing, disputes and deductions can matter alongside revenue growth. Likewise, depreciation is not a reliable substitute for the exact maintenance cash needed in a particular year.

Separate sustaining expenditure from expansion without pretending that every management-labelled growth item is optional. Equipment replacement and safe upkeep cannot be ignored to make free cash flow look attractive.

11. What makes the clinical franchise durable?

Patients are not created by a building alone. Clinical teams, referrals, reputation, coordination and care systems matter. Concentration in a few clinicians can be economically important even in a large network.

Doctor ownership or variable compensation may support alignment, but it also requires examination of control, minority interests, incentives and succession. Financial alignment does not certify clinical quality or remove conflicts.

Keep the patient-quality assessment independent of investor enthusiasm. Ask what is measured, which hospital and period it covers, how cases differ and whether evidence is independently assessed. Accreditation has a defined scope; it is not a permanent guarantee of every outcome.

NABH's published framework addresses access, continuity, medication, infection control, patient rights and quality systems. Those subjects show why one financial ratio cannot stand in for hospital quality. This article does not use company marketing claims to rank clinical care. [6]

12. How should optional businesses and governance be valued?

Digital access, diagnostics and pharmacy can extend a hospital network. They can also require acquisition spending, discounts, technology investment and operating losses. Start with paying activity, retention and cash economics rather than registrations.

Separate the established cash-generating business from the new investment, but do not value the option while ignoring the funding it requires. Check intercompany transactions and avoid double-counting revenue across referrals, distribution and consolidated sales.

Governance research needs original filings: related-party arrangements, ownership rights, guarantees, contingent payments, audit qualifications and capital allocation. A complicated structure deserves investigation; complexity alone is not proof of misconduct.

The same applies to positive stories. A well-known investor, respected founder or ambitious equipment purchase does not establish fair minority treatment or good project economics. Historical allegations and anonymous anecdotes are not findings to repeat.

13. What belongs in a hospital valuation?

Begin with sustainable earnings by business and maturity cohort. Test utilisation, payer mix, staffing costs, maintenance, new-site losses and capital still to be spent. Do not annualise a pandemic recovery quarter or an exceptional profit as a permanent run rate.

Enterprise value divided by EBITDA is not market capitalisation divided by EBITDA. An enterprise multiple produces enterprise value; deriving equity value needs a consistent bridge for net debt, leases, minorities, other claims and non-operating assets.

For example, hypothetical EBITDA of 100 at 12 times gives enterprise value of 1,200, not automatically equity value of 1,200. A suitable bridge must be completed before dividing by the diluted share count. EBITDA is also not distributable cash or an equity payback measure.

My research sheet would follow operational beds, occupied days, metric definitions, specialty and payer mix, mature/new cohorts, incremental capital, clinical-franchise risks and cash conversion. Then it would test the valuation against slower ramp-up and fresh expansion needs.

The useful question is not how many beds the chain can announce. It is whether each expansion strengthens a safe, funded care business and earns an adequate return on the full money committed.
