Part I · Foundations of Money

Chapter 1. Money, Banking and the Financial System

Chapter 1 of 85 · about 15 minutes

Your salary lands in your account on the first of the month. You pay the rent by UPI, the milkman by cash, and some of it quietly becomes part of a stranger's home loan in a city you have never visited. All of that happened inside a machine most people use daily and few can describe. This chapter opens the machine. Once you see money as a system of IOUs built on trust, everything else in finance, from the repo rate to a stock market crash, starts making sense.

1.1What money is: functions, forms and why trust makes it work

From first principles: money is not notes and coins. Money is a trusted IOU. The note in your wallet is the state's IOU; the balance in your account is your bank's IOU to you. Both work only because the next person believes they will be accepted in turn.

That trusted IOU does three jobs, and anything that does all three can serve as money:

  • A medium of exchange: you hand it over and the deal is done, no barter needed.
  • A unit of account: prices, salaries and debts are written in it, so a dosa and a data plan can be compared.
  • A store of value: it carries today's earning power into next month, leaking only through inflation.

Forms of money sit on a ladder of abstraction: cash at the bottom, then bank deposits, then the purely digital entries behind UPI and wallets. A UPI payment moves no cash at all; it edits IOUs between two banks. The chaiwala accepts it because he trusts the edit. When trust breaks, money breaks with it: in a bank run, the same deposit that was money yesterday becomes a queue outside a branch today. Hold on to this, because the 2008 crisis at the end of the chapter is exactly that story at world scale.

1.2How banks create money: deposits, loans and the multiplier

Here is the fact that surprises even market regulars: most money is not printed by the government. It is created by banks when they lend.

Rebuild it from the primitives. Your deposit is the bank's IOU to you (money). The bank is allowed to lend most of that deposit out, keeping only a slice as reserve, the slice the RBI mandates through the cash reserve ratio, which in recent years has been in the low single digits. The borrower spends the loan, the seller deposits it, and now the same base money backs two deposits. Repeat the loop and deposits multiply:

You deposit
₹1,00,000 in your savings account
Bank keeps a reserve
say 10%, ₹10,000, set aside
Bank lends the rest
₹90,000 as someone's bike loan
Borrower spends it
the shop deposits it in its own bank
Cycle repeats
the same base money backs new deposits

The money-multiplier loop. Each round is smaller than the last, because every bank keeps a slice as reserve.

Worked example: the multiplier with round numbers

Suppose the reserve is 10%. Your ₹1,00,000 lets the first bank lend ₹90,000, which returns as a new deposit, of which ₹81,000 is lent again, and so on. The chain 1,00,000 + 90,000 + 81,000 + ... adds up to ₹10,00,000 of deposits. Raise the reserve to 20% and the ceiling falls to ₹5,00,000.

Now you can read monetary policy without a textbook: when the RBI raises the reserve ratio or its policy rates, it is turning this multiplier down; when it cuts, it is turning the multiplier up. One lever, the whole money supply.

Two honest limits to the picture. Real banks lend only when they find creditworthy borrowers, so the multiplier is a ceiling, not a promise. And the deposit you "created" is still the bank's IOU: if the bank fails beyond the DICGC insurance limit (₹5 lakh per depositor per bank), some of that money was never as real as it looked.

1.3The cast of the system: households, companies, banks, markets and the state

Finance looks like thousands of institutions. It is five actors passing three things, money, risk and information, among themselves:

Households
save, borrow, insure, invest
Companies
raise money, hedge risks, pay wages
Banks and NBFCs
take deposits, make loans
Markets
NSE, BSE, bond and commodity exchanges
The state
RBI, SEBI, government: rules and the backstop

Every financial story is one of these five passing money, risk or information to another.

Households supply most of the savings and demand most of the loans. Companies do the reverse. Banks sit between the two, and markets let both deal with strangers instead of one counterparty. The state writes the rules and, in a crisis, becomes the lender and insurer of last resort. When you read any financial news, ask which actor is passing what to whom, and the story sorts itself.

1.4Financial markets vs intermediaries: where each fits

There are two routes between a saver and a borrower, and India runs on both:

Through an intermediaryThrough a market
Who stands betweenA bank, NBFC or insurer takes your money and lends in its own nameNobody; you buy the borrower's bond or share directly on an exchange
Who carries the riskThe intermediary, which is why its health matters to youYou do, which is why prices move
Can you exit early?Usually on the intermediary's terms, like breaking an FDAny day the market is open, at the market's price
Everyday examplesSavings accounts, FDs, loans, insurance policiesShares, bonds, ETFs, government securities

The two routes compete and complete each other. India's markets have grown explosively, yet most household money still sits with intermediaries, which is why a bank failure hurts this country more than a bad week on the Nifty.

1.5Why finance is regulated at all: stability, fairness and confidence

Regulation feels like friction until you rebuild its reasons from the primitives:

  • Stability (money and risk): banks create money on trust, and trust is fragile. The RBI's reserves, capital rules and deposit insurance exist so that one bank's failure does not empty everyone's account.
  • Fairness (information): a seller of financial products almost always knows more than the buyer. SEBI's disclosure and insider-trading rules narrow that gap.
  • Confidence (exchange): markets only work if strangers dare to trade with each other. Clearing corporations and settlement rules make a trade with an anonymous counterparty safe.

Every regulator you will meet in this book, RBI, SEBI, IRDAI, PFRDA, is a specialist in one of these three jobs.

1.6Worked examples and cases

Case 1: how a salary deposit becomes a home loan

An engineer in Pune is paid ₹1,20,000 on the 1st. Her bank now owes her ₹1,20,000. Keeping a small reserve, it lends within a pool that funds, among thousands of others, a family's ₹40 lakh home loan in Nagpur. The family's EMI, interest plus principal, flows back each month and pays, among thousands of others, the interest on her account and the UPI cashback she enjoyed.

Nothing moved but IOUs. Her deposit was never "kept" anywhere; it was transformed into claims on thousands of strangers' future incomes. That transformation is banking, and it works until too many strangers stop paying at once, which is what credit risk means.

Case 2: the 2008 lesson on trust

In September 2008, Lehman Brothers, a 158-year-old American bank, failed. Within hours, money-market funds that held its IOUs "broke the buck", and banks across the world stopped lending to each other, because nobody knew whose IOUs were still good. The machine from section 1.2 ran in reverse: lending froze, deposits shrank, and only governments stepping in as the backstop restarted it.

India felt its own smaller echo in 2018 when IL&FS, a giant infrastructure lender, defaulted, and for months the NBFC sector struggled to borrow. Same lesson, local accent: finance runs on trust, trust runs on information, and when information fails, money itself seizes up. Every crisis chapter later in this book is a variation on this paragraph.
