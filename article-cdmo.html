CDMO: how to understand the business behind a medicine

By Finance With Vishnu · FY25 study edition

A successful medicine has a visible face: the brand, the prescription and the company selling it. Behind that face sits a less visible network of scientists, process engineers, analytical laboratories, ingredient manufacturers and specialised factories.

A contract development and manufacturing organisation, or CDMO, develops a manufacturing process, makes pharmaceutical material or a finished product, or does both for another company. The customer may own the molecule, the brand or the marketing rights. The supplier is paid for a defined part of turning the idea into a product that can be manufactured consistently.

That sounds like a simple outsourcing business. It is not. One supplier may make established generic tablets for domestic brands. Another may support an innovator's clinical programme for years before a commercial launch. A third may fill a sterile drug into an injection device. Their scientific requirements, failure risks, margins, cash cycles and competitive positions can be very different.

The first job is therefore not to decide whether CDMO is a megatrend. It is to identify which CDMO activity a company actually performs, who pays for it and what must happen for the next rupee of revenue to arrive.

Study window: FY25 annual reports and FY25 results presentations, with historical comparisons. Company plans described from those documents are not treated as achieved outcomes. This is a historical sector study, not a November 2026 earnings update, a medical guide or a recommendation to buy a stock.

1. What do CRO, CDMO, CRDMO, API and formulation mean?

These terms describe different parts of pharmaceutical work. They overlap, but they are not interchangeable. A research service is not the same product as a commercial batch of tablets.

An active pharmaceutical ingredient, or API, is the component intended to provide a drug's therapeutic activity. Intermediates are materials used along the route to making that API. A formulation is the finished dosage form, such as a tablet, capsule or injectable product, in which the API is delivered with the required other ingredients and packaging.

A contract research organisation, or CRO, performs research services for clients. In the company context discussed here, that can include discovery and development research; elsewhere the term also covers clinical-research services. A CDMO adds development and manufacturing capabilities. CRDMO is used to emphasise a broader research-to-development-to-manufacturing offering. Corporate labels are not standardised enough to replace reading the actual service mix.

A supplier making an advanced intermediate is not necessarily the API manufacturer. An API supplier is not automatically the finished-dose manufacturer. The company with the medicine's brand is not necessarily the owner of all the factories in the chain. These distinctions matter when estimating the supplier's share of a drug's sales.

Here is the practical rule: write one plain sentence describing what the customer receives. For example, 'the customer buys process-development work and later commercial API batches' is more useful than 'the company is exposed to global pharma innovation'. The first sentence can be tested against contracts, capacity and revenue. The second can cover almost anything.

2. How does a molecule become a commercial manufacturing opportunity?

Drug development is a sequence of experiments, regulatory decisions and commercial choices. A project can generate service revenue before approval, but approval and launch are not guaranteed simply because work has begun.

The FDA describes Phase 1 as focused primarily on safety, side effects and how a drug behaves in the body. Phase 2 investigates effectiveness and continues the safety assessment in patients. Phase 3 gathers broader evidence on effectiveness and adverse effects. A regulatory review follows the relevant development work; post-marketing surveillance continues after approval. The exact pathway and trial design depend on the product. [C1]

For a supplier, discovery-stage work may mean many small assignments. Development can require route selection, process improvement, analytical methods, impurity control and scale-up. Commercial manufacturing can bring larger repeat batches, but only if the drug is approved, launched, adopted and ordered in meaningful quantities.

Do not confuse scientific progress with a purchase order. A project can pass a clinical milestone while commercial supply remains years away. A customer may change its launch plan, allocate supply to another site, run down inventory or stop development. A supplier can have a technically successful process for a molecule that never becomes a large medicine.

A numerical funnel is useful only when its assumptions are visible. If ten early-stage projects each have a hypothetical 10% chance of eventually becoming a commercial programme, the expected number is one. That does not mean one is certain to succeed, that the projects are independent or that their eventual sizes are equal. This article deliberately does not assign one universal success rate or one fixed development timeline to the industry.

The research question is what stage the supplier's programmes occupy, how revenue is recognised at each stage, how much work is already funded and how much future value still depends on clinical, regulatory and commercial events.

3. Why do pharmaceutical companies outsource difficult work?

Outsourcing allows a customer to use another organisation's capabilities and capacity instead of building every part of the development and manufacturing chain itself. The reason can be speed, specialist knowledge, flexibility, cost or access to a proven quality system.

A small biotechnology company may have a promising molecule but no large manufacturing network. A large innovator may own factories yet still need external capacity, a particular chemistry or a second supply source. A medicine can require several different capabilities across its life cycle, and no customer must perform them all internally.

The supplier's value is not simply cheaper labour. A robust process must produce the required product repeatedly, at the right purity, yield and impurity profile, with documented controls. It must work at a larger scale than the laboratory experiment and within the applicable quality and regulatory framework. Equipment, analytical methods, safety systems, documentation and trained people all matter.

Supply-chain diversification is a possible source of new enquiries, but an enquiry is not revenue. Customers still have to qualify sites, transfer processes, validate production, obtain any necessary regulatory approvals and place orders. 'China plus one' is a description of a purchasing objective, not a guarantee that every Indian supplier gains attractive business.

The best evidence is more specific: a named capability added, a funded project entering a later stage, a validated line, a commercial shipment or increased revenue from existing customers. Even then, check whether the work earns an acceptable return after the cost of acquiring and serving the customer.

4. Where can customer stickiness become a competitive advantage?

Customer stickiness is the difficulty and cost of changing a qualified supplier. In pharmaceutical manufacturing it can arise from process knowledge, transfer work, documentation, validation and the need to maintain a reliable supply of an approved product.

That can create a useful relationship. The supplier learns the customer's standards, chemistry and ways of working. The customer gains confidence in the supplier's ability to solve problems and deliver. Additional projects may become easier to win than the first one.

But stickiness is not the same as pricing power without limits. A customer may qualify multiple sources, negotiate prices, move some volumes or demand productivity improvements. A supplier with one large programme can be deeply embedded and financially exposed at the same time. The same relationship that produces strong utilisation can become a vulnerability if the molecule disappoints.

Ask whether the relationship is broadening across molecules and sites, whether volume commitments are enforceable, whether the customer funds dedicated capacity, and what happens when a programme stops. A letter of intent, a master services agreement, a capacity reservation and a binding take-or-pay commitment do not all provide the same protection.

There is also a difference between scientific complexity and economic value. A technically difficult assignment may be underpriced. A mature product may generate dependable cash despite modest glamour. The attractive business is the one that converts its capabilities into recurring, well-paid work, not necessarily the one with the longest list of sophisticated technologies.

5. Why should generic CDMO and innovator CDMO be analysed separately?

Generic formulation CDMO usually serves established medicines and customers seeking development, manufacturing and supply for their marketed portfolios. Innovator-oriented work supports proprietary drug programmes whose commercial outcome may still be uncertain. Both are legitimate businesses, but their risk maps differ.

Windlas offers a useful generic-formulation example. Its FY25 annual report reports total revenue of Rs 7,599 million, or Rs 759.9 crore, and generic formulations CDMO revenue of Rs 5,551 million, or Rs 555.1 crore. That CDMO activity represented about 73% of group revenue on those figures. It must not be confused with a portfolio of novel drug discoveries awaiting approval. [C2]

In a generic-formulation model, relevant questions include customer relationships, dosage-form capabilities, quality, new product introductions, customer concentration, payment terms, plant utilisation and pricing pressure. Growth can come from serving more brands or products, entering new channels or adding capabilities, but it still has to produce cash after working capital and capital expenditure.

In an innovator-oriented model, pipeline stage, molecule concentration, launch timing and demand for the end medicine may have greater influence. Development revenue can be lumpy; successful commercialisation can change the revenue base significantly. The supplier's reported margin can be very different before and after a large programme ramps.

A comparison table that ranks all these businesses only by revenue growth or EBITDA margin will miss the central distinction. The return required by an investor should reflect how much uncertainty stands between today's assets and tomorrow's cash.

6. What does Aarti Pharmalabs teach us about pipeline quality?

A pipeline is a portfolio of customer programmes at different stages. Its quality depends on the stage, funding, economics, concentration and likely size of those programmes, not only on how many names appear in the list.

Aarti Pharmalabs' Q4FY25 presentation reports CDMO sales of Rs 32 crore in FY22, Rs 93 crore in FY23, Rs 161 crore in FY24 and Rs 208 crore in FY25. It reports 21 customers and 60 active projects in FY25: 33 commercial and 27 under development. These are the issuer's figures, not a prediction of what all those projects will become. [C3]

The sales series shows why a smaller activity can matter over time. It also shows why one should not extrapolate the fastest historical growth rate indefinitely. Projects have different sizes, and revenue can shift when a programme moves between development, validation, launch and routine supply.

The split between 33 commercial and 27 development projects is more informative than the combined count, but it still leaves important questions. How much revenue comes from the largest commercial molecule? How many development projects have external funding? What is the expected spend per programme? Are projects independent or attached to the same customer or therapeutic area? How much capacity is dedicated rather than transferable?

The right reading is neither '60 projects guarantees growth' nor 'early projects have no value'. It is that the portfolio has several possible routes to revenue, each with its own probability, timing and economics. Track movement between stages and the revenue generated, not just additions to the total count.

7. What does Piramal show about scaling commercial manufacturing?

Commercial manufacturing can turn successful customer programmes into a larger recurring revenue base. Its quality should be judged alongside concentration, capital employed, cash generation and the continuing need to replenish the portfolio.

Piramal Pharma's FY25 results release reports CDMO revenue of Rs 5,447 crore, compared with Rs 4,750 crore in FY24, a reported increase of 15%. The same release shows on-patent commercial manufacturing revenue rising from US$53 million in FY23 to US$116 million in FY24 and US$179 million in FY25. It reports the share of innovation-related work at 54% in FY25, compared with 50% in FY24. [C4]

Those figures describe different denominators. CDMO segment revenue is in rupees. On-patent commercial manufacturing is a subset measured in US dollars. Innovation-related work includes discovery, development and commercial manufacturing of products under patent. Do not add them together or present them as separate businesses of equal scope.

Piramal's group also includes complex hospital generics and India consumer healthcare. Its group EBITDA margin is not the CDMO segment margin. Its reported PAT can be affected by finance costs, depreciation, taxes and exceptional items. A revenue-mix improvement can therefore be economically important without producing a neat, immediate change in every group ratio.

The investing lesson is to build the bridge. How much of growth came from commercial programmes? What investment supported it? Did working capital rise? What remains after interest, tax and reinvestment? Management's FY30 ambitions belong in a scenario, explicitly labelled as ambitions, rather than in the historical chart.

8. What can the other company cases teach us?

A case study is most useful when it isolates one analytical problem. The aim is not to convert a list of companies into a buy list, but to learn what to check in different business models.

Blue Jet Healthcare: mix change and concentration. Its Q4FY25 presentation reports total FY25 revenue of Rs 10,300 million, or Rs 1,030 crore, against Rs 7,116 million, or Rs 711.6 crore, in FY24. This is total company revenue, not pure CDMO revenue. Its contrast-media, sweetener and pharmaceutical intermediate activities should be separated when explaining growth and margins. A growing intermediate programme can improve the mix and simultaneously increase dependence on a limited set of customers or end products. This article does not assert a confidential customer link or infer an API supplier from an intermediate supplier. [C5]

Neuland: a portfolio does not move in a straight line. Its FY25 annual report describes a consolidation year and reports the CMS business at 43% of revenue, with 97 active CMS projects. The useful question is how commercial programmes and the pipeline interact with the established API business. A quieter revenue year does not prove a relationship has failed; a large pipeline does not prove that the next year will be strong. Examine programme timing, concentration, quality and the money invested to support future work. [C6]

Laurus: a change in mix can be more important than one headline growth rate. Its April 2025 presentation reports FY25 CDMO revenue of Rs 1,534 crore against Rs 1,082 crore in FY24, an increase of 42%. A company building new capabilities can carry costs before the corresponding revenue arrives. The question is whether the new revenue becomes large and profitable enough to justify that investment, rather than whether the phrase 'CDMO transition' sounds attractive. Look at the remaining businesses, debt, capex and actual utilisation too. [C7]

Syngene: more capabilities do not automatically produce better returns. Its FY25 annual report describes integrated research, development and manufacturing services and reports revenue from operations of Rs 3,642 crore. Research intensity, new facilities and a broad customer base have to be connected to revenue, margins, capital employed and cash. A large scientific workforce is evidence of capacity to do work, not evidence that all that capacity earns a satisfactory return. [C8]

Hikal: segment mix can hide opposing cycles. Its Q4FY25 presentation discusses both pharmaceutical and crop-protection activities. A healthy pharmaceutical programme can coexist with a weaker crop-protection cycle. Consolidated results must therefore be broken into segments before attributing a margin movement to CDMO strength or weakness. Capital tied up in the slower activity still belongs to shareholders. [C9]

OneSource: the delivery format is part of the product. Its FY25 annual report describes biologics, drug-device combinations, sterile injectables and oral technologies. It describes plans to increase cartridge capacity from 40 million units in FY25 to 220 million by FY28. That is a capacity plan, not booked revenue. The questions include commissioning, customer qualification, funded demand, commercial approvals, yields, device compatibility and the effects of alternative delivery formats. An expanding therapy category does not remove execution or modality risk. [C10]

Ami Organics: separate the core business from optionality. The May 2025 presentation filed with the NSE describes pharmaceutical intermediates alongside specialty chemicals and the semiconductor-chemicals activity through Baba Fine Chemicals. These activities should not receive one common forecast merely because they sit inside one company. A new specialty-chemical capability may require customer qualification and investment before it becomes material. Model the existing business first, then identify what evidence and capital the newer activity needs. The historical issuer name used here is the name on the May 2025 filing. [C11]

Jubilant Pharmova: do not mix the pieces of a group. Its May 2025 presentation separates radiopharma, allergy immunotherapy, sterile-injectable CDMO, CRDMO, generics and proprietary novel drugs. A radiopharmacy network is not economically identical to an Indian research service or a North American sterile-manufacturing line. Compare the business-level growth, margin and investment needs before concluding that consolidated improvement proves every division is improving. Geographic diversification can be useful, while also creating different cost bases, regulatory exposures and capital needs. [C12]

9. Why can capex make earnings look worse before it makes them better?

A pharmaceutical expansion often requires spending on assets, people, validation and quality systems before those resources produce their planned revenue. The result can be a period of weak asset utilisation and lower reported returns.

Consider a hypothetical facility with Rs 200 crore of invested capital. Suppose steady-state revenue is modelled at Rs 300 crore, variable operating costs at 50% of revenue and fixed cash operating costs at Rs 90 crore. At Rs 120 crore revenue, EBITDA is negative Rs 30 crore. At Rs 240 crore, it is positive Rs 30 crore. At Rs 300 crore, it is Rs 60 crore. The same installed assets produce very different results at different activity levels.

These are invented teaching inputs, not a prediction for any company. Real plants have different chemistry, yields, batch sizes, pricing, compliance costs and customer terms. A simplistic utilisation model cannot capture all of that.

Depreciation and interest then sit below EBITDA. A company may report improving EBITDA while PAT remains under pressure. Cash flow can be weaker still if inventory and receivables build during the ramp. Capacity reservation fees or customer-funded equipment may reduce the supplier's funding need, but their terms must be read rather than assumed.

Low reported ROCE during construction is understandable. It is not evidence that the eventual return will be attractive. Ask what normal revenue, normal operating profit and the full capital base would be after commissioning. Include working capital and subsequent maintenance or compliance spending. A return calculation that removes the new assets but includes their hoped-for earnings tells a flattering story rather than a useful one.

10. How do you connect EBITDA to cash available to shareholders?

EBITDA is operating earnings before depreciation, amortisation, interest and tax. It is not free cash flow. In a capital-intensive business the distance between the two can be large.

Start with operating profitability, then examine working-capital movement, tax, maintenance spending, growth spending and financing. Cash generated by operations can be absorbed by new equipment or a growing inventory of specialised material. A large order can be attractive and still require funding before the supplier is paid.

A simple hypothetical bridge illustrates the issue: Rs 100 crore EBITDA, less Rs 20 crore of cash taxes and other operating cash adjustments, less Rs 25 crore invested in working capital, leaves Rs 55 crore before capital expenditure. If capex is Rs 70 crore, the business has consumed Rs 15 crore before financing flows. The positive EBITDA number is true; the cash requirement is also true. This is a simplified teaching bridge, not an accounting reconciliation for a named company.

Separate maintenance spending from growth spending where the disclosures allow it, but avoid assuming that every rupee described as growth capex can be excluded forever. Growing pharmaceutical businesses may need repeated investment to keep capabilities relevant. Acquisitions can also add goodwill and integration costs that complicate return comparisons.

Read several years together. One year's cash conversion can be distorted by advances, timing of inventory purchases, milestone collections or a commissioning schedule. A durable business should eventually show how the investment cycle becomes collected cash, not merely an expanding asset base.

11. Which risks can break an otherwise attractive CDMO thesis?

A thesis breaks when an assumption essential to the expected economics no longer holds. In CDMO, the risks sit at the level of the molecule, customer, plant, contract and balance sheet.

  • Clinical and commercial failure: the customer's drug can fail a trial, approval or launch, or sell below expectations.
  • Concentration: a small number of customers, molecules or sites can account for a large share of revenue or profit.
  • Quality and regulatory action: observations, restrictions, remediation or product-quality failures can delay supply and raise costs. A historical clean inspection does not guarantee a future one.
  • Capacity mismatch: building too early, building for the wrong modality or depending on one delayed approval can leave assets idle.
  • Pricing and productivity: customers can demand lower prices or yield improvements even when volumes rise.
  • Funding and inventory: weaker customers, receivable delays and dedicated materials can turn a commercial setback into a cash problem.
  • Technology and delivery changes: an alternative molecule or dosage form can alter demand for an intermediate, API, fill-finish line or device.
  • Financial structure: debt, dilution, acquisition costs, currency movements and high reinvestment needs can reduce the shareholder's outcome even if the end market grows.

A popular medicine does not guarantee that every participant in its supply chain earns excess returns. Nor is a supplier entitled to a fixed percentage of the medicine's retail sales. The contract, the bill of materials and the supplier's actual role determine the economics.

12. How would you research and value a CDMO company?

Research should turn the broad industry story into a small set of explicit assumptions about customers, programmes, capacity, margins, reinvestment and cash. Valuation then asks what price leaves room for those assumptions to be wrong.

Start by splitting the revenue into activities that have different economics: generic and innovator work; research and commercial manufacture; intermediates, APIs and finished doses; domestic and export markets. Identify how the company itself defines each segment and whether that definition changed.

Next, map the programme portfolio. Separate signed work from enquiries, development from commercial projects and management aspirations from contractual commitments. Where confidentiality limits disclosure, admit the uncertainty instead of inventing the missing molecule or customer name.

Build a capacity bridge with commissioning dates, qualification requirements and plausible utilisation. Then build a profit and cash bridge. Model delays and lower utilisation as seriously as the successful ramp. Ask whether the company can finance the cautious case without excessive debt or dilution.

For valuation, do not apply a premium multiple simply because CDMO is in the presentation title. Use normalised earnings and cash generation across an investment cycle, and distinguish the existing business from uncertain pipeline value. A scenario analysis is often more honest than one target price built from one successful future.

My final test is whether I can explain the next stage without relying on a slogan. Which programme or capability creates the revenue? What has to be approved or qualified first? What capital is already committed? How concentrated is the outcome? What evidence would show that I am wrong?

The opportunity in CDMO is real enough to study carefully. The care is the point. The attractive business is one that turns scientific capability and dependable execution into recurring cash after the cost of staying capable has been paid.
