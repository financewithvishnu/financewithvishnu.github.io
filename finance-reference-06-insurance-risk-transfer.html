Chapter 6. Insurance as Risk Transfer

Chapter 6 of 85 · about 18 minutes

A friendly agent sits across your table with a plan that gives "insurance plus investment plus tax saving, all in one". It sounds like three wishes granted. It is actually three products stapled together, each worse than the one bought alone, and the staple is a fee. This chapter teaches you to unbundle every such offer in your head within a minute, and to buy the protection your family actually needs for a tenth of the price.

6.1Why is insurance not an investment?

From first principles: insurance is risk transfer. You pay a small, certain loss called the premium so the insurer carries a large, uncertain loss in your place. The insurer runs a pool: many people's premiums pay the few people's claims, and the premium equals the expected loss plus running costs plus a margin. Nothing in this machine is trying to grow your money. Investment is a different machine: deferring spending today for more spending later.

When a product promises "money back", part of your premium has been quietly diverted into an investment bundle with its own costs, and the insurance part is priced out of what remains. You end up under-insured and under-invested in one purchase. The fix is a habit: unbundle every offer. Ask what the pure protection costs alone and what the leftover money earns. The leftover almost always earns less than a plain index fund, and the protection is almost always thinner than a plain term plan.

6.2What is term life insurance, and why is it the only life cover most people need?

A term plan is insurance in its purest form: pay about ₹12,000 a year and your family receives ₹1,00,00,000 if you die within the term; if you survive, you get nothing back. That "nothing" is not a flaw. It is exactly why the cover is cheap: you paid only for risk transfer, the way you pay a fire premium on your home without mourning the years it did not burn down.

  • Who needs it: anyone whose death would hurt someone financially, meaning dependents or outstanding loans. Nobody depending on your income, no need for life cover.
  • How much: a quick rule is 10 to 15 times annual expenses plus outstanding loans. The careful way adds up loans, future family expenses and big goals, then subtracts assets the family could sell. Case 1 below works a full number.
  • How to buy: young and healthy, a level premium, a term that ends around retirement age, and complete honesty on the medical questions. A claim rejected over a hidden habit costs your family everything the plan was for.

6.3What should you check in a health insurance policy?

Check the sum insured, waiting periods, exclusions, co-payment and room-rent limits, and the insurer’s claim-settlement record, because these five clauses decide most claim outcomes.

The most common way Indian savings die is not a market crash; it is one week in a hospital without adequate cover. Employer insurance helps but is thinner than it looks and vanishes between jobs. Your own policy, bought while healthy, is the reliable layer. But health policies are won or lost in their features:

Worked example: the room-rent clause that halves a bill

Your ₹5,00,000 policy caps room rent at 1% of the sum insured: ₹5,000 a day. You choose a room at ₹10,000, twice the cap. On discharge you learn the insurer did not just cut the room charge. It applied proportionate deduction: because the room was 2x the limit, every linked charge, surgeon fees, nursing, theatre, is paid at half. A ₹2,00,000 bill settles near ₹1,00,000. One clause, chosen in a hurry at purchase, cost a lakh at claim time. This is why the features table comes before the premium table.

For larger protection on a budget, a super top-up adds a big cover above a deductible for a modest premium: ₹20,00,000 of extra cover beyond the first ₹5,00,000 can cost less than a dinner out each month.

6.4Which general insurance covers do you need: motor, home and liability?

The same risk-transfer logic covers the rest of life's tails. Motor: third-party cover is mandatory because it pays the stranger you might injure; own-damage cover pays for your vehicle, priced off its insured declared value. Home: insuring the structure and contents against fire, flood and theft costs a few thousand rupees a year for a large cover, and almost nobody buys it, which is strange in a country that keeps its wealth in property. Personal accident and liability covers complete the set. All of them share the term-plan shape: small certain premium, large uncertain loss, no money-back nostalgia.

6.5How do you read an insurance policy before buying it?

Read the exclusions, waiting periods, deductibles and claim conditions in the policy wording itself rather than the brochure, because the wording, not the advertisement, decides what gets paid.

The brochure sells the policy; the wording pays the claim. Twenty minutes with the wording, before buying, beats two years of grievance mail after. Read in this order: the definition of the insured event (what exactly triggers payment), the exclusions (what never pays), waiting periods (what does not pay yet), sub-limits and deductibles (what pays only partly), the claim process and timelines, and the renewal terms. If any clause is unclear, the question to the insurer is written down, and so is the answer.

6.6Worked examples and cases

Case 1: sizing term cover for a family

A 32-year-old earns ₹12,00,000 a year; family expenses ₹7,00,000; home loan outstanding ₹40,00,000; one child, college goal ₹20,00,000; existing investments ₹10,00,000. Cover needed: loan ₹40L + fifteen years of expenses ₹1,05,00,000 + college ₹20L, minus assets ₹10L, lands near ₹1,55,00,000. Rounded: a ₹1,50,00,000 term plan to age 60, roughly ₹15,000-18,000 a year, about the cost of a streaming bundle and a few dinners. The family now survives the worst day without selling the house.

Case 2: the bundle, unbundled, with twenty years of numbers

Two 30-year-olds each commit ₹1,00,000 a year for 20 years. One buys a classic endowment-style plan: life cover of about ₹10,00,000 and a maturity value growing near 5.5% a year, roughly ₹37,00,000 at year 20. The other buys a ₹1,00,00,000 term plan for ₹12,000 a year and invests the remaining ₹88,000 annually in a plain equity index fund at 11%: roughly ₹63,00,000 at year 20, plus ten times the life cover throughout.

Same money in, wildly different outcomes out: the unbundled pair ends with about 70% more wealth and ₹90,00,000 more protection in every one of the twenty years. Bundles lose on both legs because the charges live in the darkness between the two products. (Illustrative rates, before tax; the gap survives any reasonable assumption.)
