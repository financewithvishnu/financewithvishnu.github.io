Chapter 75. Asset Allocation and Diversification

Chapter 75 of 85 · about 19 minutes

After seventy-four chapters of instruments, the final part of this website turns to the only question that decides your outcome: how you combine them. The research verdict of four decades is blunt and liberating: the allocation between equity, debt, gold and cash explains the overwhelming bulk of what a portfolio does, dwarfing the stock picks and fund choices that absorb everyone's attention. Part XIV begins with the decision that matters most.

75.1Why does asset allocation drive most of your returns?

The claim has been measured every way the industry knows how, from Brinson's pension-fund studies onward, and the finding survives every methodological fight: variation in returns between portfolios comes mostly from policy allocation, not from security selection or timing. The intuition is arithmetic. The difference between an 80% and a 60% equity portfolio is a 20-point swing in every crisis; the difference between the best and worst large-cap fund in a normal year is a few points. You are choosing which ocean to sail, and the choice of ocean is the weather. The practical consequence inverts most people's effort budget: the allocation deserves the annual deep thought, the quarterly anxiety belongs to nothing, and the fund selection deserves an afternoon, rules-based and final. Get the big ratio right, and the small decisions are rounding; get it wrong, and no brilliance downstream saves you, because brilliance cannot repeal a crash you were 90% exposed to.

75.2What roles do equity, debt, gold and cash play in one portfolio?

Give each asset a job description and the allocation writes itself. Equity is the growth engine: the only mainstream asset with a claim on rising productivity, and the price is volatility and the behavioural tax of sitting through it. Debt is the ballast: steady, taxable, low-return, and its real job is not return at all, it is to be there, liquid and intact, when equity is on sale and when life presents a bill. Gold is the insurance: the crisis asset that rises when the others fall, paying nothing, and Chapter 64's wrappers decide how much of its price you keep. Cash is the option: dry powder and emergency reserve, costing a carry loss every year, buying the ability to act when others must sell. A portfolio is these four employees in the right staffing ratio for your job requirements, horizon, obligations, temperament, and the test of any allocation is not its back-test but whether you can hold it through the day each employee earns its salary: the crash, the crisis, the panic, the call for cash.

75.3What are age-based and goal-based allocation rules, and their limits?

The heuristics exist to start conversations, not end them. The age rules, "100 minus age in equity", and its aggressive cousins, encode the true observation that horizon shrinks with age, and encode it crudely: a healthy 60-year-old with a pension and no mortgage is not the rule's 60-year-old. The goal-based frame is sharper: each goal gets its horizon, and the horizon sets the asset, equity for the goal fifteen years out, debt for the goal in three, cash for the goal in one, so the portfolio is a ladder of goal-specific allocations rather than one blended guess. The limits matter more than the rules. Both frames miss capacity vs tolerance: the ability to take risk, stable income, deep reserves, is not the willingness, the stomach for watching half disappear, and the right allocation respects the tighter of the two. And both miss the liabilities: the home loan, the child's admission year, the parents' medical horizon, which are negative assets that belong on the same page. Use the rules to draft, your own balance sheet to edit, and Chapter 78's retirement math to verify.

75.4What is correlation, and why does it make diversification real?

Diversification is not owning many things; it is owning things that move differently, and correlation is the measuring tape. Two funds holding the same fifty large-caps are one bet with two expense ratios; equity and gold are two bets whose bad decades alternate, which is why the 60/35/5 of Case 1 bleeds so much less than its equity sleeve. The subtleties are the discipline. Correlations are unstable: assets that sleepwalk apart in calm years run together in panics, the 2008 lesson that "everything correlates in a crisis", except the true hedges, quality debt and gold, which is exactly when you were counting on them. And correlation is backward-looking: the measured number describes a regime that may be ending, as stock-bond correlation flipped sign when inflation returned in 2022, punishing the portfolio built on forty years of the old one. The robust response is structural, not statistical: hold assets whose economic drivers differ, claims on profits, claims on interest, claims on fear, so that the diversification survives the next regime rather than fitting the last one.

75.5When and how should you rebalance your portfolio?

The allocation drifts with every market move, and rebalancing, selling what grew fat, buying what grew cheap, is the only investment discipline that is simultaneously free, unemotional and reliably contrary. Two calendars govern it. Time-based: rebalance annually, on a fixed date, simple and sufficient. Threshold-based: rebalance when any sleeve wanders more than five percentage points from target, more work, better risk control. The how has an Indian tax texture: prefer flow rebalancing first, direct new SIP money and withdrawals to the underweight sleeve, because selling triggers the capital gains of Chapter 73, and only sell when flows cannot catch the drift. The hidden benefit is behavioural: rebalancing hands you a written order to buy the crash and trim the mania, converting the two hardest trades in investing into maintenance. The investor who rebalanced in March 2009 and March 2020 did not need courage; they needed a rule, and this is the chapter where the rule comes from.

75.6Worked examples and cases

Case: a 60/35/5 Indian portfolio across 2008 and 2020

Hold a portfolio of 60% equity, 35% debt, 5% gold through the two great stress tests, illustrative returns. 2008: equity −52%, debt +8%, gold +25%. The blend loses 27%, savage, but against equity's −52%, it is the difference between a wound and a funeral, and here is the decisive detail: the equity sleeve has shrunk to 40% of the portfolio, so the annual rebalance forces the purchase of equity at half-price with the profits of debt and gold, setting up the recovery's arithmetic. 2020: the crash-and-recovery year ends equity +15%, debt +12%, gold +28%, and the blend prints +15%, while the all-equity investor who panic-sold in March watched it from cash. The two years together are the whole chapter: the blend's job is not to avoid losses but to keep you in the game, solvent, unbroken, and mechanically buying, so that the recovery, which never announces its date, finds you holding. Allocation is not a prediction about markets; it is a prediction about yourself.
