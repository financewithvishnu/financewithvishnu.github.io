APIs: the real product is a reliable process

By Finance With Vishnu · Source period: FY2021-era; exact class date unconfirmed

A medicine's active ingredient can be essential to the patient while its manufacturer has very little pricing power. Several qualified factories may be able to supply it. The customer can negotiate. A new plant can arrive just as prices soften. Being important is different from being difficult to replace.

I would therefore start an API study with a manufacturing question: can this company repeatedly deliver the required material, at the required quality, on time, at a full cost that leaves an adequate return on all the capital tied up? The molecule's name comes before the market-size slide.

1. Where does an API sit?

An active pharmaceutical ingredient is the component intended to provide pharmacological activity or another direct effect in a medicine. It need not be a salt. An intermediate is material made along the way that undergoes further processing before becoming the API. The finished formulation combines the ingredient with other materials and a dosage form suitable for use. These are connected businesses, not interchangeable products. ICH Q7 distinguishes API starting materials, intermediates and API manufacturing, and describes different routes including synthesis, extraction and fermentation. [1]

Granules' FY2025 report describes its activities across APIs, pharmaceutical formulation intermediates and finished dosages. That is a useful example of why a company labelled an API producer may earn money at several points in the chain. Its PFI category should not be confused with every chemical intermediate used in API synthesis. [6]

Before comparing companies, draw their actual perimeter. Does the API go to external customers or to a captive formulation plant? Does the company own the process, undertake paid development, sell a catalogue product or manufacture under another party's dossier? Consolidated sales can conceal these differences.

2. Why is there no single API business model?

For a mature generic ingredient, process efficiency, dependable quality and scale can be decisive. A more difficult API may require specialised chemistry, analytical work or purification that narrows the effective supplier set. Contract development and manufacturing adds another layer: the customer may pay for development before large commercial volumes exist, while individual programmes can fail or change direction.

These categories overlap. Neuland's FY2025 report separates Generic Drug Substances from Custom Manufacturing Solutions, and within GDS distinguishes large-volume mature products from lower-volume complex products. Its CMS description covers development and manufacture of new-chemical-entity APIs and advanced intermediates. This is evidence of differing commercial models inside one business, not proof that one category always earns better returns. [5]

A high selling price per kilogram is an incomplete clue. Difficult inputs, long cycles, low yields, expensive purification and small campaigns may absorb that price. Equally, a widely used generic ingredient can have attractive economics if a producer is genuinely efficient. Start with contribution per accepted batch and the capital needed to deliver it, rather than assigning quality from a label.

The separate CDMO study explores programme pipelines and development-to-commercial transitions. Here the focus is the process, supply chain and capital economics shared by ingredient manufacturers.

3. What does process expertise actually buy?

Process expertise must show up in repeatable output. Possible benefits include less input consumed per accepted unit, fewer rejected batches, shorter equipment occupation, lower solvent or energy use, better recovery and simpler waste handling. A laboratory success is an important milestone. A reliable commercial process at a competitive full cost is a different achievement.

The assumptions matter more than the attractive percentage. A process improvement may require more expensive inputs or new equipment. Purification can recover less final product than a crude reaction yield suggests. A saving should be measured against accepted saleable output after the additional costs, not against the most flattering intermediate number.

ICH Q7 calls for master production instructions covering process controls and expected yield ranges, and batch records covering actual parameters, yields, testing and deviations. That connects the economic idea to documented manufacturing discipline. Quality data are part of the process, not a marketing attachment. [1]

4. Is the reactor really the bottleneck?

A large installed reactor does not by itself establish annual output. Campaign scheduling, reaction time, filtration, drying, cleaning, laboratory release, utilities and effluent handling can constrain the flow. Capacity expressed in reactor volume is not automatically tonnes of accepted API, still less revenue.

Consider a fictional plant that produces 100 accepted units per completed campaign. Ten campaigns yield 1,000 units; twelve yield 1,200. Faster turnaround could raise output by 20% without a larger reactor, but only if downstream equipment, release testing, demand and qualified scheduling also permit twelve campaigns. If the dryer remains the bottleneck, a faster reaction may add little.

Multipurpose equipment offers flexibility, not instant interchangeability. Cleaning and cross-contamination controls, equipment compatibility, customer requirements and validated processes still govern switching. A catalogue of products cannot all occupy the same vessel at the same time. The model needs available campaign days and changeovers as well as nameplate equipment.

5. Who keeps a cost saving?

A producer can become more efficient while its selling price falls. Some of the gain may go to customers through negotiation or competing suppliers. The question is whether cost falls sufficiently to protect contribution, grow accepted volume and earn on the capital employed.

Suppose selling price is 100 and variable cost 65, giving contribution of 35 before fixed costs. If price falls 15% to 85 while variable cost falls 10% to 58.5, contribution becomes 26.5, down about 24.3%. Cost improvement did not preserve profit per unit. A volume increase might offset the decline, but it would require plant time and potentially additional working capital.

That is why a falling product price is neither automatically disastrous nor automatically evidence of a stronger moat. Reconcile price, volume, mix, raw materials, yield and fixed costs. A temporary supply shortage can make an ordinary product look exceptional. A later price normalisation can expose the difference between a durable process advantage and a fortunate cycle.

6. What would make China+1 real?

Supply diversification begins with a customer's concern, not a supplier's revenue. It becomes commercially meaningful through qualification, accepted purchases and repeat orders at adequate economics. A plant in another country may still depend on the same critical upstream input. The final manufacturing address is not a complete supply-chain map.

Different signals can reinforce the same thesis without being independent proof. Upstream suppliers expanding, API manufacturers announcing projects and customers discussing resilience may all reflect the same optimistic demand assumption. Actual utilisation, repeat commercial purchases and cash conversion provide a harder test.

Country-level import shares also need a denominator. A percentage of imports sourced from one country is not the same as that country's share of all domestic consumption. Plant counts do not measure qualified capacity. An old market forecast should not silently become an achieved outcome merely because its final year has passed.

7. When does backward integration help?

Making an input internally can improve scheduling, control over specifications and supply continuity. It may also capture economics previously earned upstream. But the company has exchanged some purchasing flexibility for capital, fixed costs, operating responsibilities and another plant that needs sufficient utilisation.

Divi's FY2025 report describes in-house manufacture of most key starting materials as part of its backward-integrated model, linking it to impurity control, costs and supply resilience. Those are the company's stated mechanisms, not an independent certification of lowest cost or immunity to shortages. [4]

Ask which inputs are actually covered. Integration into one intermediate can leave another essential reagent externally concentrated. Then compare buy versus make at an equivalent quality and delivery basis, including depreciation, utilities, working capital, waste treatment, maintenance and idle capacity. A captive transfer price is not proof of a consolidated saving.

Integration can also reduce flexibility if an outside supplier's price falls sharply. A useful investment case should survive normal competitive conditions rather than require permanently disrupted external supply.

8. What do DMFs and CEPs prove?

Filing counts can show regulatory participation, but they are not a revenue register. FDA's DMF guidance explains that a drug master file can provide confidential manufacturing or related information supporting an application. FDA does not approve or disapprove the DMF itself; its technical contents are reviewed in connection with a relevant application. Calling each filing an approved medicine or a guaranteed commercial product skips essential steps. [2]

A certificate of suitability, or CEP, answers a different question. EDQM explains that it demonstrates how the quality of a substance can be controlled using the relevant European Pharmacopoeia monograph, with additional tests where needed. EDQM explicitly says a CEP is neither equivalent to a GMP certificate nor a replacement for one. [3]

Even a small apparent supplier count needs care. Are the entries active, assessed, available at scale and qualified for the customer concerned? Are different salts, grades or sites being combined? A larger stock of valid certificates and a larger flow of new filings are different observations. Neither directly measures market share, customer allocation or profitability.

9. Why is quality part of the business model?

Quality failures can interrupt shipments, require investigation and remediation, absorb management time and weaken customer confidence. Compliance capability can support customer relationships, but no favourable past record guarantees future performance. A regulator's action should be studied at the exact site, product and scope rather than reduced to a share-price opportunity.

ICH Q7 frames manufacturing broadly, including quality control, release, storage and distribution alongside production. It also separates its API GMP guidance from personnel safety and environmental protection, which remain manufacturer responsibilities under applicable national laws. One quality document should not be presented as blanket proof of environmental or occupational safety compliance. [1]

In financial analysis, distinguish a management response from the regulator's conclusion, remediation spending from actual resolution, and permitted operations from restored customer orders. Oral medicines do not escape quality requirements simply because injectable manufacturing has additional sterility demands. This article does not assess the current status of any named company's facilities.

10. Why can a promising plant depress returns?

Capital often arrives before sales. Equipment, buildings, validation work and inventories can enter the denominator while operating profit is still small. That can explain weak returns during a ramp. It does not prove that returns must recover.

The final row is conditional, not a forecast. Track commissioning, qualification, first orders, repeat batches, pricing and remaining spending separately. A project can be physically ready but commercially underused. An order can be large in headline value but spread over years. A customer can grow while reducing purchases temporarily as inventories normalise.

Compare asset turns only after matching definitions. Sales divided by gross fixed assets differs from sales divided by depreciated net fixed assets, and both differ from sales divided by total capital employed. EBITDA margin multiplied by gross-asset turns is not automatically ROCE. Depreciation can lift net-asset turns without improving physical output.

11. Where can the accounting profit get stuck?

Inventory protects supply continuity, but it uses cash. Products awaiting acceptance or release, long transit times and customer receivables can expand the funding need. Integration can add upstream stocks just as a new plant adds downstream inventory. Working capital is part of the cost of growth.

Suppose fictional annual sales are 1,000 and receivable days rise from 60 to 90 on a 365-day basis. The extra 30 days tie up about 82.2 of cash if other conditions are unchanged. This is not an expense of 82.2 in that year's income statement, but it is money unavailable for debt repayment or another project. Inventory and payables require their own bridge.

Review operating cash over a suitable period, then deduct maintenance and growth spending explicitly. A multi-year development business may have uneven collections; smoothing can help interpretation but should not hide growing overdue balances or permanent cash consumption. High growth funded repeatedly at poor incremental returns is not rescued by a sophisticated molecule.

12. What would change the view?

A useful study is falsifiable. Write the exact product opportunity, what this supplier can do, how accepted volume becomes cash, and what would invalidate the expectation. Replace broad claims of expertise with dated evidence.

  • Process: repeatable accepted quality, yields, campaign times and delivered cost, with full definitions.
  • Demand: qualified customers, recurring purchases, accessible volumes and pricing, not only enquiries.
  • Capital: actual spending, capacity bottlenecks, remaining commitments and consistent return measures.
  • Resilience: critical upstream dependencies, alternative sources, site concentration and financing headroom.
  • Cash: inventories, receivables, collections and free cash after the spending needed to sustain the business.

The warning signs are the reverse: qualification repeatedly slips, customer interest never becomes orders, prices fall faster than costs, inventory keeps rising, another round of capital is needed before the first earns adequately, or quality explanations replace resolved evidence. A long horizon is useful only if the business milestones keep progressing.

The central distinction is simple. An API is what the company sells. A reliable, economical and accepted process is what can make that sale worth repeating. Investment value adds one more test: whether the future cash earned on all that work is sufficient for the risk and the price paid.
