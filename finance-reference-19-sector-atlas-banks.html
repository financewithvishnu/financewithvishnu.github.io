Chapter 19. Sector Atlas: Banks, NBFCs and Financial Services

Chapter 19 of 85 · about 23 minutes

Open the Nifty's list of heavyweights and you will find a bank, an NBFC or an insurer every second name. Indian equity is, to an unusual degree, a leveraged bet on Indian credit, which makes lenders the one sector every investor must be able to read even if they never buy one. Lenders are also the sector where ordinary accounting misleads most, because the product is money, the raw material is money, and the profit is the difference between the two. This atlas chapter, the first of six, teaches you to read a lender's books the way a credit analyst does.

19.1How lenders make money: NIM, credit costs and the loan book

From first principles, a lender is a spread machine with a quality problem. It borrows money at one rate (deposits, bonds, wholesale funding), lends at a higher rate, and keeps the difference: the net interest margin, NIM, interest earned minus interest paid as a share of earning assets, typically 2.5-4.5% for Indian lenders. Two costs then eat the spread. Operating costs, branches, people, technology, tracked by the cost-to-income ratio. And credit costs, the loans that do not come back, provisioned against profit. The lethal feature of the model is leverage: a bank runs on roughly ten rupees of assets per rupee of equity, so a 1% mistake on assets is a 10% hit to the owner's capital. This is why lending quality, not growth, is the master variable of the sector: any fool can grow a loan book, and in every Indian credit cycle, several do.

19.2The metrics that matter: GNPA, CASA, capital adequacy, ROA

  • GNPA, gross non-performing assets: the share of loans not being serviced. The sector's fever thermometer. Read it with the provision coverage ratio: a 4% GNPA fully provided for is a scar; a 2% GNPA thinly provided is a suspicion.
  • CASA ratio: current and savings account deposits as a share of total deposits. These are the cheapest funds in the system; a high CASA franchise is a structural cost advantage no competitor can quickly copy.
  • Capital adequacy (CRAR): the bank's own capital against its risk-weighted assets, floored by RBI at 9% plus buffers, so about 11.5% in practice. Capital is the shock absorber; growth consumes it, losses eat it, and a bank below comfort must either raise equity, diluting you, or stop growing.
  • ROA, return on assets: profit over total assets. The cleanest single summary of a lender: 1.8-2% is excellent in India, 1% is ordinary, below 0.5% is a value trap in a suit. ROE is ROA multiplied by leverage, which is why two banks with the same ROE can be entirely different risks.

19.3Credit cycles and how they rhyme in India

Indian credit moves in long, rhyming cycles. The 2003-08 boom funded infrastructure with short money; the 2011-14 bust put those loans on the NPA books of the PSU banks that made them; the 2015-19 clean-up, bankruptcy code included, transferred the growth to private lenders and NBFCs; the 2018-19 NBFC stress re-priced wholesale funding; and the post-2020 cycle ran on retail credit. Each act features the same script: spreads look safest exactly when competition for growth is quietly weakening underwriting; the bad loans are made in the good times and only recognised in the bad. The investor's rule: judge a lender by the underwriting it did three years ago, which is the NPA book it owns today, and be most suspicious when its loan growth is most celebrated.

19.4Regulation: RBI rules that shape bank economics

The RBI is not background noise in this sector; it is a co-author of every lender's P&L. Capital floors set how much equity supports how much lending, and therefore the sector's maximum ROE. Priority-sector requirements direct a large share of credit to mandated segments, which is a tax by another name, priced into spreads. Liquidity requirements (the statutory liquidity ratio's government-bond pile and the cash reserve ratio's interest-free deposits with the RBI) earn below-market returns and quietly lower every bank's ROA. And supervision, prompt corrective action for weak banks, tightening rules on unsecured retail credit, caps on how NBFCs fund themselves, repeatedly redraws which business models are allowed to be profitable. Reading a lender without reading the current regulatory stance is reading half the book.

19.5How to value a lender: P/B vs ROE done properly

Chapter 16 gave the identity: justified P/B = (ROE - g) / (r - g). This chapter supplies the judgement inside it. The ROE that matters is the sustainable, clean ROE: through-the-cycle credit costs, not this year's unusually low ones; fully provided books, not hopeful ones; and growth that the capital actually funds. A lender reporting 18% ROE while growing 25% a year on thin capital is borrowing its future ROE. The checklist synthesis: start with ROA and NIM for the engine, GNPA and provisioning for the brakes, CRAR for the fuel tank, CASA for the franchise's moat, and only then convert the honest ROE into a justified multiple. Price follows that identity over time, which is why the cheapest P/B in the sector is usually the most expensive mistake.

Read the table as one argument. The private bank's edge is not one metric but a system: CASA lowers funding cost, which funds better underwriting, which lowers credit costs, which produces ROA, which supports capital, which funds growth. The PSU bank's discount is not prejudice; at 0.7x book the market is pricing a 10% ROE against a 14% cost of equity, plus governance history, which Chapter 16's formula translates directly into the multiple. The recovery trade in such a bank is a bet on the ROE line, and it works exactly when credit costs normalise faster than the market believes, as the 2020-24 PSU re-rating showed. Both investments are legible; neither is mysterious. That is the whole point of a sector atlas.
