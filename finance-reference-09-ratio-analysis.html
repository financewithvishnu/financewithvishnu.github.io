Chapter 9. Ratio Analysis and Financial Health Check

Chapter 9 of 85 · about 20 minutes

A doctor does not read your entire body; she takes five measurements and knows where to look. Ratios are the investor's vital signs: a dozen small divisions that turn 300 pages of annual report into a health chart you can read in minutes. This chapter gives you the kit, ratio by ratio, and ends by running the full check on two companies side by side, one of which is quietly dying at a P/E of 9.

9.1Why ratios, and their one great weakness

From first principles: absolute numbers do not compare. ₹500 crore of profit is magnificent for a small-cap and disappointing for Reliance. A ratio divides one statement number by another, stripping out size so that companies, years and industries can be compared honestly. The weakness to hold in mind throughout: a ratio is a ratio of accounting numbers. It inherits every assumption in the statements, which is why Chapter 8's cash-flow scepticism and Chapter 10's red flags sit on either side of this chapter.

9.2Profitability: margins, ROE, ROCE

  • Margins (gross, operating, net): profit as a share of revenue. The trend matters more than the level; a margin sliding three years in a row is competition or cost trouble arriving.
  • ROE, return on equity: net profit / shareholders' equity. What the owners earn on their capital. The trap: debt inflates it. A company earning 18% ROE on a mountain of borrowing is running a leveraged trade, not a great business.
  • ROCE, return on capital employed: operating profit / (equity + debt). The honest version, because it pays the capital whoever supplied it. A business earning 24% ROCE with money that costs 9% is printing value; one earning 9% ROCE with money costing 9% is running hard to stand still. This single spread, ROCE minus cost of capital, is the closest thing finance has to a quality thermometer.

9.3Leverage and solvency: debt-to-equity, interest cover

Debt-to-equity counts how many rupees lenders supplied for each rupee of the owners'. Above 1, ask why; above 2, demand an excellent answer. Interest cover, operating profit / interest expense, asks the survival question directly: how many times over does the year's operating earning pay the year's interest? Below 2, a single bad year threatens default. Remember from Chapter 8 that debt is a fixed claim on an uncertain stream; these two ratios measure how fixed and how uncertain.

9.4Efficiency: working capital and the cash conversion cycle

A business buys inputs, holds inventory, sells on credit, and eventually collects. The cash conversion cycle, inventory days + receivable days - payable days, measures how many days the company's own money is trapped in that loop. Shorter is stronger; negative is beautiful, because customers and suppliers are funding the business (many retailers run negative cycles). A lengthening cycle is one of the earliest, dullest, most reliable warnings in all of analysis: growth that needs more and more working capital per rupee of sales is growth that eats cash.

9.5Liquidity ratios and the warning zone

The current ratio, current assets / current liabilities, asks whether the next twelve months' bills are covered by the next twelve months' resources. Under 1 deserves a hard look; the quick ratio, which removes inventory from the numerator, deserves it harder, because inventory is the current asset that turns to cash slowest and most disappointingly. Liquidity ratios rarely make you money; they keep you from owning the company that dies of a cash squeeze while showing an accounting profit.

9.6Valuation ratios as a first screen

Valuation gets two full chapters later; here the ratios serve only as a first filter. P/E, price / earnings per share: what you pay for one rupee of annual profit. P/B, price / book value: essential for lenders, whose assets are financial. EV/EBITDA: enterprise value over operating earnings, the multiple that ignores capital structure and suits cross-company comparison. Each is a price tag, not a verdict: 28 times earnings can be cheap for a 24%-ROCE compounder and 9 times can be expensive for a leveraged business one bad year from trouble. The peers below make that concrete.

9.7Worked examples and cases

Case 1: two listed peers, one full health check

Two companies, same industry, same revenue. Peer A runs clean; Peer B runs hot. The ten-ratio read:

RatioPeer A (quality)Peer B (leveraged)Reading
Operating margin22%11%A prices better or runs leaner
ROE21%18%Close, until you check leverage
ROCE24%9%A earns well on all capital; B barely beats its loan rate

Notice the trap the last row springs. A screen sorted by low P/E puts Peer B at the top and Peer A at the bottom, the exact opposite of their health. B's 18% ROE seduces until ROCE and debt-to-equity expose it as leverage; its 9 P/E is not a bargain, it is the market pricing the interest cover of 1.8x. Cheap and good are different axes, and the ratio kit keeps them separate.

Case 2: the ten-ratio health check as a ritual

Run it on any company in this order, one line each, fifteen minutes: operating margin (trend), ROCE (vs cost of capital), ROE (then check the debt behind it), debt-to-equity, interest cover, cash conversion cycle (trend), current ratio, CFO-to-profit over five years (Chapter 8's lie detector), promoter pledge percentage (Chapter 10), and only then a valuation multiple. Nine health questions before the one price question. Investors who reverse the order, price first, health later, are the natural buyers of every Peer B.
