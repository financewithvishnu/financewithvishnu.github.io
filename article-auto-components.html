Auto components: follow the part, the platform and the cash

By Finance With Vishnu · Source period: approximately August 2025 · Exact class date unconfirmed

A car can become more valuable to a component supplier without the manufacturer building more cars. A basic badge may become an illuminated logo. A simple lamp may become a more complex lighting system. More products may be fitted to each model. But the supplier earns only on the parts it actually wins, produces, delivers and gets paid for.

That distinction is the starting point. An expanding vehicle market, a successful customer and increasing content per vehicle are separate growth drivers. Each can help. None removes price negotiations, model changes, quality costs or the money needed to fund production.

Study window: this is an original explanation of an approximately August 2025 framework, with the exact class day unconfirmed. FY2024-25 company reports and ACMA's FY2025 industry review provide dated checks. They are not current order-status audits, company rankings or investment recommendations. All worked financial examples are hypothetical.

1. What kind of auto-component business is this?

Auto components include very different activities: mechanical parts, lighting, interiors, sensors, instrument displays, electronics and complete assemblies. A supplier making to the customer's drawing differs from one that designs a system, owns relevant technology or sells replacement parts under its own brand.

Also separate the vehicle categories and selling channels. Two-wheelers, passenger vehicles, commercial vehicles, tractors and off-road equipment have different production and replacement cycles. A business concentrated in one successful passenger-vehicle model does not have the same demand exposure as a diversified replacement-parts supplier.

ACMA's July 2025 review defines its FY2025 industry scope across OEM supply, aftermarket and exports, including captive and smaller suppliers. It excludes tyres, batteries, paints and consumables.[6] Its reported industry total is therefore not a measure of every rupee spent on every vehicle-related product.

2. How does vehicle production become supplier revenue?

A useful starting bridge is relevant vehicle production multiplied by the supplier's fitment share and realised content per fitted vehicle. Then add other customers, replacement sales and separate activities. This is a model to reconcile with disclosures, not permission to multiply the entire automobile market by management's highest kit-value ambition.

Suppose a customer produces 100,000 vehicles. A supplier's parts are fitted on 60%, at Rs 2,000 per fitted vehicle. Revenue is Rs 12 crore: 100,000 multiplied by 60% multiplied by Rs 2,000. If customer output rises 10% and content rises 20%, with fitment unchanged, revenue becomes Rs 15.84 crore, up 32%.

The growth drivers multiply; they do not simply add to 30%. But they can also reverse. An order lost on the replacement model, a lower-specification variant gaining share or a customer's production cut can offset the content improvement. Track the relevant production base, not only industry retail registrations or a broad SUV-growth headline.

3. What does higher content per vehicle really mean?

Content can rise because more parts are fitted, a supplier wins a larger assembly, the product becomes more complex, or higher-priced variants become more popular. Raw-material pass-through and acquisitions can also raise reported revenue without creating the same increase in underlying value added.

SJS's FY2025 report discusses decorative and functional products, technology additions and kit-value ambitions. Those ambitions are opportunities, not achieved revenue on every vehicle. Its report also connects portfolio changes to acquisitions.[1] I would ask which models and variants use the new product, what proportion of production receives it, when supply begins and what contribution remains after the added cost.

A premium feature can create extra engineering, electronics, testing and tooling expense. The supplier needs to earn an adequate return on that work. A larger invoice is not automatically better economics. Nor is every new component protected forever: technology, alternative designs and customers' cost-reduction programmes can change the competitive position.

4. Is a nomination the same as a firm order?

Being selected for a vehicle programme can establish an important commercial relationship. It still leaves questions about the production start, programme life, variants, annual volumes, prices and the customer's right to change plans. A lifetime business estimate may depend on production forecasts across several years.

Read the exact order-book definition. Does it represent annual revenue, lifetime revenue, development work or a pipeline of requests? Is acquired business included? Has the programme started producing? A large number without those definitions can create false confidence.

Even a long relationship must be renewed economically. A supplier that spends heavily before launch can face a painful delay if the customer's model arrives late or sells poorly. Technical success and sufficient commercial volume are different milestones.

5. Why must revenue mix and order-book mix stay separate?

The current revenue mix describes business already recognised in a period. Order-book mix describes future business under the company's chosen definition. They can point in the same direction without being interchangeable.

Lumax Industries' FY2025 report gives a useful example: LED lighting contributed 58% of FY2025 revenue, compared with 39% in FY2024, while 88% of the reported order book was LED-based.[3] Calling 88% the achieved revenue share would materially misstate the period. Nor does a future LED-heavy mix by itself establish the margin on every programme.

For each mix claim, record the denominator and date: revenue, units, order book, development projects or market opportunity. Then test the economic mechanism. Does the new product improve contribution, require more purchased electronics, change tooling needs or create higher warranty exposure? The label alone cannot answer.

6. When does technical qualification create an advantage?

A component has to work inside a larger system. Its dimensions, appearance, durability, software behaviour or safety performance may matter to the customer's entire vehicle. Repeated production quality and reliable delivery can therefore be valuable beyond the cost of the material.

Qualification is still not an automatic monopoly. Ask who owns the design and tools, whether another supplier is approved, how quickly customers can switch and what happens when the vehicle platform changes. A supplier may have high switching costs within one programme but intense competition when the next programme is awarded.

Pricol's FY2025 report describes reliability testing, manufacturing capabilities and customer delivery recognition.[4] These are company-reported examples of operational capability, not independent proof of permanent pricing power. Examine rejection costs, customer claims, warranty provisions, on-time delivery and the spending needed to keep capability current. A low defect rate is valuable only alongside an economic selling price and responsible production controls.

7. Can raw-material pass-through distort the margin?

If a contract adjusts selling prices for an input-cost change, revenue can rise even when the supplier's rupee value added does not. The reported margin percentage may fall without the same deterioration in rupee operating profit. Conversely, a lag in recovery can create a genuine temporary loss.

Check the adjustment formula, review period, scrap recovery, currency exposure and claims outstanding. Customers may also expect productivity savings or price reductions over a programme's life. A supplier can win revenue yet keep little of the gain if the customer captures most of the benefit. Compare rupee contribution, cash and capital returns as well as percentage margin.

8. Is a fast-growing OEM a strength or a risk?

It can be both. A successful customer can lift supplier volumes and plant utilisation. Concentration makes the supplier more sensitive to that customer's model launches, sourcing strategy and bargaining power. A great year for one model is not diversification.

In a hypothetical Rs 100 revenue business, one customer accounts for Rs 50. If that customer's purchases fall 20%, with other sales unchanged, total revenue falls to Rs 90. If the rest of the business grows 10%, total becomes Rs 95, still down 5%. Supplier growth needs a weighted customer bridge, not a list of impressive customer logos.

Concentration also appears below the company level: a plant can depend on one platform even when group customer mix looks broad. Inspect factory location, programme dependence, receivables and utilisation. A customer bringing work in-house can be as important as losing share to another external supplier. Do not infer a captive contract merely because a plant is near an OEM.

9. How should we separate acquired growth from organic growth?

An acquisition can add products, customers, technology and revenue immediately. Organic growth measures progress on a consistent business base. Both can create value, but they require different analysis. Consolidating more of an existing subsidiary can also change the profit attributable to shareholders differently from the operating revenue shown.

Pricol's FY2025 report says its subsidiary acquired the injection-moulded plastic-component solutions division of Sundaram Auto Components on 31 January 2025.[4] That is a specific acquired division, not a purchase of every business with a similar group name. Compare the consolidation period before treating the next year's reported growth as all organic.

Lumax Auto Technologies' FY2025 report describes several partnerships and acquisition activity, including IAC-related ownership changes.[2] It is a different listed company from Lumax Industries. Always confirm the legal entity. Then include purchase consideration, debt, minority interests, integration costs and the returns on the acquired capital. Higher consolidated EBITDA is not enough.

10. Does electrification make every traditional component obsolete?

No single EV label answers that. A battery-electric vehicle changes the propulsion architecture, but still needs many body, interior, lighting, chassis, thermal and electronic functions. The design and value of those functions can change. Hybrids and different vehicle classes can create different transition paths.

The right unit is the actual product and application. Which part is specific to an internal-combustion engine? Which works across several propulsion types? Which needs redesign, new customer qualification or licensing? An existing customer relationship does not guarantee an award for the next architecture.

Lumax Auto Technologies' FY2025 discussion describes evaluating products across alternative powertrain and connected-vehicle technologies.[2] Treat that as a strategy to test through development milestones, production launches and returns. Spending on future capability is not already profitable future revenue. Avoid claiming every engine-related supplier disappears on a fixed date or every electronics supplier wins.

11. Why is the replacement market different?

OEM supply depends heavily on new-vehicle production and programme share. Replacement demand relates to vehicles already in use, their age, utilisation, failure or wear, repair economics and the route through which parts reach workshops. The installed fleet and annual new production are different demand bases.

A replacement-parts brand can need distribution stock, workshop trust, availability and safeguards against unsuitable or counterfeit products. A component designed to last longer may create a different replacement pattern. It is unsafe to assume every original part generates a predictable annual replacement annuity.

Bosch Limited's FY2025 report discusses its powertrain and mobility-aftermarket activities separately.[5] That distinction is more useful than treating all component revenue as one OEM cycle. Also distinguish Bosch Limited from the entire international Bosch Group: a global technology presentation does not show that every business belongs to the Indian listed company.

12. How can supplier growth consume cash?

New programmes can require tools, machines, development work, inventory and receivables before stable cash arrives. Tooling may be customer-funded, supplier-funded or recovered through piece prices, depending on the arrangement. The research file should establish the actual terms rather than assume tooling is free.

Higher production also needs working capital. A hypothetical Rs 500 crore business adding Rs 100 crore revenue needs Rs 20 crore extra capital if incremental working capital is 20% of sales, before any new fixed assets. Add Rs 30 crore for equipment and tools, and the total commitment becomes Rs 50 crore.

If profit reaches only Rs 4 crore, the same simplified return falls to 8%. A delayed launch can make the early cash result worse. Track the full funding requirement, customer recoveries, maintenance, utilisation and debt, not only the hoped-for steady-state margin.

13. What should the tracking sheet explain?

Start with products, customers, platforms and selling channels. Build the volume-fitment-content bridge. Separate organic growth, acquisitions, raw-material adjustments and currency. Record what order book means, the expected production start and the capital already committed.

Then connect quality and delivery performance to rupee contribution, customer concentration, working capital and incremental return. Compare a supplier with others doing similar work, on aligned periods and definitions. An electronics assembly, a branded repair product and a forged part should not receive one margin benchmark simply because each goes into a vehicle.

The durable thesis explains why the supplier is difficult to replace, what it earns after serving the customer, and how much cash shareholders must reinvest to keep that position. More vehicles and better features can help. The part, the programme, the contract and the capital decide who keeps the benefit.
