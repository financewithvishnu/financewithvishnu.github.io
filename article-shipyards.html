Shipyards: why a large order book is only the beginning

By Finance With Vishnu · Source period: approximately autumn 2025 · Sector study

A shipyard can have years of work ahead and still face a difficult year financially. Contracts are large, construction takes time and cash moves according to terms that do not necessarily match reported revenue. A busy yard is not automatically a profitable investment.

The industry becomes easier to understand when we separate the company building a vessel from the company operating it, and both from the suppliers making equipment. They share a maritime theme but earn money in different ways.

I would follow a shipbuilding order through design, procurement, construction, acceptance and collection. At every stage, ask who carries the cost, the delay risk and the obligation to finish the work.

Study window: the shipbuilding value-chain and company discussions from approximately autumn 2025. Exact class days are unconfirmed. Historical illustrations use FY2024-25 company reports and the Cabinet's 24 September 2025 maritime-package announcement. The IMO source explains energy-efficiency requirements and ongoing review, not a forecast that every existing ship must be replaced. This is a business study, not a current order-book, tender-status or valuation update. Operational military details are outside its scope.

1. Who builds the vessel, and who earns the freight?

A shipyard constructs, repairs or modifies vessels for customers. A shipping operator uses vessels to transport cargo or provide another service. A port handles cargo and vessel calls, while a terminal may handle a particular cargo stream or storage activity.

These businesses can respond differently to the same event. Higher freight earnings may encourage an operator to order new ships. Those orders can support a shipyard's workload. Later, the additional fleet capacity can put pressure on freight rates even while the yard is still completing its contracts.

Do not use the margin or valuation of one layer as a shortcut for another. A long shipbuilding contract is not a freight charter. A port concession is not ownership of the vessels calling there.

This article stays with yards and their suppliers. Commercial vessel operations, ports and terminals deserve their own study.

2. Is every shipyard competing for the same work?

No. The product, location, infrastructure, workforce and design capability determine which projects a yard can realistically deliver. A large piece of waterfront land is not enough.

Cochin Shipyard's FY2025 report describes shipbuilding and ship repair across commercial and defence customers. GRSE's report describes shipbuilding, repair and engineering activities. Mazagon Dock's report describes its own shipbuilding programmes and associated capabilities. These are different mixes, not three identical businesses distinguished only by market value. [1, 2, 3]

Yard capacity also needs a unit. Number of vessels, vessel size, available dock space, work stages and the ability to handle several projects at once do not mean the same thing. A yard suited to one class of vessel cannot be assumed to accept every larger or more complex project.

I would compare relevant capacity with the work actually contracted. Also ask which constraint matters next: a dock, skilled workers, design work, equipment supply, subcontractors or customer acceptance. Capacity spending creates an asset before it creates a proven stream of cash.

3. Why is shipbuilding an integration business?

A completed vessel combines structure, propulsion, power, piping, electrical equipment, controls, accommodation and other systems appropriate to its purpose. The yard does not necessarily manufacture each item itself.

Its work includes coordinating designs, suppliers, fabrication, assembly, outfitting, inspection and delivery. Outfitting means installing and integrating equipment and systems into the vessel. A small delayed item can affect a much larger schedule if later work depends on it.

That creates opportunities for specialised suppliers, but avoid treating an illustrative cost split as universal. A commercial cargo vessel and a highly customised vessel can have very different equipment mixes. Contract boundaries also differ: the customer may provide some equipment directly.

Triveni's FY2025 power-transmission discussion is a useful supplier example. It describes industrial gear products, aftermarket activities and a defence-related marine offering. The same division serves several end markets. Its entire revenue should not be relabelled as shipbuilding revenue. [6]

I would map the product and the contracting customer before estimating the supplier's share of a vessel's value. Being somewhere in the chain does not establish either a dominant position or a large contribution to the listed parent.

4. What does an order book actually establish?

An order book records a company's disclosed contracted work under its own definition. It can support visibility, but its value depends on execution timing, scope, pricing, cancellation terms and the cost still required to finish.

A pipeline is different. It may include possible tenders, projects awaiting decisions and contracts the company hopes to win. Lowest-bidder status is another stage again. It should not be treated as an unconditional signed order with a known allocation and delivery schedule.

Do not add the full opportunity pipeline to the current order book and call the total guaranteed revenue. Nor does applying an arbitrary discount make the pipeline certain.

I would keep project-level status, the source date and the next required milestone visible. Historical bids are not assumed to be outstanding today. Any current tender claim needs a fresh filing or customer announcement.

5. Does five years of coverage mean five years of growth?

Order-book coverage is often calculated as outstanding contracted value divided by annual revenue. It is a useful scale check, not a delivery calendar or a promise of uninterrupted growth.

Suppose a hypothetical yard has ₹10,000 crore of work and annual revenue of ₹2,000 crore. The ratio is five times. But the work could be spread unevenly, with design and procurement preceding the most intensive construction stage. Different projects may peak in different years.

Margin also matters. A large low-return contract can consume capacity that cannot then be sold to another customer. If costs rise faster than the contract allows the yard to recover, more revenue can coincide with weaker profitability.

I would build a schedule from disclosed milestones and realistic capacity rather than dividing one total by another. Where project detail is limited, use scenarios and label the uncertainty. The most attractive pipeline number is not a substitute for understanding the existing contracts.

6. When is revenue earned before the ship is delivered?

Shipbuilding revenue does not always wait until the completed vessel changes hands. The accounting depends on the contract and the applicable policy.

Cochin Shipyard's FY2025 financial statements describe recognising revenue over time for relevant shipbuilding and repair work, with progress measured under the methods set out in its policy. They also explain that amounts recognised as revenue need not match amounts billed or certified by the customer. [1]

This is why launch, revenue recognition, billing, final delivery and payment are separate events. A launch is a visible construction milestone. It does not by itself establish that all work is complete or every performance obligation has been met.

A contract asset can arise when recognised revenue exceeds the amount billed or certified under the relevant terms. It is not the same as unrestricted cash in the bank. Customer advances can create contract liabilities because the company still owes work.

I would read the actual recognition policy and related notes. Do not assume every yard uses one identical percentage-of-completion formula, or that a particular stage always carries a predetermined profit margin.

7. How can a yard have cash while its suppliers need working capital?

The yard may receive customer advances or milestone payments. A supplier can have different terms with that yard, including payment after delivery, inspection or acceptance. Cash support at the top of the chain need not pass through on the same timetable.

A hypothetical project receives ₹30 crore in customer advances and spends ₹20 crore early on. It temporarily holds ₹10 crore of cash. That is not automatically surplus cash available to shareholders. The company still needs to complete the contracted work.

Similarly, a supplier can recognise a sale while waiting for collection. Material, work in progress, finished goods and receivables can tie up money even when its reported margin looks attractive.

A debt-free balance sheet does not prove that recent profit has converted into cash. Past equity funding, customer advances, earlier cash reserves or asset sales can also explain the absence of borrowings. Reconcile the cash-flow statement and changes in contract balances rather than relying on one debt number.

I would compare the funding of each layer independently. Customer-funded construction can be attractive, but the associated obligations and the timing of future spending remain part of the valuation.

8. Why can a small cost overrun matter so much?

A long contract exposes the yard to changes in material, equipment, labour, currency and subcontracting costs. Whether those changes can be recovered depends on the terms. Fixed-price and cost-reimbursable arrangements should not be modelled as if they transfer the same risk.

In an original simplified example, a ₹100 crore contract has expected costs of ₹90 crore and a ₹10 crore project surplus. If costs rise to ₹95 crore without a price adjustment, the surplus falls to ₹5 crore. A cost increase of about 5.6% halves the surplus.

Mazagon Dock's FY2025 report discusses provisions and equipment-cost increases relative to earlier bidding assumptions. This illustrates why a healthy order pipeline does not eliminate risk inside existing contracts. It is historical disclosure, not a current estimate of losses on any programme. [3]

Delay penalties, warranty obligations and changes in estimated completion costs also need attention. I would look for contract protections and contingencies, then stress-test the risks that remain with the company.

9. Which earnings should be treated as repeatable?

Reported profit can include operating performance, interest income, provision changes and other items. The categories and the business reason behind them matter more than a single headline margin.

Mazagon Dock's FY2025 statements separately disclose other income, including interest, and operating-revenue items including reversals of liquidated damages. These are real accounting disclosures. They should not all be assumed to recur at the same level indefinitely. [3]

Where customer advances support a large deposit balance, interest income can contribute materially to profit. Ask what happens as those funds are spent, new advances arrive or interest rates change. Also check any interest obligation to the customer.

Do not compare one company's profit-before-tax margin directly with another's operating margin. Reconcile the numerator, denominator and treatment of other income first.

I would normalise earnings using a range, with clear assumptions for project mix, provisions and cash balances. A high historical margin deserves explanation before extrapolation. An adjustment is useful only if it helps explain the business rather than merely improving the investment story.

10. Is repair a simpler version of shipbuilding?

Repair serves an installed fleet rather than only demand for new vessels. It can include maintenance, refurbishment, conversion or more extensive work. The mix and contract duration vary.

Cochin Shipyard's FY2025 report discusses ship repair as a distinct activity and describes its infrastructure investments. GRSE also describes repair work alongside shipbuilding and engineering. These examples support separating the activities rather than assuming every yard earns solely from new construction. [1, 2]

Repair demand can be recurring at an industry level without every contract being a subscription. The yard still needs customers, suitable capacity, equipment, skilled workers and timely execution.

Higher margins in one reporting period can reflect the type of repair, contract terms, capacity use or the mix of work. Do not apply the best historical repair margin to all future shipbuilding revenue.

I would assess the repair business through customer mix, repeat work, capacity utilisation, scheduling and cash conversion. Expansion into a new repair facility should be judged by actual utilisation and returns after the ramp, not its opening ceremony.

11. What does a policy package change?

Policy can help financing, infrastructure and the economics of establishing capacity. It does not award every project to a listed company or remove the need to compete and execute.

On 24 September 2025, the Cabinet announced a ₹69,725 crore maritime package. The announcement included extending shipbuilding financial assistance to March 2036, a ₹25,000 crore Maritime Development Fund and a shipbuilding development scheme. These are dated policy decisions, not revenue already earned by shipyards. [4]

Before assigning a financial benefit, establish eligibility, the relevant scheme, payment conditions and whether the company has actually qualified. Avoid converting the entire package into an assumed order book.

A memorandum of understanding with another shipbuilder is similarly an early piece of evidence. It may support learning or cooperation, but its effect depends on binding scope, investment, execution and customer contracts.

I would keep policy, partnership, capability and contracted revenue in separate rows. That allows us to recognise a helpful industry environment without confusing an announced ambition with shareholder cash.

12. Do environmental rules force every owner to buy a new ship?

No. Environmental requirements can affect vessel efficiency, operating decisions, upgrades and newbuilding demand, but their consequences are not identical for every ship.

The IMO explains the Energy Efficiency Existing Ship Index and annual Carbon Intensity Indicator framework, including requirements that took effect in 2023 for applicable ships. Its guidance also describes operational and technical responses and a continuing review process. [5]

That is not evidence that all older vessels must be scrapped at one age or replaced by a particular fuel technology. The relevant vessel, trade, economics and regulatory scope must be checked.

For a yard or equipment supplier, an environmental theme becomes a business opportunity only when there is a deliverable customers are willing and able to buy. New designs can also create development, sourcing and execution risk.

I would use regulation as a prompt for research, not a ready-made revenue forecast. Separate industry need from orders won, and separate a technically possible product from a profitable delivery record.

13. What belongs in the investment research file?

For a supplier, add its actual product contribution, customer concentration and approval scope. For a diversified group, establish how much the maritime activity contributes before treating the entire company as a shipbuilding proxy.

I would gain confidence when orders convert into accepted work, sensible project returns and cash after the required investment. I would be more cautious when opportunity pipelines grow faster than the evidence of execution.

A large backlog can make the next several years look visible. Good research asks how much value remains after the company has done all the work it promised.
