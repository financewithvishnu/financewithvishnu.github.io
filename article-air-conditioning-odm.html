Air conditioning and ODM: the brand, the factory and the summer

By Finance With Vishnu · Source period: approximately August 2025 · Exact class date unconfirmed

The name on an air conditioner and the company that designed and built it are often different businesses. A brand can sell millions of units without bending a single sheet of metal. A factory can run at full capacity without its name appearing on the carton. Both can be good businesses, but they answer to different economics.

Add one more twist: most of the year's demand arrives in a few hot months. Decisions about what to build, where to stock it and who bears the risk are all taken before the weather confirms them. Understanding this industry means following the brand, the factory and the summer at the same time.

FY2024-25 annual reports and 2025 investor presentations provide dated checks. They are not current market-share rankings, order books or investment recommendations. 1. Who actually makes the air conditioner?

Separate four roles. The brand owner designs the product range, sets prices, advertises and answers to the customer. The original design manufacturer, or ODM, designs and builds the physical product, often for several competing brands at once. Component suppliers make the heat exchangers, motors, circuit boards and plastics inside. The channel and service network sells, installs and repairs.

The same listed company may sit in more than one box. A brand can own factories; an ODM can launch its own label. The first question is never "is this a good AC company" but "which role earns this rupee of revenue?"

2. Why is cooling demand seasonal but growing?

Air conditioners sell hardest when it is hottest. That makes the year lumpy: demand concentrates in the summer months and around heat waves, while factories and finances run all year. Amber Enterprises' FY2024-25 report describes an industry that remains seasonal and weather-influenced, yet capable of sustaining long-term growth through those swings.[3]

The structural story is low penetration meeting hotter years. The same report puts household RAC penetration in India at roughly 10%, against about 68 to 80% in countries such as China and Malaysia, cites 2024 as India's hottest year since 1901, and projects industry growth of 15 to 20% a year over five years, driven by rising incomes, urbanisation, replacement demand and consumer financing.[3] Treat that projection as a company's cited estimate, not a promise. The analytical point is the shape: a growing category whose annual results still swing with the weather.

3. Why do factories ship before the summer?

Because the channel must be full when the heat arrives. Blue Star's Q4 FY2025 presentation, dated 8 May 2025, says its room air-conditioner business grew strongly in the January to March quarter "in anticipation of robust summer season demand".[2] That phrase is the industry's calendar in one line: revenue is booked when units ship into distribution, weeks or months before a customer carries one home.

This is why I separate sell-in from sell-through. Sell-in is what the manufacturer ships to distributors and retailers; sell-through is what customers actually buy. A great first quarter of shipments followed by a weak summer does not create demand, it creates a stocked channel that orders less later. Voltas' FY2024-25 report from the brand side stresses demand forecasting, an agile supply chain for peak-season availability and advanced inventory management.[1] Those are the disciplines that keep sell-in honest.

4. What does the brand actually own?

A cooling brand owns demand generation and the customer relationship: the range, the pricing ladder, advertising, the dealer network, installation and after-sales service. Voltas' FY2024-25 report describes over 30,000 customer touchpoints for its room-AC business, expansion across traditional retail, modern trade, exclusive brand outlets and e-commerce, in-shop demonstrators, and a customer satisfaction index above 90%, while sustaining what it describes as market leadership through intensifying competition and pricing pressure.[1]

Those assets explain both the margin and the vulnerability. Distribution and service are expensive to build and slow to replicate, which protects the brand. But the brand also carries the demand risk: it commits to ranges, prices and stock before it knows the summer. Voltas reported its highest-ever consolidated total income of Rs 15,737 crore in FY2024-25, up 24%, with profit after tax of Rs 834 crore against Rs 248 crore the year before, helped by strong air-conditioner demand and the summer surge.[1] A brand captures the upside of a good season directly, and absorbs a bad one the same way.

5. What does an ODM actually own?

An original design manufacturer owns the product development and the factory. Brands bring volume targets and a specification; the ODM designs the unit, builds it and earns a manufacturing margin without owning the customer. Amber Enterprises' FY2024-25 report traces the climb up this ladder: customers it once served with gas charging have been converted to full ODM relationships, and it describes itself as the first Indian B2B player to offer ODM solutions in window top-throw inverter models, tropical high-efficiency splits and cassette air conditioners for light commercial use.[3]

The same report shows the scale and the deliberate dilution of dependence: room-AC completely built units are 43% of Amber's consolidated business, down from 72% in FY2017-18, with consumer durables contributing Rs 7,329 crore in FY2024-25 alongside electronics and railway subsystems.[3] An ODM's assets are design capability, tooling, quality systems and capacity. It wins when brands outsource more; it suffers when a large customer dual-sources, insources or has a weak season.

6. Why do ODM rankings need a denominator?

EPACK Durable's Q4 FY2025 presentation describes the company as the second-largest RAC ODM player in India with a 24% market share, citing a Frost and Sullivan report, measured by domestically manufactured ODM units in FY2023.[5] PG Electroplast's FY2024-25 report calls it the preferred ODM partner for over 35 brands, and in one section describes itself as the second-largest ODM manufacturer for room ACs while a timeline page says it became India's largest.[4]

Two companies, two overlapping claims. This is normal in industry material, and it is a useful habit to check the denominator before using any ranking: units or revenue, ODM only or OEM included, which fiscal year, domestic production or sales, self-declared or third-party measured. The ranking matters far less than the trend each company's own numbers confirm: more brands outsourcing, and more units moving through Indian ODM factories.

7. What does localisation change?

An air conditioner is a bundle of specialised parts: heat exchangers, condensers, motors, printed circuit board assemblies, sheet metal, tubing, plastics and a compressor. Amber's report lists exactly these among its manufactured components, alongside complete window units, indoor units and outdoor units, plus air conditioners for railways, metros, buses and defence.[3] The more of that bundle is made in-house or in India, the shorter the supply chain, the lower the currency exposure and the faster a design change reaches production.

Policy pushed in the same direction. Amber's report describes the Production Linked Incentive scheme as pivotal in reducing import dependence and fostering backward integration, spurring investment in high-value components such as compressors, heat exchangers, motors and PCBs.[3] PG Electroplast's report records PLI approval and capacity of over 2 million indoor units and 1 million outdoor units a year, with a new Bhiwadi facility adding scale.[4] For each company, the tracking question is concrete: which components are made in-house, sourced locally, or still imported, and what does each choice do to cost, quality control and lead time?

8. How do ODM economics differ from brand economics?

The ODM sells manufacturing capacity and design output to business customers at a thinner margin per unit, and wins through volume, utilisation, yield and customer additions. The brand sells to consumers at a wider margin, then spends much of the difference on distribution, advertising, channel credit and service. EPACK's presentation shows the focus: roughly 70 to 75% of its revenue comes from room air conditioners, with small domestic appliances, components and large appliances making up the rest.[5] PG Electroplast reports Rs 3,009 crore of room-AC revenue for FY2024-25.[4] Voltas, on the brand side, reports Rs 15,737 crore of consolidated total income across cooling and other businesses.[1]

Neither model is superior in principle. The ODM's risk is a full factory and an empty order book; the brand's risk is a full channel and a weak summer. When comparing companies, match the model first, then judge execution inside it. Margins across the two models measure different things and should not be lined up side by side as if they were the same business.

9. Who carries the warranty?

The customer sees one promise, but the cost is divided by contract. The brand warrants the product to the buyer. The manufacturer in turn warrants its work to the brand. Amber's FY2024-25 accounts show how the factory side books this: the group gives warranties on certain products, undertakes to repair or replace failures during the warranty period, and carries a provision under Ind AS 37 estimated from historical warranty data on similar products, with outflows expected within a year.[3]

The study question is where each failure's cost lands: a workmanship defect, a design flaw, a bad component batch and a mishandled installation can all be allocated differently between brand, ODM and component supplier. Warranty terms, claim trends and the size of the provision relative to sales tell you who is quietly paying for quality.

10. How does seasonal inventory cut both ways?

Building before the season is unavoidable; building the wrong amount is the risk. Too little stock in a heat wave means lost sales that do not return. Too much stock in a mild summer means carrying cost, aged models and discounting that resets price expectations. Both errors are visible only after the weather has voted.

This is why the same discipline appears on both sides of the industry: Voltas highlights demand forecasting, inventory management and peak-season availability,[1] while Amber describes an industry that is seasonal yet keeps growing through the swings.[3] Watch inventory days, channel fill commentary and end-of-season discounting rather than any single quarter's shipments.

11. What does customer concentration do to an ODM?

An ODM's customers are brands, and brands have choices. A large customer can bring multi-year volume and fill a new plant, but it can also negotiate hard, shift share to a second supplier, or pull production in-house. PG Electroplast's report frames the breadth version of the story: a preferred ODM partner for over 35 leading brands.[4] Amber's report frames the depth version: customers originally served with gas charging converted into full ODM relationships.[3]

For each ODM, record how revenue splits across its top customers, how long those relationships have run, and what would happen to utilisation if the largest one halved its orders. High utilisation earned from one or two big brands is not the same asset as high utilisation earned from fifteen.

12. What resets demand and design?

Three forces periodically reshuffle the product itself. Replacement cycles bring older units back into the market. Energy-efficiency rules push buyers toward higher-rated models and make old designs obsolete. Refrigerant and technology transitions force redesigns that reward factories with real development capability. Amber's report lists replacement cycles and consumer financing among demand drivers, and describes its own new ODM platforms across inverter, tropical high-efficiency and cassette designs.[3] Voltas points to energy-efficient ranges, IoT-enabled products and five-star rated models as growth contributors.[1]

A regulatory or technology reset favours whoever can redesign quickly and retool credibly. It hurts whoever is left holding yesterday's platform and yesterday's inventory. When a new standard is announced, the study question is not only who gains demand, but who bears the write-down and the retooling bill.

13. What should the tracking sheet explain?

My tracking sheet for this industry would answer, with dates: Which role does this company play: brand, ODM, component maker or a mix, and where is each rupee of revenue earned? What share of revenue is seasonal, and how did the last two summers actually turn out against expectations? What do sell-in and sell-through each say, and how full is the channel? Which components are made in-house, sourced locally or imported? For an ODM, how concentrated are customers, how utilised are plants and what new platforms were added? For a brand, how wide is distribution, what does service cover and how is pricing holding? What do the warranty provision and claim trends say about quality? And what inventory position is being carried into the next season?

None of these questions requires predicting the weather. They record whether the business is understood well enough that a hot May, a weak July or a lost customer would be noticed and read correctly. That record is the real output of the study.
