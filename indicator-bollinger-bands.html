Bollinger Bands: the indicator that breathes with the market

By Finance With Vishnu · Indicators series, part 1 · 21 November 2026

1983

The year John Bollinger's bands were published. Forty years later they are still on almost every charting platform, because they answer one simple question better than most tools: is the price high or low compared with its own recent past?

3curves: upper, middle, lower
20 / 2default periods / standard deviations
~90%of prices usually inside the bands

Most indicators tell you where the price has been. Bollinger Bands tell you something more useful: whether today's price is stretched or quiet compared with what is normal for that stock right now. The bands widen when the market gets violent and tighten when it goes to sleep, like a rubber band that breathes with the market.

Short answer: Bollinger Bands are three lines around the price. The middle line is a 20-period average. The upper and lower lines sit two standard deviations above and below it. Price near the upper band is relatively high, price near the lower band is relatively low, and the distance between the bands measures how nervous or calm the market is.

1. Who created Bollinger Bands, and why?

Short answer: John Bollinger, an American analyst (CFA, CMT), created them in the early 1980s and published them in 1983. He wanted bands that adjust themselves, because the fixed bands traders used at the time forced people to keep redrawing them, and emotions crept into every adjustment.[1][3]

To see the problem he solved, start with what came before. Traders have drawn bands around prices for a long time. In 1960 Wilfrid Ledoux connected the monthly highs and lows of the Dow Jones index as a timing tool. Chester Keltner published a 10-day moving-average rule that later became Keltner bands. In the 1970s the most popular tool was the percentage band: take a moving average and draw one line a fixed percent above it and another the same percent below, say 107% and 93%.[1]

Percentage bands have a hidden flaw. A quiet stock and a wild stock need different widths, and the same stock needs different widths in different months. So the trader must keep adjusting the percentage by hand. Bollinger noticed that this is where discipline dies: if you feel bullish, you nudge the bands until the chart looks bullish. The tool that was supposed to keep you honest started agreeing with your mood.[1]

The fix came from options work. While building volatility models in an early spreadsheet called SuperCalc, Bollinger copied a volatility formula down a column of prices and saw that volatility is not fixed at all. It changes constantly. At the time, most people believed volatility was a static property of a stock. His leap: if volatility moves, let volatility set the width of the bands. That is the whole invention in one sentence. The name came later, on live television, when the Financial News Network host Bill Griffeth asked him what those lines were called and he blurted out the alliterative answer: "Bollinger Bands."[1]

2. What exactly are Bollinger Bands?

Short answer: three curves drawn around the price. The middle band is a simple moving average of the last 20 closing prices. The upper band is that average plus two standard deviations. The lower band is the average minus two standard deviations. Together they give a relative definition of high and low: at the upper band the price is high compared with its recent past, and at the lower band it is low.[2]

This relative definition is the entire point. A stock at Rs 500 tells you nothing by itself. A stock at Rs 500 touching its upper band after a calm month tells you the price is stretched. The same Rs 500 sitting on the lower band tells you the opposite. The bands turn a bare number into a location.

Two companions complete the toolkit. %b says exactly where the price sits between the bands as a number. BandWidth says how far apart the bands are. Both come from Bollinger himself, and both show up in section 5.[1]

3. How are the three bands calculated?

Short answer: average the last 20 closes for the middle band. Measure how spread out those 20 closes are (the standard deviation). Add two of those spreads for the upper band, subtract two for the lower band.[2]

Worked example with 5 closes (made up)

Closes: 100, 102, 101, 103, 105.

Step 1, the average: (100 + 102 + 101 + 103 + 105) / 5 = 102.2. That is the middle band.

Step 2, how far each close sits from 102.2: -2.2, -0.2, -1.2, +0.8, +2.8.

Step 3, square those gaps: 4.84, 0.04, 1.44, 0.64, 7.84. Average them: 14.8 / 5 = 2.96.

Step 4, take the square root: standard deviation = 1.72.

Step 5, the bands: upper = 102.2 + 2 x 1.72 = 105.64. Lower = 102.2 - 2 x 1.72 = 98.76.

Real charts use 20 closes, not 5, but the steps are identical. Two details from the creator's own rules are worth knowing. First, the average and the standard deviation both use the same simple average, on purpose, so the maths stays consistent. Second, the defaults are only defaults. If you lengthen the average to 50 periods, widen the bands to 2.1 standard deviations; if you shorten it to 10 periods, narrow them to 1.9. That keeps the bands containing roughly the same share of prices.[2]

Moving average lengthBand width to use
10 periods1.9 standard deviations
20 periods (default)2.0 standard deviations
50 periods2.1 standard deviations

One warning that surprises people. Two standard deviations sounds like it should hold 95% of prices, the number from a textbook normal distribution. Markets are not normal distributions, and 20 prices is a small sample. In practice the default bands hold about 90% of prices, and the creator himself says to make no statistical assumptions from the standard deviation formula.[2]

4. Why do the bands widen and narrow?

Short answer: because the bands are built from volatility, and volatility is never still. Wide bands mean the last 20 bars were violent. Narrow bands mean they were calm. The calmest reading in months is called the Squeeze, and it matters because quiet periods often come before big moves.[2]

Bollinger tracks this with BandWidth: (upper band - lower band) divided by the middle band. When BandWidth falls to its lowest level in months, the spring is coiled. The Squeeze does not tell you which way the price will break, only that the market is storing energy. Direction has to come from somewhere else: the breakout itself, the wider trend, or a second indicator.[2]

5. What are %b and BandWidth?

Short answer: two companion indicators Bollinger built from the bands. %b tells you where the price sits inside the bands as a number: 1.0 at the upper band, 0.5 at the middle, 0 at the lower band, and above 1.0 when the price closes outside. BandWidth tells you how wide the bands are, and its most famous use is spotting the Squeeze.[1][2]

The formulas

%b = (last price - lower band) / (upper band - lower band)

BandWidth = (upper band - lower band) / middle band

Why bother turning a picture into a number? Because numbers can be compared and tested. "Price touched the lower band" is vague. "%b fell to -0.05 while the stock made a higher low" is precise enough to write down, backtest and code. Bollinger lists %b's main uses as spotting divergences, recognising patterns and building trading systems, and notes you can even plot %b on another indicator to replace fixed thresholds.[2]

6. What mistakes do beginners make with the bands?

Short answer: treating every touch of a band as a signal. The creator's own rule list says the opposite: a tag of the upper band is not, by itself, a sell signal, and a tag of the lower band is not, by itself, a buy signal.[2]

  • Selling every touch of the upper band. In a strong trend the price can ride the upper band for weeks. That is called walking the band, and it is a sign of strength, not weakness.
  • Buying every touch of the lower band. A falling stock can walk down the lower band just as easily. A tag tells you where the price is, not what it will do next.
  • Fading closes outside the bands. A close outside the bands is initially a continuation signal, not a reversal signal. Many volatility breakout systems are built on exactly that.
  • Assuming the 95% rule. The bands hold about 90% of prices in practice. Prices are not normally distributed.
  • Stacking lookalike indicators. If you add a second indicator for confirmation, pick one from a different family. A momentum indicator plus a volume indicator can work well together; two momentum indicators mostly repeat each other.

7. How can intraday traders use Bollinger Bands?

Short answer: the same three lines work on 5-minute, 15-minute or hourly bars, as long as each bar contains enough trades to draw a meaningful picture. The two classic intraday setups are the Squeeze breakout and the range bounce, and both still need confirmation.[2]

The Squeeze breakout. When the morning session goes dead quiet and BandWidth shrinks to a multi-day low, the intraday trader waits for the price to close outside the bands with strong volume, in the direction of the break. The close outside the bands is treated as a continuation signal, exactly as the rules describe. The invalidation point is clear: if the price falls straight back inside the bands, the breakout failed.

The range bounce. In a sideways day the bands act like the walls of the range. A tag of the lower band followed by a strong reversal candle, with %b turning up, is the classic fade-the-tag setup. The mistake section explains why the tag alone is not enough: the reversal evidence has to appear before the trade, not after the hope.

Intraday adds two practical rules of its own. Activity matters: a 5-minute bar with three trades in it draws bands that mean nothing, which is why the creator says the bars must contain enough activity to give a robust picture. And risk comes first: with only a few hours per trade, position size and a pre-decided stop matter more than the indicator.

8. How can swing traders use Bollinger Bands?

Short answer: on daily bars with the default 20 and 2, swing traders use the bands to frame pullbacks in trends and to spot W bottoms and M tops, checking %b to see whether momentum agrees.[2]

The W bottom. This is Bollinger's classic reversal shape. The price makes a first low at or below the lower band, bounces, then falls again. The second low is the test: if it holds above the lower band while the price makes a similar or even lower low, the bands are saying the selling is weaker the second time. %b makes this visible: the second low shows a clearly higher %b reading than the first. That divergence, price flat or lower with momentum higher, is the setup. The trade is still not the pattern itself; entry comes after the price turns up from the second low.[1]

Pullbacks in an uptrend. In a stock that is walking the upper band, swing traders watch the dips back to the middle band. A pullback that finds support near the 20-period average, inside a healthy uptrend, is the bread-and-butter continuation setup. The middle band works here because the creator chose it to describe the intermediate-term trend, not to be a crossover tool.[2]

9. How can long-term investors use them?

Short answer: on weekly or monthly bars, with the longer 50-period setting and 2.1 standard deviations, the bands become a thermometer for how stretched a long-term holding is, useful for pacing entries and trims rather than timing trades.[2]

An investor who buys in installments can use the bands to add discipline. Adding when the price sits near the lower weekly band, and pausing or trimming when it rides the upper band for months, replaces guesswork with a rule. The bands answer the question they were built for, "is the price high or low on a relative basis?", at whatever speed you trade.[1]

One limit matters more here than anywhere else: the bands know nothing about value. A stock can hug its lower monthly band and still be expensive if the business is deteriorating. For long-term decisions the bands are a timing layer on top of fundamental work, never a replacement for it.

10. Which strategies did the creator himself describe?

Short answer: four show up again and again in his own writing: the Squeeze, walking the bands, W bottoms and M tops, and the tag that a second, different kind of indicator refuses to confirm.[1][2]

  • The Squeeze: BandWidth at a multi-month low warns that a big move is being stored up. Direction comes from the break, not from the Squeeze.
  • Walking the bands: repeated tags of one band confirm a trend. Strength stays long, weakness stays short, until the walk ends.
  • W bottoms and M tops: the bands define whether the second push is genuinely weaker, and %b measures it.
  • The unconfirmed tag: Bollinger's original 1980s setup compared a band tag with a volume indicator (Intraday Intensity). A tag of the upper band that volume does not confirm was a sell alert; the first down day was the trigger. The principle generalises: confirm price with something from a different family.

His closing rule frames all of them: the bands do not give continuous advice. They help you find setups where the odds may be in your favour, and then the rest of your process, entry, exit, size, has to do its job.[2]

11. What can go wrong?

Short answer: the same things that go wrong with every indicator: false breaks, lag, and over-fitted settings, plus one trap specific to the bands, assuming a touch means a turn.

  • Whipsaws in ranges. In a choppy market the price crosses the middle band constantly and squeezes lead nowhere. Each failed signal costs a small loss and some brokerage.
  • One-way days. In a panic or a mania the price closes outside the bands again and again. Anyone fading those closes, against rule 8, gets run over.
  • News shocks. Earnings and policy announcements can gap the price straight through both bands. The bands recompute afterwards; the loss happens immediately.
  • Parameter tinkering. Adjusting 20 and 2 until last year's chart looks perfect is curve-fitting. The settings that flattered the past rarely survive the future, which is why the defaults have survived forty years.
  • Lag. The bands are built from the last 20 bars. They describe the present relative to the recent past; they do not see the future.

12. A simple checklist before trusting a band signal

  1. Which timeframe am I trading, and do my bars hold enough activity for the bands to mean anything?
  2. Is this a tag, a walk, a Squeeze or a W or M shape, and does my plan match that specific picture?
  3. What confirms it from a different indicator family, volume or momentum, and have I waited for that confirmation?
  4. Where exactly is this idea wrong, and how much money am I prepared to lose finding out?
  5. Am I treating a tag of a band as a signal, the mistake the creator warns about first?
