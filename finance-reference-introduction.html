The First-Principles Approach of This Website

The unnumbered opening chapter · read this first

Ask a room full of investors why a share has value and you will hear twenty rules: buy low, buy quality, buy the dip, never catch a falling knife. Now ask which of those rules still works when interest rates double, or when a new technology eats an industry. The room goes quiet. This website exists so that you are never that room. Instead of collecting rules, you will learn to rebuild any financial idea from five basic truths, the way an engineer rebuilds a machine from its parts. Once you can do that, no product, pitch or panic can fool you for long.

0.1 What is first-principles thinking in finance?

It means rebuilding every financial product and strategy from five basic blocks, money, time, risk, exchange and information, instead of memorising rules that break when conditions change.

Most of us learn finance the way most of us learn to cook: by recipe. Do this, avoid that, follow the steps and hope. Recipes are fine until the ingredients change, and in finance the ingredients change constantly. Rates rise and fall, new instruments appear, old tricks stop working precisely because everyone knows them.

First-principles thinking is the cook who understands salt, heat and acid instead of memorising dishes. You start from what is provably true, the basic facts that no market can repeal, and you rebuild each idea from there. It is slower on day one and faster forever after, because every new topic becomes an assembly job, not a new subject to memorise.

This matters doubly in India right now. Crores of new investors have entered the market since 2020, and the products sold to them change every year: UPI, BNPL, fractional property, crypto, market-linked debentures. Nobody can memorise a rule for each. Everybody can learn the five blocks they are all built from.

0.2 What are the five building blocks of every financial product?

Money (what is paid), time (when it is paid), risk (who carries the uncertainty), exchange (who trades with whom) and information (who knows what). Every instrument, from a fixed deposit to a credit default swap, is a combination of these five.

Everything in this website, from a savings account to a credit default swap, is assembled from five building blocks:

Money
A trusted IOU. It stores value, measures prices and settles debts.
Time
Money today is worth more than money tomorrow. Every future promise gets discounted.
Risk
Outcomes are uncertain. Every return is payment for bearing some risk.
Exchange
Markets exist to match people who want opposite sides of a trade.
Information
Prices move on what people know, expect and believe.

Keep these five in front of you as you read. Each chapter starts by showing which blocks its subject is made of.

0.3 How is every financial instrument assembled from these blocks?

An instrument is not a thing; it is a bundle of promises about money, time, risk, exchange and information. Lay the familiar ones side by side and the pattern shows:

InstrumentWhat it really is, in primitives
A fixed depositYou lend money (money) for a fixed period (time) at almost no uncertainty (low risk), so you earn a small return.
A shareA slice of a business's future cash (money + time), with no promise attached (high risk), bought in a market (exchange) whose price reacts to news (information).
A bondA loan with a schedule: money now against fixed payments later, priced by time and the borrower's risk.
A futureA promise to exchange something at a set price on a set date: exchange and time, with risk transferred from one side to the other.
An optionThe right, not the duty, to exchange later. You pay a premium for protection against risk, and its price is mostly time and uncertainty.
A swapTwo parties exchange one stream of payments for another, say fixed for floating: pure exchange of risk over time.

Notice what this buys you. When someone offers you a new product you have never heard of, you do not need a rule about it. You ask five questions: whose money moves, over what time, who carries which risk, where does it trade, and what does the seller know that I do not. The Introduction's worked examples below show this questioning in action.

0.4 Why is every investment strategy a bet on one building block?

Because a strategy can only earn by being paid for one of the five: time (compounding), risk (equity, insurance), exchange (market-making), information (a research edge) or money itself (arbitrage). Naming which one a strategy sells is the test of whether it is real.

Strategies feel like magic tricks with names: momentum, arbitrage, compounding, carry. Under the hood, each is a repeatable way to get paid for one primitive:

Strategy familyThe primitive it bets on
Long-term compounding (SIPs, buy and hold)Time: returns reinvested for decades snowball on themselves.
Value and risk-premium investingRisk: you are paid for holding what others find scary, when the fear is overpriced.
ArbitrageExchange: the same thing priced differently in two places at once.
Research-based stock pickingInformation: knowing something true that the price does not yet reflect.
Lending and carry strategiesMoney and time: collecting interest for funding someone else's present.

When a strategy stops working, it is usually because its primitive got crowded or repriced. Reading strategies this way tells you in advance why each one should make money, which is the only honest test of whether it deserves your money.

0.5 Why does this method work in every market?

Rules are local. The five primitives are not. The same blocks rebuild a chit fund in Kerala, a Treasury bond in Washington and a bitcoin wallet anywhere. That is why this website can be India-first in its examples, the NSE, SEBI, the RBI, MCX, UPI, and still teach you to read any market on earth. Countries change the labels; they cannot change the blocks.

0.6 How should you use this website?

  • Read this Introduction once, slowly. Everything else stands on it.
  • Each chapter then opens with its first-principles rebuild, deepens into practice, and closes with worked examples.
  • Every section ends with active-recall questions. Answer them out loud or on paper before moving on; recalling is what moves an idea from the page into your head.

0.7 Worked examples and cases: three rebuilds, side by side

The method is only useful if it works on real products. Here are three everyday ones taken apart with the five questions.

Rebuild 1: a bank fixed deposit

Money: you hand the bank, say, ₹1,00,000. Time: locked for 5 years. Risk: nearly none, since the bank promises the return and deposits are insured up to ₹5 lakh per depositor per bank by DICGC. Exchange: none, there is no market; you deal only with the bank. Information: the rate is known in advance.

Conclusion without any rulebook: an FD pays modestly precisely because it strips out risk, exchange and information. What is left is time, so the return is roughly the price of time, the risk-free rate. If an FD-like product ever offers far more, some removed block has been quietly put back, usually risk.

Rebuild 2: a monthly SIP in an equity index fund

Money: ₹10,000 every month. Time: 20 years. Risk: the stock market's, in full. Exchange: you buy units through the fund, priced daily at NAV. Information: deliberately ignored; an index fund refuses to bet on anyone's stock insight.

The maths of time at work: at a 12% annual return, ₹10,000 a month for 20 years invests ₹24,00,000 and grows to roughly ₹99,00,000. About ₹75,00,000 of the result is the market paying you for time and risk, the two blocks you chose to supply. The SIP adds nothing clever; it simply keeps the supply of time and risk steady through every headline.

Rebuild 3: a Nifty call option

Money: a premium, say ₹80 per unit. Time: the option dies at expiry, and its price melts a little every day. Risk: asymmetric; you can lose only the premium, the seller can lose far more. Exchange: a live auction on the NSE. Information: the price embeds the market's collective guess about how violently the index will move, the implied volatility.

Now you can see why most option buyers lose: they are paying for time and uncertainty, both of which drain value daily, so they must be right about direction and timing before the clock wins. The derivatives part of this website rebuilds every option idea from exactly this footing.

Active recall

  1. Name the five primitives from memory, then check. Which two are missing from a plain bank deposit, and why does that make its return small?
  2. A friend pitches a product paying 12% a year, "guaranteed". Using only the five questions, what do you ask first, and which block almost certainly hides the catch?
  3. Match each to its primitive: arbitrage, compounding, an analyst's edge, a risk premium. Do it without looking back.
  4. Take any instrument you own today and rebuild it: whose money, what time, whose risk, which exchange, whose information?
  5. Why does a recipe-style rule like "never buy when the market is falling" fail exactly when you need it most?
  6. In the SIP rebuild, roughly ₹75 lakh of the ₹99 lakh came from the market paying for two blocks. Which two?
