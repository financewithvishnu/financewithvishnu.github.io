Part XIV · Portfolio Management and Wealth

Chapter 77. Behavioural Finance: The Investor's Own Worst Enemy

Chapter 77 of 85 · about 19 minutes

Every previous chapter assumed an investor who could execute the plan. This one studies why you will not, at the moments it matters most, and what to build so that it does not matter. Behavioural finance is the most personally profitable subject in the book: the returns it protects are yours.

77.1Loss aversion, overconfidence and anchoring

Three bugs in the firmware, documented across forty years of experiments. Loss aversion: a loss hurts roughly twice as much as an equal gain pleases, so the investor refuses to sell losers, the pain of admitting, and sells winners early, the relief of securing, harvesting flowers and watering weeds. Overconfidence: nearly everyone rates themselves above average at investing, the one domain where the average is defined by the market and the market is everyone, and the symptom is trading frequency, which the data ties, study after study, inversely to returns. Anchoring: the brain bolts to the first number it meets, the purchase price, the 52-week high, the target in a headline, and every later judgment orbits it: "it will come back to my buying price" is not analysis but an anchor defending itself. The three compound: the anchored, overconfident, loss-averse brain constructs a private story in which selling is never required, and the account statement tells a different one.

77.2Herd behaviour and FOMO in the app era

Herding is not stupidity; it is a survival instinct misapplied, the crowd was safety on the savannah, and the market prices the instinct against you. The app era industrialised it: the portfolio in your pocket refreshes by the second, the group chat reports everyone's wins and no one's losses, the finfluencer of Chapter 72 manufactures urgency professionally, and the F&O tab is one thumb-flick away, so the interval between fear, greed and action has collapsed from days to seconds. The signatures are recognisable in yourself: checking prices more as the market falls, which measures anxiety, not information; feeling the specific pain of a friend who bought the thing you passed on, envy being the strongest buy signal ever studied; and the creeping belief, after three green years, that you are good at this. The defence is structural, not moral: remove the slot machine from the pocket, the daily-check habit is a position, and it is short your own plan.

77.3Mental accounting and the disposition effect

The brain keeps ledgers that reality does not. Mental accounting: the salary is "safe money", the bonus is "risk money", the trading account is "play money", and the same rupee obeys different rules in each pocket, which is how a household can hold a 9% loan and a 3% deposit simultaneously, the two pockets never introduced. The disposition effect, the formal name for Section 77.1's asymmetry, has been measured in brokerage records across continents: investors sell their winners at roughly one-and-a-half times the rate they sell losers, locking in the tax, the commission and the small gain, and keeping the hope. The repair begins with the consolidated view: one net-worth page, all pockets visible, every rupee judged by its next use rather than its origin story. Money has no memory; the pocket it sits in is not a fact about the future, and the portfolio that knows this outperforms the one with feelings about its history.

77.4Checklists, rules and automation as behaviour design

Willpower is a depreciating asset; the research and the practitioners agree on the same fix, decide once, execute never. The toolkit, in ascending strength. Checklists: a written pre-trade interrogation, what am I buying, why, what is the exit, what would prove me wrong, slows the impulse to the speed of thought. Rules: the investment policy statement of Chapter 75, allocation, rebalancing bands, the 48-hour rule, converts future decisions into present obedience. Automation: the SIP that buys through the crash while you sleep, the auto-escalation that raises savings with the increment, the standing instruction that rebalance which requires no March courage. And the friction audit in reverse: add steps to selling, remove steps from saving, because every interface is a choice architect and you may as well be your own. The deep principle: your future self is a stranger with your passwords, and the plan's job is to make the stranger's worst impulses expensive and best ones automatic.

77.5What 40 years of investor-return studies show

The most important number in this chapter is a gap. Measure what a fund earns, time-weighted, and what its investors earn, money-weighted, and across four decades and every market studied, the investors earn less, one to three percentage points a year, year after year, the "behaviour gap". The mechanism is the cycle you can now name: performance chases, money floods in after the good years and flees after the bad, so the average rupee experiences the fund's worst timing even while the fund's own record looks fine. The gap dwarfs every fee debate, every fund-selection argument, every factor tilt: behaviour is the largest line item in the personal P&L, larger than expense ratios, larger than taxes for the average saver. And it is the one item entirely under your control, or rather, under your architecture's, because the studies' other finding is that the gap closes with exactly the tools of Section 77.4: automated contributions, illiquid wrappers, and advisers whose real product is standing between the client and the exit.

77.6Worked examples and cases

Case 1: why average fund investors earn less than their funds

The chart shows the pattern with illustrative numbers shaped on the published studies: a good fund compounds at 13.1% over a decade while its average investor earns 9.4%. The arithmetic of the gap: the fund's record assumes a rupee present throughout; the investors' rupees arrived late, after the doubling that made the marketing, and left early, during the drawdown that made the news, so the average rupee was absent for the best quarters and fully present for the worst. Each individual decision felt locally rational, buy the proven fund, sell the scary one, and the sequence was systematically ruinous, because both decisions were made by reading the rear-view mirror. The fix is embarrassingly cheap and feels wrong: pick the boring adequate fund, automate the purchase, and forbid the checking. The behaviour gap is the only market anomaly that pays the person who stops trading against it.

Case 2: a panic-sell, reconstructed

March 2020. A salaried professional, ₹40 lakh in equity funds accumulated over nine disciplined years, watches the index fall 38% in five weeks, the portfolio to ₹25 lakh, the news cycle unanimous. On the day of the second lower circuit, he redeems everything, "until things are clear". Things become clear in the only way they ever do: the index doubles over the next eighteen months, and clarity arrives with the market already 60% above his exit. He re-enters, eighteen months later, at higher prices, having converted a temporary mark-to-market loss into a permanent one, ₹15 lakh of foregone recovery, the single most expensive click of his life. Reconstruct the failure precisely, because it was not cowardice: he had no written rule naming what would make him sell, so the decision was made by the news; he checked the portfolio four times a day, so the drawdown was experienced as continuous torture rather than one quarterly statement; and no one in his life was obligated to talk him off the ledge. Every repair is in Section 77.4, written in the subjunctive, waiting for the next crash, which is also waiting.
