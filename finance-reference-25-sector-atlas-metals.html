Part IV · Stocks: The Indian Market, Its Sectors and Its Strategies

Chapter 25. Sector Atlas: Metals, Mining and Materials

Chapter 25 of 85 · about 20 minutes

A tonne of Indian steel is priced in London and decided in China. That one sentence contains the whole sector: metals companies sell into a global price they do not set, with costs they partly control, and their profits are the violent, swinging difference. These are the most cyclical stocks in the Indian market, the ones that quintuple and then give two-thirds back, and this chapter teaches you to hold them by the handle and not the blade.

25.1Commodity producers as price-takers: LME and China

No Indian steelmaker, aluminium smelter or zinc miner sets its product's price. Global benchmarks, the London Metal Exchange for base metals, regional indices for steel, set it, and the single largest force behind those benchmarks is Chinese demand and Chinese supply policy, because China produces and consumes roughly half of most industrial metals. The Indian company is a price-taker twice over: it accepts the global price, adjusted for import parity, freight and duties, and it accepts that a policy meeting in Beijing moves its margins more than any decision in its own boardroom. The investor's consequence: analysing a metals company is analysing a spread, the global price minus the company's cost, and the spread's direction is a macro judgment you must make consciously or not at all.

25.2Cost curves: why position on the curve decides survival

Stack all the world's producers of a metal from cheapest to most expensive and you have the cost curve: the single most useful picture in commodity analysis. The market price, in any sustained period, gravitates toward the cost of the marginal producer, the most expensive tonne the world still needs. Producers to the left of that point earn the difference; producers to the right bleed until they shut. The investor's rules follow directly. In the down-cycle, only the low-cost left of the curve survives with its balance sheet intact, which is when you must own the survivors, not the casualties with the "cheaper" P/E. In the up-cycle, every producer looks like a genius, and the high-cost ones' earnings multiply fastest, which is leverage on the spread, and exactly why they are the ones to sell first when the price turns.

25.3Captive mines and integration as advantage

In India, the cost curve has a domestic twist: iron ore and coal. A steelmaker with captive mines buys its main input at extraction cost while rivals pay market prices or auction premiums; the advantage shows up as margins that stay positive when the industry's unintegrated half goes loss-making. Integration is the closest thing this sector has to a moat: it moves the company left on the cost curve permanently, and it is why two Indian steelmakers of similar size can deserve entirely different multiples. The same logic runs through aluminium (captive bauxite, captive power, the cost of which is half the battle) and cement (captive limestone). When you read a materials company, the first table to find is the raw-material security one: what fraction of each key input is owned, contracted, or hostage to the market.

25.4Cement: a regional, freight-bound business

Cement deserves its own lens because it is a commodity that behaves like a local franchise. The product is cheap per tonne and ruinously expensive to truck, so competition is regional: a plant's true rivals are the plants within a few hundred kilometres, and pricing power depends on regional capacity, not national headlines. The economics to track: realisations per tonne (the price net of everything), variable cost per tonne (limestone, power, fuel, freight), and the spread between them, the EBITDA per tonne that every analyst in the sector quotes. Consolidation has steadily improved the industry's discipline, and capacity announcements, Chapter 23's supply forecasts, are the early warning that the discipline is about to be tested in your region of interest.

25.5The capex supercycle and its busts

Metals history is one long lesson in capital cycles, Chapter 23's principle at maximum volume. Demand grows; prices rise; every producer's spreadsheet says build; the plants arrive together years later; the glut halves the price; the leveraged builders break; a decade passes before anyone builds again. The 2003-2011 China supercycle and its long hangover wrote the modern version, and the energy transition is writing the next, this time for copper, aluminium and lithium rather than bulk steel alone. The investor's calendar is contrarian by construction: the time to study a metals company is when its price chart is a flat line of despair and its capex announcements have stopped; the time to distrust it is when the chairman announces a record expansion and the business pages applaud.

25.6Worked example: an Indian steel maker across one up-cycle and one down

Case: the same company, genius and fool, thirty months apart

Composite of the integrated Indian steelmakers, numbers illustrative. The chart tracks the spread logic: steel prices climb 70% over eighteen months, and because costs move far less, the company's margin index climbs 130%, earnings triple, and the stock re-rates as if the new plateau is permanent. Then China slows, the price gives back half its rise, and the same operating leverage runs in reverse: margins collapse 70%, the leveraged expansion announced at the top now weighs on the balance sheet, and the stock falls by two-thirds from its peak.

Nothing about the company's management changed between genius and fool; the price of its product did. The disciplines that survive this: value the company on mid-cycle margins, never peak ones (Chapter 16's cyclically-adjusted lesson); prefer the integrated, low-debt producer so the down-cycle is a buying opportunity rather than a solvency scare; and treat peak earnings multiplied by a low P/E as the most expensive sentence in investing. The stock was never cheap at the top. It was cheap at the bottom, when the P/E looked infinite.
