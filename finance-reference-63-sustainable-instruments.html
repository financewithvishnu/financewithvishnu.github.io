Chapter 63. New and Sustainable Instruments

Chapter 63 of 85 · about 18 minutes

A bond is a promise to pay; a green bond is a promise to pay and to spend the money on something green. That second promise, how it is written, verified and occasionally betrayed, is the subject of this chapter: the new instruments through which finance now tries to price carbon, catastrophe and conscience, and the labels that deserve your trust only after your reading.

63.1Green bonds and sustainability-linked bonds

Two designs dominate. A green bond earmarks proceeds: the money must fund eligible projects, renewable energy, clean transport, water, with frameworks, second-party opinions and post-issue reporting as the verification machinery, and "use of proceeds" as the legal heart. India's sovereign entered the market with its own green framework and five and ten-year issues, and Indian corporates and banks have followed. A sustainability-linked bond flips the design: proceeds are general, but the coupon steps up if the issuer misses a stated target, emissions intensity, renewable share, putting the company's own money behind its pledge. The investor's reading list is therefore different for each. In a green bond, interrogate the earmark: which projects, verified by whom, reported how often. In an SLB, interrogate the target: is the KPI ambitious or already achieved, is the step-up a real penalty or a rounding error, and who audits the metric? The pricing wrinkle, the "greenium", a few basis points of yield foregone for the label, means buyers pay a small, real price for virtue; know that you are paying it, and decide if the earmark is worth the spread.

63.2Social and impact bonds

The family extends from finance into outcomes. Social bonds earmark proceeds for affordable housing, health, livelihoods, the green-bond machinery pointed at people. Development impact bonds go furthest: private investors fund a social programme upfront, and government or donors repay with a return only if measured outcomes arrive, girls' enrolment up, skilling targets met, transferring performance risk to the investor. India hosted some of the world's early education impact bonds. The appeal is accountability: money follows measured results, not intentions. The honest difficulties are measurement itself, outcomes take years and attribution is arguable; scale, since these remain boutique instruments; and the uncomfortable question of which public goods should be subject to investor returns at all. As an investor, read an impact bond as a structured product from Chapter 50: the payoff formula is the entire instrument, and the party measuring the outcome is the counterparty.

63.3Carbon credits and carbon markets

A carbon credit is a transferable certificate for one tonne of CO₂ avoided or removed, and markets exist to price it in two flavours. Compliance markets, the EU's ETS is the giant, cap total emissions and let obligated firms trade allowances: the price is set by the cap's scarcity, and the EU's carbon border adjustment now reaches into Indian exporters' economics, which is why this distant market matters here. Voluntary markets let companies buy offsets toward net-zero claims, and there the verification problem bites hardest: a credit is only as real as its additionality, would the tonne have been avoided anyway, and investigations have repeatedly found forests that were never threatened and cookstove counts that never cooked. India is building its own carbon market framework, and its firms are large credit suppliers. For investors: credits are a commodity with policy as both supply and demand, project developers carry additionality risk, and any fund selling "carbon exposure" is selling one of the least standardised assets in finance. Verify the verifier, then verify the tonne.

63.4Catastrophe bonds and insurance-linked securities

A catastrophe bond is reinsurance securitised: investors buy a bond paying a fat coupon, and if the named disaster, a cyclone of given strength, an earthquake of given magnitude, strikes within the term, the principal transfers to the sponsor to pay claims. The investor is the insurer of last resort, and the coupon is Chapter 47's premium again, earned for carrying a tail. The investment case is genuinely interesting: cat risk correlates with almost nothing in a financial portfolio, hurricanes do not read the Sensex, so a small allocation can diversify at the portfolio level even while being terrifying at the instrument level. The disciplines: the trigger must be objective, parametric (wind speed, magnitude) or indemnity-based, each with its own basis risk between the disaster and the payout; climate change is repricing the tails the models were fitted to; and the coupon must be read against modelled expected loss, the same premium-versus-odds arithmetic as any insurance. India's cyclone and quake exposure makes it a natural future sponsor; the World Bank has already issued cat bonds covering Indian events.

63.5ESG funds: labels, standards and greenwashing

ESG investing wraps three unlike ideas, environmental, social, governance, into one score, and the wrapper is where the trouble lives. The rating agencies disagree with each other about the same company far more than credit raters ever do, because each measures a different definition of good. The funds range from genuine exclusions and engagement to a Nifty portfolio minus one coal stock, relabelled and repriced. And the incentives run toward the label: the fund earns a fee premium, the company earns a valuation premium, and both are paid before any outcome is measured. Greenwashing enforcement has begun globally, with fines for overstated sustainability claims. The Indian investor's protocol is empirical. Read the actual holdings: an ESG fund is its portfolio, not its name. Compare the expense ratio and overlap against the plain index it shadows. Ask what the strategy does beyond exclusion, engagement, voting, thresholds, and whether any of it is disclosed. And hold the honest possibility that the best ESG outcome for many portfolios is governance first, the G being the letter with the longest evidence of protecting minority investors, with the rest bought directly, through where you spend and vote, rather than through a fee.

63.6Worked examples and cases

Case 1: an Indian sovereign green bond issue

When the Government of India issued its first sovereign green bonds, the structure followed the global template with local texture: a published green finance framework listing eligible categories, renewables, energy efficiency, clean transport, water; proceeds tracked and reported annually; an external review of the framework. The bonds priced at a small greenium, a few basis points below the equivalent plain G-sec, meaning investors paid a modest premium for the earmark, and the government saved a modest amount for being green. Read the transaction three ways. As a funding tool, it works: the money demonstrably flows to the eligible pool, and India's solar buildout is the kind of asset it buys. As an investment, it is a G-sec with a conscience and a slightly thinner yield, Chapter 43's instrument with a coloured wrapper. As a signal, it matters most: a sovereign putting its borrowing behind a framework creates the reference curve, the reporting habits and the verifier ecosystem that corporate green issuance then inherits. The greenium's smallness is the healthiest sign in the whole structure: the market prices the earmark as worth something, and not very much, which is roughly what a promise about spending should be worth.

Case 2: testing an ESG fund's holdings against its label

The test takes twenty minutes and a factsheet. Take a fund marketed on sustainability. Pull its top twenty holdings and its sector weights, and set them beside the Nifty's. In this illustrative exercise, the overlap is 85% by weight: the same banks, the same IT majors, the same conglomerate, minus a coal name and plus a renewables midcap. The tracking difference against the index is a fraction of a percent; the expense ratio is three times the plain index fund's; the "ESG integration" section of the document describes a screen and a quarterly letter. Verdict: the label is doing the marketing, the portfolio is doing the index, and the fee is doing the compounding against you. This is not an argument against values-aligned investing; it is the method for doing it honestly. If your goal is impact, measure the mechanism: engagement reports with votes cast, exclusion thresholds in writing, outcomes reported against them. If your goal is return, remember Chapter 40's hierarchy, costs and behaviour first, and let the values enter through funds whose differences you can actually see in the holdings, priced at a fee the difference justifies. The twenty-minute test, applied before the SIP starts, is worth more than any label on the tin.
