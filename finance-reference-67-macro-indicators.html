Chapter 67. Macro Indicators Every Investor Must Read

Chapter 67 of 85 · about 20 minutes

You do not need to forecast the economy; you need to read its instruments. A dozen numbers, released on a calendar known in advance, move every market you own, and the investor who can read them is not smarter, merely less surprised. Part XII opens with the dashboard: what each gauge measures, what it misses, and how the readings connect into a story about your portfolio.

67.1What are the big three macro indicators: GDP, inflation and employment?

GDP is the economy's turnover: the value of final production, reported quarterly in India, in real (inflation-stripped) and nominal terms. Read it for the trend and the composition, consumption, investment, government spending, net exports, because a 7% print driven by government capex is a different animal from 7% driven by private investment. Its flaws: it is backward-looking, revised for years, and blind to distribution and the informal economy it must estimate. Inflation, CPI for the consumer, WPI for the producer pipeline, is the number with a legal target: the RBI's mandate is 4% within a 2-6% band, which makes every monthly print a vote on interest rates. Read core inflation, excluding food and fuel, for the underlying pressure the RBI can actually influence. Employment in India arrives fuzzily, periodic surveys rather than one monthly payroll number, so investors triangulate: the periodic labour force surveys, EPFO payroll additions, hiring indices, and the wage data inside company results. The big three define the cycle's stage; everything else refines it.

67.2What are the fiscal deficit and current account, and how are they linked?

Two deficits frame India's macro story. The fiscal deficit is the government's annual overspend, funded by borrowing: it matters because every rupee the government borrows competes with private borrowers, and because the bond market prices the supply of G-secs you met in Chapter 43. The current account is the country's external ledger, exports minus imports plus remittances and income: a deficit means India spends more abroad than it earns and must attract the difference as capital inflows. The twin-deficit link is the chain to remember: a government running large deficits can overheat demand, sucking in imports and widening the current account, until the country leans on foreign capital to fund both, which is precisely the configuration that made India a "fragile five" member in 2013. Neither deficit is automatically bad, borrowing to build infrastructure is investment, borrowing to fund consumption is a habit, but the pair together, large and persistent, is the setup for currency and rate crises, and the reason the budget's deficit number moves bond yields the day it is read.

67.3What do PMI, industrial production and high-frequency data tell you?

Between the big quarterly numbers runs a faster ticker. The PMI, purchasing managers' index, is a monthly survey scored around 50: above means expansion, below means contraction, and its speed makes it the market's favourite early gauge of manufacturing and services momentum, a survey of what managers are doing now, not what the economy did last quarter. Industrial production (the IIP) measures factory output monthly, with capital goods and consumer durables as the sub-readings that telegraph investment and demand. And India has assembled a uniquely modern set of high-frequency gauges: GST collections as a monthly read on formal turnover, UPI volumes on consumption, power demand, freight and toll traffic on activity, e-way bills on goods movement. None is the economy; together they form a nowcast that often sees the turn before GDP admits it. The discipline: high-frequency data is noisy and seasonal, so read three-month trends, never one print, and let it change your mind only when the gauges agree.

67.4How do you read the Union Budget and the Economic Survey?

Two documents anchor the Indian macro year, and both are more readable than their coverage suggests. The Economic Survey, tabled the day before the budget, is the honest one: the government's own diagnosis, state of growth, inflation, jobs, external balance, often with a genuinely interesting thematic essay, and it costs you an evening a year. The budget itself: skip the speech's poetry and read four numbers. The fiscal deficit target and the path behind it, which the bond market prices within minutes. The capex allocation, infrastructure spending being the current era's growth engine, and its growth rate versus nominal GDP. The tax changes that touch you directly, slabs, capital gains, exemptions, which the middle-class coverage amplifies and distorts in equal measure. And the borrowings line, gross and net, because that is the supply your G-sec holdings must absorb. The ritual's real lesson: markets price the budget in an afternoon, but the budget prices the country's priorities for a year, and the investor who reads the document reads both.

67.5Which data calendar moves markets: the Fed, the RBI and the releases?

Two central banks set the temperature of an Indian portfolio. The RBI, six monetary policy meetings a year, sets the repo rate that anchors every loan, deposit and bond in the country. The Federal Reserve sets the world's risk-free rate: its hiking and easing cycles reprice the dollar, global bond yields, and the flows into and out of every emerging market, India included, so Fed days are Indian market days whether or not the RBI meets. Around them runs a calendar worth keeping: US CPI and payrolls, Indian CPI and WPI, the PMI first days of the month, GDP quarters, Fed and RBI decisions, and the budget on February 1. The reading habit that pays: for each event, know the expectation before the print, because markets move on the surprise, the gap between priced-in and actual, not on the number. A 5% inflation print that was feared at 5.5% is a rally; the same print hoped at 4.5% is a rout. The data calendar is a schedule of expectations waiting to be violated, which is why professionals watch the consensus as closely as the data.

67.6Worked examples and cases

Case 1: a rate-hike cycle read through inflation prints

The chart tells a generic tightening cycle the way an investor should read it, illustrative numbers shaped on recent Indian history. Inflation runs above the 6% tolerance ceiling for months while the central bank waits, judging it transitory; the prints keep arriving hot; then the repo line starts climbing, 40, 50 basis points at a time, chasing the red line. Notice the lags that govern everything: policy follows the data by months, and the economy follows policy by quarters, so the repo peaks after inflation has already rolled over, and the first cut arrives long after the last hike's pain. The portfolio translations are this website's earlier chapters wearing dates: the rising-rate window crushes long-duration bonds and the rate-sensitive growth stocks, lifts deposit and liquid-fund rates, and pressures the leveraged; the peak, knowable only in hindsight, is when duration becomes the buy. The habit to build: plot the two lines yourself each month, because the gap between them, the real rate, is the single most informative macro number for your asset allocation.

Case 2: India's 2013 taper tantrum

May 2013: the Fed's chairman merely hints that bond-buying might someday slow, and global capital reprices emerging markets overnight. India arrives at the shock with the twin deficits of Section 67.2 wide open, inflation near double digits, and growth slowing: the market's verdict is swift. The rupee slides from the mid-50s to a record near 69 to the dollar in three months, bond yields spike, and India is grouped into the "fragile five". The response is the playbook worth remembering: the RBI defends the currency with rates, liquidity measures and a subsidised deposit scheme that pulls in over $30 billion of NRI money, a new governor talks the panic down, and the government commits to deficit compression; by the following year the same India is a market darling. The lessons are structural. External dependence is the vulnerability: the countries that suffered were those borrowing the world's money, and India entered later Fed cycles with fatter reserves, a smaller current account gap, and correspondingly calmer markets. And tantrums are tests of the plumbing, not the prophecy: every panic in this website ends with the survivors being those whose leverage was low and whose liabilities were in their own currency.
