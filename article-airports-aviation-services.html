Airports and aviation services: follow the passenger, the concession and the cash

By Finance With Vishnu · Source period: approximately August 2025 · Exact class date unconfirmed

An airport looks like one business from the departure gate. It is actually several businesses sharing the same fence. One sells a regulated service to airlines and passengers. Another rents space to shops and restaurants. A third may load bags, clean cabins or store cargo. Each earns money in a different way and carries a different risk.

The same flight can be a poor deal for the airline and a satisfactory one for the airport. The airline worries about fuel, fares and filled seats. The airport charges for the runway and terminal whether or not the seat was sold at a profit. Studying everything under one aviation label hides that difference.

1. What kind of aviation business is this?

Start by separating the players. An airport operator develops and runs the airport. A concessionaire runs a shop, food outlet, car park or duty-free store inside it. A service provider handles passengers, bags, ramps, cabins or cargo for the airlines. The airline sells seats and cargo space. A lessor owns aircraft and rents them out. All five appear in the same airport, but they sign different contracts and carry different risks.

A large airport group can own several of these activities at once. That makes the first analytical question simple: which contract produces this rupee of revenue, and what could interrupt it?

2. How does a passenger become airport revenue?

A passenger creates revenue at two taps. The first is aeronautical: charges tied to the aircraft and the traveller. Delhi International Airport's FY2024-25 report lists its aeronautical sources as landing fees, parking and housing fees, user development fees, baggage X-ray charges and fuel-farm charges.[2] Some are billed to the airline and some to the passenger; all are tied to flights and traffic.

The second tap is commercial, usually called non-aeronautical: shops, food and beverage, parking, advertising and property development. The same Delhi report records non-aeronautical revenue of Rs 33,012.6 million for FY2024-25.[2] A useful bridge is passengers times aeronautical yield per passenger, plus passengers times commercial income per passenger. Each side has its own driver: flights and regulation on one, spending on the other.

3. Why is aeronautical revenue regulated?

A city usually has one main airport, and an airline cannot easily move its passengers to another building across town. That gives the airport pricing power over an essential service. In India, the Airports Economic Regulatory Authority of India determines tariffs for the major airports under the AERA Act.

Regulation converts an unconstrained monopoly price into an allowed revenue. The concession agreement then decides how that income is shared with the government-side authority that granted the right to operate. I would read both together: the tariff orders describe what the airport may charge, and the concession describes what it may keep.

4. How does the regulator build a tariff?

The broad machinery is a building-block model. The regulator estimates the regulated asset base, allows a fair return on it through a weighted average cost of capital, adds depreciation, operating costs and tax, and subtracts the offsetting revenues required by the chosen tariff design. The result is an aggregate revenue requirement, divided by forecast traffic to set charges for a multi-year control period.

A real example is AERA's Consultation Paper No. 07/2024-25, dated 31 January 2025, for Delhi airport's fourth control period.[5] It works through true-ups for earlier control periods, the asset base, the cost of capital, depreciation, tax and operating costs, and invites comments before a final order. I cite it as a dated proposal that shows the process, never as a current tariff.

5. Why does the choice of till change incentives?

The till decides how much commercial income must subsidise aeronautical charges. Under a single till, all revenues together fund the allowed return, so charges stay low but the operator keeps little of the commercial upside. Under a dual till, aeronautical assets must pay for themselves and the operator keeps the commercial income. A hybrid till shares that income between lower charges and operator upside.

Hyderabad shows the hybrid design. Crisil's April 2025 rationale describes GMR Hyderabad International Airport as earning fixed returns on its regulated asset base plus upside on 70% of non-aeronautical revenue, with about 68% of FY2024 revenue from regulated aero charges.[8] Aeronautical revenue is trued up when traffic, capital spending, operating cost or tax differs from plan, which lends stability to the revenue base. The design decides whether management works harder on flights or on shops.

6. Why does the aero and non-aero label matter?

Because the label decides who benefits from a rupee of income. If cargo or ground-handling income is aeronautical, it can reduce the charges airlines pay. If it is non-aeronautical, the operator may keep more of it. Hundreds of crores can turn on a classification.

This is not theoretical. GMR Hyderabad International Airport's FY2024-25 report describes its challenge to the regulator's third-control-period order: the tribunal TDSAT held on 2 February 2024 that cargo, ground-handling and fuelling revenues are non-aeronautical, that land meant for commercial real-estate development lies beyond the regulator's jurisdiction, and that dividend and interest income is not non-aeronautical income. The regulator appealed to the Supreme Court on 11 May 2024, and the matter remained undecided as of that report and of Crisil's April 2025 rationale.[3][8] Treat the classification of every large revenue line as an open question until the legal process ends, and never state a disputed outcome as settled.

7. Is more traffic the same as more revenue?

No. Traffic counts passengers; yield measures what each passenger is worth. GMR Airports' FY2024-25 report records traffic growth of 8% at Delhi to 79.3 million passengers, 18% at Hyderabad to 29.5 million and 7% at Goa to 4.7 million.[1] Revenue per passenger depends on the aeronautical tariff, the international and domestic mix, and how much travellers actually spend.

The example shows why contract shape matters as much as passenger count. A minimum guarantee shifts spending risk to the concessionaire; a pure revenue share leaves it with the airport. When commercial income rises, ask how much came from more passengers, how much from higher spending per passenger, and how much from a change in contract terms.

8. What does the concession agreement decide?

Almost everything that matters: duration of the operating right, the revenue share paid to the granting authority, exclusivity protection and capacity obligations. Hyderabad's concession runs thirty years to 2038 and was extended in May 2022 to 2068; the Airports Authority of India and the Government of Telangana each hold 13%, with the GMR group at 74% through GMR Airports. The concession also bars a new airport within an aerial distance of 150 kilometres before the twenty-fifth year of operations, 2033.[8]

The revenue-share line is large enough to see in consolidated accounts. GMR Airports reported FY2024-25 revenue from continuing operations of Rs 10,414.24 crore, falling to Rs 7,779.46 crore after deducting the revenue share paid or payable to granting authorities.[1] Delhi's operator separately reports an annual fee to the Airports Authority of India.[2] The gap between gross and net revenue is the price of the right to run the airport.

9. Why do ownership and perimeter changes distort growth?

Revenue can rise because the business grew, or because the boundary of the business moved. During FY2024-25, GMR Airports raised its shareholding in Delhi International Airport to 74% by acquiring the 10% earlier held by Fraport for USD 126 million.[1] A changed stake alters what is consolidated and what appears as a share of joint-venture profit, so year-on-year comparisons need a perimeter check first.

The same caution applies when an operator brings an activity in-house. GMR's report records that it secured duty-free concession rights at Delhi and Hyderabad, with Delhi operations commencing on 28 July 2025, after the reporting year.[1] Running a store directly records sales and costs, where the earlier arrangement recorded only concession income; reported revenue jumps without any passenger spending an extra rupee. Concessions can also be reshaped or ended: Delhi's report describes the Celebi cargo concession terminated on a government directive and replaced by an interim concession to GMR Airports Limited until a new concessionaire is appointed, shortening a tenure that earlier ran to 31 March 2034.[2] Before celebrating growth, ask whether the fence moved.

10. Why does an airport keep needing capital?

An airport that works well attracts more passengers than it was built for. Hyderabad expanded capacity from 12 million to 34 million passengers in April 2024 and plans a further expansion to 54 million, expected during FY2028 to FY2032 at a 70:30 debt-to-equity ratio.[8] Regulated capital spending is recoverable through future tariffs, but only after approval, so timely sign-off is a real business risk.

Crisil's rationale puts Hyderabad's gross debt at 6.2 times EBITDA in March 2025, down from 7.5 times a year earlier. It also records about Rs 669 crore of aeronautical revenue deferred within the third control period by holding back tariff increases, with recovery expected in the fourth control period along with carrying costs, and a regulator-ordered tariff reduction in the fourth quarter of FY2026.[8] Such levers decide when invested capital actually comes back.

11. What do aviation service contracts look like?

Around the airport sits a layer of contracted service businesses. AI Airport Services Limited's FY2024-25 report describes comprehensive ground handling: passenger, ramp, baggage, cargo and cabin services plus station management, and cargo warehousing, delivered to the Air India group, Alliance Air, other domestic and foreign airlines, cargo charter operators and non-scheduled operators.[4] Revenue arrives per flight, per passenger or per tonne, and the work depends on airline customers staying solvent and schedules staying intact.

Two features deserve attention. First, permission and renewal: the same report records Bureau of Civil Aviation Security approval to function as a regulated agent at a Chennai cargo terminal, a reminder that this work needs security clearances, not just commercial contracts.[4] Second, counterparty and concession risk: the Delhi cargo episode shows a concession terminated on a government directive and re-awarded on an interim basis.[2] A service contract can be profitable and still disappear at renewal, retender or directive.

12. How is an airport different from an airline?

An airline is a volatile operating business bolted to an expensive machine. Crisil's July 2025 rationale for InterGlobe Aviation, the operator of IndiGo, records FY2024-25 revenue of Rs 80,803 crore, up 17%, with an EBITDAR margin of 26.3%.[7] The fragility underneath: aviation turbine fuel is 35 to 40% of industry operating cost, lease rentals and maintenance at 30 to 35% of operating cost are denominated in US dollars, and aircraft groundings from an engine powder-metal issue forced damp-leased aircraft into the fleet to protect capacity.

The rating treatment also differs. Crisil counts aircraft lease liabilities as debt because the leases cover core assets with unconditional, long-tenure payment obligations, and it separates the airline's Rs 33,153 crore of free cash from Rs 15,017 crore of restricted cash placed with lessors and maintenance providers as of 31 March 2025.[7] Compare that with the airport: a regulated revenue requirement, a multi-decade concession, catchment exclusivity and capital-heavy but recoverable expansion. Both live off the same passenger, yet one is a fare-and-fuel business while the other is closer to a regulated utility with a shopping mall attached. Their margins and failure modes should never be compared directly.

13. What should the tracking sheet explain?

My tracking sheet would answer, with dates: How many passengers and movements did the airport handle, using the Airports Authority of India's monthly and yearly traffic reports as the reference series?[6] What are the aeronautical and commercial yields per passenger, and which tariff order and contracts produce them? What does the concession require and protect: duration, revenue share, exclusivity, capacity obligations? What sits in the regulated asset base, what return is allowed, and what true-ups and classification disputes are pending? What capital spending is approved, under way and planned, and how is it funded? For service companies, which contracts renew when, which clearances are required, and how concentrated are the customers?

None of these questions needs a forecast. They ask whether the business is understood well enough that a surprise in traffic, regulation or contracts would be noticed quickly and read correctly. That understanding, recorded line by line, is the real output of the study.
