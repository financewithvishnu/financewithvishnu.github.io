Alcohol businesses: when premiumisation becomes profitable growth

By Finance With Vishnu · Source period: September 2025

A company can sell more expensive products without becoming a better business. Higher prices may come with higher ingredient costs, packaging costs, brand spending and capital tied up in inventory. The useful question is how much additional cash the company earns after all of that.

I would study an alcohol business by separating volume, realisation, product mix, distribution and capital employed. This makes the premiumisation story testable rather than treating the launch of every expensive bottle as a success.

There is another distinction to make at the outset. Studying the economics of alcohol is not an endorsement of drinking. The World Health Organization describes alcohol as a toxic, psychoactive substance with dependence-producing properties and associated health and social harms. These risks belong in the sector analysis. [6]

This is a study of business models, not current stock prices, acquisition completion, import-duty schedules, bottle prices or state-law guidance. All worked examples are hypothetical and use consistent net-revenue assumptions.

1. Which part of the value chain earns the profit?

A branded spirits company, a bulk-alcohol producer and a contract bottler can all participate in the same supply chain. Their source of value is different.

A branded company needs products that customers choose repeatedly and a route to market that makes those products available. A bulk producer depends more directly on manufacturing economics, input costs and the price of its output. A contract manufacturer earns under agreements to produce for another business.

A group may operate across several layers. Radico Khaitan's FY2025 report describes distillation, owned bottling and contract or royalty bottling arrangements. That is a reminder to distinguish ownership of a brand from ownership of every manufacturing asset. [1]

I would identify which activity drives revenue and which absorbs capital before comparing the company with a peer. Similar-looking turnover can represent very different economics.

2. What does premiumisation actually mean?

Premiumisation is a shift toward higher-value products within the relevant comparison. It can increase average realisation when a greater share of sales comes from more expensive products.

It is not the same as increasing the price of the same product. Price increases, changes in pack size, geographic mix and a shift between brands can all affect average realisation. Their effects need separating.

Companies also use different portfolio classifications. A category called Prestige & Above is a company reporting boundary that should be read with its definitions. It is not automatically a universal bottle-price band across every state and company.

United Spirits' FY2025 results separate Prestige & Above from Popular and distinguish reported from underlying net sales value. Those distinctions are useful inputs to analysis, not labels to combine without checking. [3]

I would ask whether customers are choosing the higher-value portfolio repeatedly, whether it is growing in absolute terms and whether its economics remain attractive after support spending. A higher percentage mix alone cannot answer those questions.

3. How do volume and realisation combine?

On consistent definitions, net revenue equals volume multiplied by average net realisation. Growth can come from either component, but the combination is multiplicative.

Suppose a hypothetical business sells one million equivalent cases at ₹1,000 net revenue per case. It earns ₹100 crore of net revenue. If volume rises 10% and net realisation rises 8%, revenue becomes ₹118.8 crore, an increase of 18.8%.

The result is not exactly 18%, because the higher realisation also applies to the additional volume. Equally, a decline in volume can offset a rising realisation.

Case definitions and pack sizes must match. Comparing an inconsistent physical-unit measure with a value measure can create a false premiumisation signal.

I would also distinguish company shipments from consumer demand where the evidence allows. More stock entering the channel does not by itself establish an equivalent increase in consumption or repeat purchase.

4. Can a better mix hide a weak business?

Yes. A premium category can become a larger share of sales because it grows, because the lower-value category shrinks, or because both happen.

Imagine premium revenue of ₹40 crore and other revenue of ₹60 crore. Premium is 40% of total revenue. If premium stays at ₹40 crore while the other category falls to ₹40 crore, premium becomes 50% even though total revenue declines 20%.

The mix improved, but the premium business did not grow. This is why I would keep absolute category revenue and volume beside mix percentages.

Allied Blenders and Distillers' FY2025 report discusses portfolio mix, manufacturing investment and a move into higher-value categories. These are separate initiatives that should be assessed independently rather than merged into one guaranteed margin outcome. [2]

A healthier pattern is one where the desired portfolio grows, the company retains the relevant customers and margins improve without excessive capital or working-capital strain. That pattern needs several reporting periods, not one favourable percentage.

5. Does the shelf price belong to the company?

No. A customer's payment at retail is not the same as the producer's net revenue. Taxes, duties and the commercial structure between producer and retailer affect the amount retained by each participant.

The Central GST Act's section 9 excludes the supply of alcoholic liquor for human consumption from the levy described there. That does not make the product tax-free. It is one reason not to model the sector as an ordinary uniform-GST consumer category. [5]

For company analysis, identify whether the reported revenue includes excise duty and whether a margin is calculated on gross revenue, net revenue or another measure. An apparently low margin can be partly a denominator issue.

I would not use a single national tax percentage or a bottle-price ladder without specifying the jurisdiction, product and date. State rules and commercial arrangements matter, and an old illustration is not a current price list.

Tilaknagar Industries' FY2025 report describes a price reduction in Andhra Pradesh affecting net sales realisation per case. It is a historical example of why volume growth need not translate into the same rate of value growth. [4]

6. How much does a premium product really add?

The relevant measure is the additional revenue less the additional costs required to earn it. Expensive packaging and higher brand spending can consume part of the apparent advantage.

Consider two hypothetical products, measured per equivalent case on the same net-revenue basis. Product A earns ₹1,000 and has ₹650 of production, packaging and delivery costs, leaving ₹350 before brand support and fixed costs. Product B earns ₹1,500 but has ₹900 of those costs, leaving ₹600.

Product B creates ₹250 more contribution, not the entire ₹500 revenue difference. If launching it requires another ₹180 per case of relevant support costs during that period, the incremental amount falls to ₹70 before further overhead or capital costs.

This example does not imply that spending is wasteful. It shows why the time required to build repeat demand matters. A successful product can improve economics as it scales, while a failed launch leaves spending without a durable revenue stream.

United Spirits' FY2025 disclosure discusses gross-margin drivers, ENA inflation and advertising and promotion reinvestment. Reading these together gives a fuller picture than looking at sales growth alone. [3]

7. Is backward integration automatically a margin improvement?

Backward integration means bringing an upstream activity inside the business. It can improve supply reliability and reduce purchased-input exposure. It also adds capital, operating responsibility and utilisation risk.

Extra neutral alcohol, or ENA, is a key input discussed in spirits-company disclosures. Owning an ENA facility does not make grain, energy, water, maintenance and labour free. The company has changed how it obtains the input, not removed its economic cost.

Radico's FY2025 report discusses its integrated manufacturing platform. Allied Blenders and Distillers' FY2025 report sets out ENA and packaging investments with expected capacity and timing. Historical project plans should not be silently treated as completed capacity today. [1, 2]

I would compare the avoided external cost with the full internal cost and the capital employed. Also test what happens if demand is lower than planned or purchased-input prices fall.

A project that improves EBITDA can still disappoint on returns if it costs too much, takes too long or operates below the expected utilisation. Supply control and shareholder value are related, but they are not identical.

8. Why can a distribution network be valuable?

A product must be available through the appropriate route to market before brand demand becomes revenue. Manufacturing capacity alone cannot establish that access.

Radico's FY2025 report describes a broad bottling and distribution footprint alongside its brand portfolio. The usefulness of such a network lies in its ability to support product availability and execution, rather than in the number of locations by itself. [1]

A new product may benefit from existing relationships and infrastructure. It may also need registration, channel support, working capital and evidence that it fits local demand. Geographic expansion is not costless.

I would separate an initial launch from repeat sales. Track how the product performs after early stocking and promotion, and whether receivables and inventory grow faster than the business.

A large distribution network is a potential advantage, not proof that every new brand will work. The quality of execution and the economics of the product still decide the outcome.

9. What is different about matured spirits?

Some products require spirit to mature before sale. Radico's FY2025 report describes cask maturation and finishing for selected products. That adds a time dimension to production and inventory planning. [1]

Money can be committed to stock before it is ready to sell. Growth may therefore require funding production today for sales in a later period. Demand forecasts and the age profile of available stock become important.

I would not assume that every spirit has the same production cycle, or that every litre of distillation capacity is immediately equivalent to saleable finished inventory. The product specification and the stage of the stock matter.

A useful research file separates capacity, production, maturing stock, finished goods and sales. It also examines storage, quality, insurance, losses and the accounting policy where disclosed.

Scarcity can support a brand story, but it can also constrain volume. The investor needs to know whether limited supply reflects deliberate positioning, a production bottleneck or insufficient inventory planning.

10. Why does cash conversion deserve its own section?

Profit is not cash collection. Inventory, customer credit, taxes, supplier payments and capital spending influence how much cash remains available.

In an original simplified example, a business reports ₹100 crore of operating profit before depreciation and financing. Receivables increase ₹25 crore and inventory increases ₹20 crore, while payables increase ₹10 crore. Those working-capital movements absorb ₹35 crore before other cash-flow adjustments.

The company may still be growing well. The point is that somebody must fund the growth. More sales do not automatically fund themselves.

I would be more cautious when reported growth repeatedly arrives with weaker collection and rising borrowing. A one-off movement needs explanation; a repeated pattern needs a change in the thesis.

11. Can an acquisition increase sales but reduce earnings quality?

Yes. Buying a brand or business can add revenue quickly while introducing financing costs, integration work, new working capital and a different margin profile.

The price paid matters as much as the acquired sales. So do the rights transferred, manufacturing arrangements, distribution access and any obligations that stay with the buyer.

A simplified example makes the distinction clear. Adding ₹200 crore of revenue at a 10% operating margin adds ₹20 crore of operating earnings before purchase-related effects. If the associated annual financing and integration costs are ₹18 crore, the near-term earnings contribution is much smaller than the revenue addition suggests.

Equity financing changes the share count, while debt financing changes risk and interest obligations. Neither is free. Compare returns and earnings per share after the transaction, not only the size of the combined company.

I would verify announcement, approval, completion and integration as separate stages. No historical proposed acquisition or funding estimate in this study is presented as a current completed event.

12. Which risks can a premiumisation story overlook?

Regulation, input costs, concentration, funding and valuation can all challenge the story. Radico's FY2025 risk discussion addresses commodity exposure and the complexity of the regulated operating environment. [1]

A company concentrated in one brand, category or region has fewer alternatives if demand or rules change there. A broad portfolio can help, but maintaining it also requires investment.

Imported inputs introduce questions about currency, duties and supply arrangements. A trade-agreement headline is not enough to calculate savings: effective dates, eligible goods and the company's actual exposure must be checked. I would not hard-code an old tariff assumption into a current forecast.

Health and social harms are part of the sector's long-term context. They can influence consumer choices, policy and restrictions. The WHO source is included for that context, not as a forecast of any company's volume. [6]

Finally, a sound business can be an unattractive investment at an excessive price. Premiumisation does not remove the need to test downside earnings, cash requirements and the return implied by the valuation.

13. What would make the thesis stronger or weaker?

I would gain confidence from repeat demand, consistent definitions, improved cash conversion and investments that earn their expected returns. I would be more cautious when mix improves only because the rest of the portfolio shrinks, or when growth continually requires more debt.

The central question is not whether the company sells an expensive bottle. It is whether the company can turn a product customers choose into durable earnings and cash, within the industry's commercial, regulatory and social constraints.
