Pre-engineered buildings: the business behind the factory shed

By Finance With Vishnu · FY25 study edition

A building is not always a property investment. Sometimes it is the tool that allows somebody else's factory to start earning. That is the useful starting point for understanding pre-engineered buildings.

A pre-engineered building, or PEB, is a steel building whose structural system is designed for a particular project, fabricated largely in a factory, transported to the location and assembled on site. The product is engineered in advance. It is not necessarily a standard, identical shed pulled from a catalogue.

The investing question is more interesting than whether India will build more factories. It is whether a supplier can turn that demand into well-priced orders, deliver those orders without mistakes, collect its money and earn a good return on the capital used. A long runway does not protect a contractor from a bad contract.

Study window: FY24 and FY25 results, with selected industry estimates published in Interarch's FY25 annual report. Forecasts retain their original dates. This is a historical business-model study, not an update on November 2026 trading conditions or a stock recommendation.

1. What exactly does a PEB company sell?

A PEB company sells an integrated engineering and construction solution for the steel-building scope agreed with the customer. Its responsibility can include structural design, fabrication, supply and supervision or execution of erection. The exact contract matters: land, foundations, utilities, fire systems and internal fit-outs are not automatically included just because the building is called turnkey.

Think of a manufacturer setting up an air-conditioner factory. The manufacturer understands its production line, movement of materials, power needs and dispatch schedule. It may not want to coordinate a separate structural designer, several steel vendors, a fabricator and an erection team. An integrated PEB supplier brings much of that coordination into one contract.

The work starts with questions rather than steel. How wide must the unobstructed floor be? Will cranes move overhead? How high must the building be? What loads must the roof support? What wind, seismic, temperature and corrosion conditions apply? Where might the customer expand? A cheap structure that fails those requirements is not a cheaper version of the same product.

The supplier then converts the agreed design into drawings and bills of material, procures steel, cuts and welds built-up sections, makes secondary members, applies the specified protection and sends a planned sequence of components to the site. Columns and rafters form the primary frame; purlins and girts support the roof and walls; bracing stabilises the structure; cladding and roofing enclose it. Connections and installation quality link the design on paper to the building that actually stands.

A useful distinction follows. A steel mill sells material. A PEB supplier uses material, design and execution to sell a functioning structure. Steel prices influence its reported revenue, but engineering quality and contract discipline determine how much of that revenue becomes profit.

2. Why might a customer choose PEB instead of conventional construction?

PEB can shorten the path from design approval to an operating building because factory fabrication can overlap with work on the site. Time is an economic input, not merely a construction statistic.

In a largely sequential project, the next major activity waits for the preceding one. In a PEB project, parts of fabrication can proceed while foundations and site preparation are being completed, subject to design freeze and coordination. This does not remove permissions, weather, foundations or installation risk. It changes how much work can happen in parallel.

Consider a hypothetical customer for whom an earlier factory opening creates Rs 30 lakh of monthly operating contribution. A three-month earlier opening would be worth Rs 90 lakh before financing, commissioning and other differences. The customer should compare that benefit with the full delivered cost, not simply the quoted price per tonne of steel. This arithmetic is illustrative, not a measured saving for a named project.

Large open spans are another attraction. A warehouse, manufacturing hall or aircraft hangar may value usable floor space without frequent internal columns. Factory fabrication can also improve repeatability of manufacturing processes. But the relevant comparison depends on the building's purpose, local conditions, height, loading, fire performance, thermal requirements and life-cycle maintenance.

It would be wrong to conclude that PEB replaces all reinforced cement concrete construction. Foundations may still use concrete. Many buildings use hybrid systems. A tower, a process plant, a cold store and a distribution shed do not have identical requirements. The better question is where engineered steel offers the customer a useful combination of speed, span, adaptability and total cost.

Interarch's annual report discusses industrial facilities, warehouses, data centres, aircraft hangars and railway-related applications. These are examples of demand categories, not proof that every project in those categories will use PEB or that one supplier wins the work. [P1]

3. How large is the opportunity, and what does a market forecast really mean?

An addressable market is the spending pool a supplier might compete for. It is not the supplier's future revenue. Market growth must pass through competitive bidding, available capacity, execution and cash collection before it can help shareholders.

Interarch's FY25 annual report reproduces a CRISIL industry series showing the Indian PEB market at Rs 130 billion in FY19, Rs 110 billion in FY21, Rs 180 billion in FY23 and an estimated Rs 195 billion in FY24. That is Rs 13,000 crore, Rs 11,000 crore, Rs 18,000 crore and Rs 19,500 crore respectively. The same chart projects Rs 330-340 billion, or Rs 33,000-34,000 crore, for FY29. It labels the FY24-FY29 growth range at 11-12%. [P1, p.76]

Two points matter more than the apparent precision. First, the FY21 decline is a reminder that a growth industry can still have a weak year. Second, FY29 is a projection from that source period. It must not be presented as an achieved outcome or a fresh forecast.

A supplier can grow faster than the industry if it gains share, adds capacity, wins more complex work or improves its mix. It can also grow more slowly if it cannot hire engineers, commission a plant or accept orders without damaging delivery standards. A sector forecast is therefore the beginning of a model, not its conclusion.

There are also two different share shifts to study. One is a construction customer choosing PEB over another method. The other is a PEB customer moving from a small fabricator to an organised supplier. Both may help established businesses, but they should not be added together casually: the same project can sit inside both stories.

I would rather see the company demonstrate repeat orders, acceptable margins and cash collection than multiply several optimistic market-share assumptions into a very large revenue forecast.

4. Where is the competitive advantage if other companies can buy steel too?

The competitive advantage is the ability to design, deliver and support the right building reliably, at a cost and price that leave an acceptable return. Access to steel alone does not establish it.

The visible factory is only part of the business. The less visible system includes structural engineers, detailers, estimation teams, procurement, production planning, quality control, transport coordination and erection capability. An error in one part can create delay or rework in another. A factory that produces more tonnes than the site can accept has not solved the customer's problem.

Execution history can help a supplier qualify for larger and more complicated projects. A customer committing expensive machinery to a new plant may place a high value on delivery certainty. The supplier still has to win on price and capability, but the decision need not go to the lowest raw-material markup.

Repeat orders should be interpreted carefully. Repeat business from a large industrial group can suggest trust and access to the next project. It does not guarantee the same margins, eliminate competition or make customer concentration harmless. Ask whether repeat work comes from many independent customers or from several entities belonging to one group.

Geography matters too. Heavy and bulky steel structures must travel. A manufacturing footprint closer to important demand centres may reduce delivery complexity and freight disadvantages. Expansion still has to earn its cost. More plants are not automatically better if existing facilities are underused.

I would avoid giving each supplier a permanent tier label. A supplier's qualification for one complicated project does not make it superior on every building, every location and every commercial term. Compare actual capabilities, project history and financial results instead.

5. How do steel prices distort revenue growth and margins?

Steel pass-through means some movement in input cost is reflected in the contract price. When that happens, revenue can change without a matching change in physical work or operating profit. The extent and timing depend on the contract and procurement policy.

Here is a deliberately simple example. Suppose a job has Rs 80 of steel and other variable material cost, Rs 10 of conversion cost and Rs 10 of operating profit. Billing is Rs 100 and the operating margin is 10%. If a fully recoverable steel-cost increase takes material cost to Rs 100, billing could rise to Rs 120 while the Rs 10 profit remains unchanged. The margin falls to 8.3%, even though absolute profit did not fall.

The reverse can make percentage margins look better when steel becomes cheaper. Neither result tells the whole story about execution quality. Track physical volume, realisations, gross contribution, absolute EBITDA and cash alongside the margin percentage.

Actual contracts are not a perfect pass-through machine. A fixed-price contract signed before steel is procured exposes the supplier to price changes. Back-to-back procurement can reduce that exposure but may create inventory, funding or cancellation risks. Escalation clauses may use formulas, reference prices and time lags. Not all costs move with steel, and customer changes may require a separate agreement.

The analytical mistake is to say that rising steel always helps sales and therefore always helps profit, or that steel risk is zero because management mentions a pass-through mechanism. The right questions are what percentage of the order book is exposed, how quickly procurement is matched and who bears a change in design or delivery schedule.

6. Why can a modest increase in sales produce a larger increase in profit?

Operating leverage is the effect of spreading relatively fixed operating costs over more revenue. It can make profit rise faster than sales, and it works in reverse when activity slows.

Take a hypothetical supplier with a 20% contribution margin after variable costs and Rs 100 crore of annual fixed operating costs. At Rs 800 crore of revenue, contribution is Rs 160 crore and operating profit is Rs 60 crore. At Rs 1,000 crore, profit becomes Rs 100 crore. At Rs 1,200 crore, it becomes Rs 140 crore. A 20% rise from the middle revenue case produces a 40% profit increase; a 20% fall produces a 40% profit decline.

This is a teaching model, not a forecast for Interarch or Pennar. In reality, fixed costs rise in steps, project mix changes and overtime, freight and subcontracting may increase at high utilisation. A model that holds every cost constant forever will overstate the benefit.

A new plant can initially make reported margins worse. Engineers, supervisors, maintenance teams and other staff may be hired before the plant earns meaningful revenue. Depreciation begins when assets are ready for use, while customer approvals and the production ramp may take longer. That gap is real, but it is not a permanent excuse. Compare the promised commissioning date, actual output, orders allocated to the facility and subsequent cash generation.

Capacity should also be defined precisely. Rated tonnes, practical throughput and saleable output are different. A complex project can consume more engineering and fabrication effort than a simpler project of similar weight. Revenue per tonne is influenced by steel prices and project scope. Multiplying nameplate capacity by one peak-period realisation is not a reliable earnings forecast.

7. How should you read the order book and working capital together?

An order book is contracted work not yet recognised as revenue. It provides evidence of future activity, but it is neither cash in the bank nor profit already earned.

Start with a bridge: opening order book plus new orders, less execution and cancellations or adjustments, equals closing order book. Then ask about delivery dates, customer readiness, pricing terms, advances, retention money and the concentration of large projects. A new order helps only if the company can execute it profitably and the customer can pay.

A hypothetical order book of Rs 1,000 crore with Rs 1,200 crore of new orders and Rs 1,100 crore of execution ends at Rs 1,100 crore, assuming no other changes. Intake divided by execution is 1.09 times. That is useful information, but it does not reveal the margin in the new orders. Winning poor work can improve this ratio while reducing future earnings quality.

Working capital connects execution to financing. Inventory, receivables and contract balances can absorb cash; supplier credit and customer advances can offset some of the need. The accounting presentation must be read carefully because advances, retention and contract assets are not interchangeable.

At an illustrative annual revenue of Rs 1,000 crore, 30 days of revenue is about Rs 82.2 crore. A 15-day movement is about Rs 41.1 crore. This is a rough sensitivity using revenue as the denominator, not a full cash-conversion-cycle calculation. Even that simple exercise shows why a few days can matter to a modest-margin business.

An apparently attractive order book combined with stretched receivables, rising inventory and weak operating cash flow deserves more attention than the headline order value. Read at least several periods together. Customer advances can make one period look unusually cash-rich, just as delivery timing can make another look unusually weak.

8. What does Interarch's FY25 result teach us?

Interarch is a useful focused example of how revenue growth, margin improvement and profit growth can differ. Its May 22, 2025 release reports FY25 consolidated revenue of Rs 1,453.8 crore against Rs 1,293.3 crore in FY24, a 12.4% increase. EBITDA excluding other income rose from Rs 113.0 crore to Rs 136.2 crore, or 20.6%. PAT rose from Rs 86.3 crore to Rs 107.8 crore, or 25.0%. [P2]

This pattern is consistent with an improvement in operating profitability, but the percentages alone do not isolate its causes. The release attributes the performance to volume and execution. A full analysis should still separate mix, steel realisations, utilisation, other income, tax and the effects of changes in the share base.

The same release places the order book at Rs 1,646 crore on April 30, 2025. That is a dated snapshot, not a current order book. It also discusses capacity moving from 161,000 tonnes toward approximately 200,000 tonnes, with planned commissioning in Q1 FY26, and a management ambition to double revenue over three to four years. These were plans stated in May 2025, not outcomes established by this article.

The research task is to turn the ambition into testable milestones: was the new capacity commissioned; did output rise; was enough profitable work won; did working capital stay controlled; and did returns remain satisfactory after the additional capital entered service? A good result in the base year is not a substitute for those checks.

9. What can Pennar, Bajaj Steel, Everest and other suppliers teach us?

Peer comparison means comparing similar business scopes, capital requirements and accounting periods. A group with several engineering businesses is not automatically comparable with a more focused PEB company.

Pennar's May 30, 2025 release reports FY25 consolidated sales of Rs 3,226.58 crore and PAT of Rs 119.45 crore. These are group numbers, not pure PEB numbers. The release describes metal buildings alongside tubes, boilers, industrial components and engineering services, and lists both Indian PEB orders and its US Ascent Buildings activity. [P3]

The useful question is whether growth in the desired vertical is large enough to change the economics of the whole group. A fast-growing business can be diluted by weaker divisions. Conversely, exiting a low-return activity may improve capital efficiency even before consolidated revenue accelerates. Segment disclosure, intercompany transactions, working capital and central costs matter more than a simple group price-to-earnings comparison.

Bajaj Steel is another type of research question: can cash from an established engineering business be reinvested in newer activities at attractive incremental returns? Its FY25 annual report describes the infrastructure division's PEB revenue rising from Rs 36 crore in FY22 to Rs 86 crore in FY25, with 35,000 tonnes of capacity and a Rs 66 crore order book at March 31, 2025. These are historical divisional figures, not the group's revenue or a forecast. A PEB opportunity inside a diversified company should be valued with its actual segment disclosures, not as if every rupee of group revenue were PEB. Smaller starting capacity may create a high percentage growth rate without changing group earnings very much. [P5]

Everest is the counterweight to an optimistic demand story. Its March 2025 credit-rating discussion links weaker operating performance to, among other factors, low-margin PEB contracts. The lesson is not that the entire sector is unattractive. It is that revenue won through poor pricing can damage the supplier even when end-demand exists. [P4]

The wider competitive landscape also deserves attention. Private and newly listed suppliers can influence pricing and customer choice even if their shares do not appear in a familiar portfolio tracker. This article does not rank those suppliers or assert a current market-share league table.

10. How would you value a PEB business without getting carried away?

Valuation is the price paid for the cash a business may generate after meeting its operating and reinvestment needs. In a cyclical, project-driven business, peak earnings are a dangerous shortcut to normal earnings.

Build revenue from a defensible execution level, not from the largest possible market share. Test margins at more than one utilisation level. Include maintenance spending, expansion spending, taxes and the cash tied up in growth. Adjust for genuinely surplus cash and debt without counting the same benefit twice.

A business earning a high return on a small asset base may still require meaningful working capital as it grows. A business reporting low returns during construction may improve after commissioning, but that improvement must be demonstrated. Neither a high historical ROCE nor a low current price-to-earnings multiple settles the question.

Use at least three cases. In the cautious case, customer decisions slow, the new plant ramps later and receivables stretch. In the middle case, execution broadly matches capacity and pricing remains sensible. In the strong case, demand, mix and utilisation improve together. Do not make the strong case the base case simply because management has announced a large pipeline.

The most useful valuation output may be the assumptions required to justify the price. If the market price needs uninterrupted growth, peak margins and perfect cash collection at the same time, the margin of safety may be smaller than the sector story suggests.

11. What should stay on the investor's dashboard?

A dashboard is a small set of recurring checks that tests whether the original business thesis is becoming more or less credible.

  • Demand: order intake, customer project readiness and the private-capex exposure of major end markets.
  • Order quality: pricing terms, advances, cancellations, concentration and margins on the work being won.
  • Execution: physical output, on-time delivery, rework, safety and trained-manpower availability.
  • Profit quality: absolute EBITDA, margin changes explained by mix or steel, and operating cash flow over several periods.
  • Capital: utilisation of new plants, working-capital needs, net debt and incremental returns after expansion.
  • Governance: related-party transactions, promoter actions, contingent liabilities and whether guidance is reconciled with results.

A new order announcement answers only one part of this list. The central idea is simple: the attractive PEB company is not the one that promises the most buildings. It is the one that repeatedly converts engineering skill into profitable delivery and collected cash.
