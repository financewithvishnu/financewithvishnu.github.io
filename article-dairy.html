Dairy: the business between the farmer and the fridge

By Finance With Vishnu · Source period: August 2025

A dairy company can sell milk every morning and still face an unpredictable year. The household buys a familiar packet. Behind it are thousands of small suppliers, changing milk composition, collection vehicles, chilling equipment, processing plants and a distribution route that cannot simply wait for a better selling price.

The appealing story is that consumers will buy more branded curd, paneer, cheese and ice cream. The harder question is whether the company earns enough extra cash after paying for the milk, refrigeration, packaging, distribution, promotion and inventory those products need.

Source period: August 2025. FY2024-25 company reports and primary dairy-processing explanations provide checks. They are dated examples, not current earnings estimates, a ranking of dairy brands or nutrition advice. Every worked financial example is hypothetical.

1. What business does a dairy company actually run?

A dairy business connects an agricultural supply system with a food-processing and distribution system. Fresh milk, milk powder, ghee, fermented products and frozen desserts do not have identical customers, storage needs or economics. A company may sell daily household packets, branded products through shops, or bulk ingredients to other food manufacturers.

I would first separate the activities. Does the company procure directly from farmers, buy through intermediaries, process for another brand, own consumer brands, or combine these models? Does it operate only in India or also own overseas businesses? A consolidated margin is the result of that mix, not a universal dairy margin.

Dodla's FY2025 report describes an integrated procurement-to-distribution model. That is a useful starting map, but integration must be tested link by link: a weak collection network cannot be repaired merely by installing a larger processing line.[2]

2. Why is a litre not the whole unit of analysis?

Milk contains water, fat and solids not fat, commonly shortened to SNF. NDDB explains that SNF includes proteins, carbohydrates and salts or ash, excluding milk fat. It also distinguishes separation, which divides milk into cream and skim milk, from standardisation, which adjusts fat and SNF to prepare a specified milk variety.[5]

That matters financially. Two supplies of the same volume can contain different amounts of useful solids. Procurement cost per litre, fat/SNF composition, product yield and selling mix must be read together. Comparing only litres bought and litres of packaged milk sold leaves out the solids that can enter other products.

Hatsun's FY2025 report says its farmer pricing uses fat and SNF tests and describes payments every ten days. Heritage likewise describes payment based on measured solids. Those are company-specific procurement practices, not proof that every farmer or dairy has the same contract.[1][3] The analytical point is simple: check what is paid for, how it is measured and what the plant can sell from it.

3. Can the buyer simply squeeze the farmer?

A temporary reduction in the buying price may improve today's spread. It can also weaken tomorrow's supply if competing buyers offer better terms. The processor needs farmers to keep delivering acceptable milk, including when procurement becomes difficult. Testing accuracy, payment reliability, local access and useful support can matter alongside the headline rate.

The farmer relationship is therefore part of the operating system, not a decorative social programme. A network built around timely collection and payment is harder to reproduce than a product label. But a large farmer count alone says little about daily supply, retention, quality or collection cost.

Hatsun describes village-level cooling and collection infrastructure. Heritage describes connected procurement, processing and consumption locations, and emphasises accurate measurement and timely payments in its FY2025 report.[1][3] I would investigate route-level supply, procurement concentration, rejected milk, payment discipline and the cost of maintaining that relationship. A national consumption story does not answer those local questions.

4. Why are chilling and hygiene economic assets?

A packet's printed price is meaningless if the product cannot reach the buyer in acceptable condition. Chilling, cleaning, laboratory testing, reliable power, suitable transport and traceability protect both the consumer and the business. They require spending before a failure becomes visible in revenue.

FSSAI's milk-products guidance discusses laboratory testing, separation of storage areas, temperature monitoring during transport, sanitation and effluent treatment. It is a processing-control reference, not a substitute for current product-specific rules or a certification of any named company.[4]

Pasteurisation and homogenisation are different processes. NDDB explains that homogenisation reduces fat-globule size, while pasteurisation uses heat treatment followed by cooling. Neither term should become a vague marketing synonym for every aspect of quality.[5] For business research, ask about rejected batches, returns, complaints, recall readiness and maintenance. Lower costs achieved by neglecting these controls are not better economics.

5. Why does route density matter?

A vehicle serving nearby outlets can spread its route cost over more delivered product. A new region may initially require longer distances, smaller drops and additional supervision. The processing plant can be efficient while the route remains uneconomic.

Consider two hypothetical routes, each costing Rs 4,000 a day before product costs. Delivering 2,000 litres allocates Rs 2 per litre to the route. Delivering only 1,000 litres allocates Rs 4. This is not a quoted industry rate. It shows why local density matters even when the national addressable market is large.

There is a limit to this logic. More volume is not helpful if it requires excessive discounts, creates unsold returns or overloads cold-chain controls. Track realised revenue after channel terms and returns, not just dispatched volume. Heritage's FY2025 description of closely connected supply-chain nodes supports examining distance and service together, rather than assuming a larger map always means a stronger network.[3]

6. How can a small milk-price change move profit sharply?

A dairy retains only part of its selling price after buying milk and paying other operating costs. A modest increase in procurement cost can therefore remove a large share of operating profit if selling prices do not change at once.

A price increase may restore the spread, but it can affect demand or take time to pass through channels. Product mix and milk composition can change at the same time. Avoid treating the difference between the household packet price and a quoted farmer rate as pure profit: they may refer to different specifications, locations and commercial boundaries.

7. What do flush, lean and milk solids change?

Milk availability is seasonal and regional. A processor needs a way to manage mismatches between fresh supply and demand. Converting surplus milk into products such as skimmed milk powder and butter can store some of that value for later use or sale. It also adds processing, storage and price risk.

NDDB's procurement handbook describes conversion capacity as part of balancing lean and flush supplies in the historical cooperative model. Its old statistics are not used here as current industry data.[6] Dodla's FY2025 report gives a dated operating example: it describes lower procurement costs benefiting the first half, later pressure in southern markets, geographic diversification and a move from net buying to net selling of milk solids.[2]

The key is to reconcile the full cycle. Selling stored solids can support revenue while carrying a different realisation or margin from branded products. A strong quarter may reflect inventory timing or a favourable procurement cycle rather than permanent brand improvement. Compare similar seasons, inventory quantities and procurement conditions before extrapolating.

8. Does value-added mean higher shareholder value?

Value-added products are not one uniform category. Curd, paneer, ghee, ice cream and protein products require different ingredients, processes, storage, packaging and selling effort. A premium shelf price is only the beginning of the calculation.

Suppose a hypothetical business earns Rs 8 contribution on Rs 100 of fresh-milk revenue and Rs 16 on Rs 100 of another product. Moving from an 80:20 revenue mix to 60:40 raises weighted contribution from Rs 9.60 to Rs 11.20 per Rs 100, before additional fixed costs. That is a 1.6 percentage-point improvement, not a doubling of profit.

Heritage reports FY2025 VAP contribution of 32% while excluding fat products from that measure.[3] Preserve that definition. Another company's inclusion of butter or ghee could make its reported VAP share incomparable. Ask for absolute sales, volumes, contribution and capital employed, not only the mix percentage.

9. When does a brand create pricing power?

Brand strength matters when consumers repeatedly choose the product at an economic price and can find it reliably. Advertising may create trial; distribution and consistent experience must turn trial into repeat purchasing. A launch is not proof of a durable franchise.

Measure repeat demand, channel replenishment, discounting, returns and profitability after promotional spending. A product can grow fast from a small base while adding little to total earnings. An established regional franchise can also struggle when entering a different taste, price or retail environment.

Dodla's FY2025 report describes increased brand investment alongside its value-added strategy.[2] That is a reminder to include the cost of building demand. A higher gross margin is not entirely available to shareholders if the company must continually spend more on advertising, chilled cabinets or channel incentives to keep the product moving.

10. Why can a daily-sales business still need working capital?

Frequent consumption does not guarantee immediate cash conversion. Farmers need dependable payment. Shops, distributors and institutional customers can pay on different schedules. Seasonal powder and butter stocks may remain on the balance sheet much longer than a fresh packet remains in a fridge.

Heritage explicitly discusses the timing between immediate payouts and receivables, particularly for value-added products, in its FY2025 working-capital explanation.[3] Read that alongside the actual inventory, receivable and payable notes rather than assuming all dairy sales are cash sales.

In a hypothetical business with annual revenue of Rs 1,000 crore, an extra ten revenue-equivalent days tied up in working capital is roughly Rs 27.4 crore: 1,000 multiplied by 10/365. Real inventory and payable calculations use their appropriate cost bases. The simplified number makes one point: small changes in days can absorb meaningful cash even while reported profit rises.

11. When does a new plant become productive capital?

A new plant needs milk supply, workable routes, product capability and customers. Commissioning creates the ability to produce; it does not prove profitable utilisation. The cash commitment can include collection infrastructure, processing lines, cold storage, vehicles, distribution support and opening inventory.

Dodla's FY2025 report discusses securing procurement ahead of its planned Maharashtra facility and distinguishes the proposed western B2B model from its southern operations.[2] This is a dated planning example, not confirmation of its current construction or utilisation status.

Compare incremental operating profit with all incremental capital required. A mature plant with depreciated assets can show an attractive historical return while a new project earns much less on today's construction cost. Separate replacement, compliance and growth spending.

12. What can defeat the premiumisation story?

Procurement inflation, weak demand, regional competition and distribution costs can arrive together. Weather can affect supply and seasonal product sales in different ways. A product launch may miss demand, a new plant may ramp slowly, or stored solids may be worth less than expected.

Food safety and environmental controls are also business risks with consequences beyond an earnings miss. Traceability, testing, cleaning, effluent management and corrective action require evidence. A familiar brand or an attractive return ratio does not certify those systems. FSSAI's guidance helps identify control areas, but actual compliance needs the applicable rules and site-specific records.[4]

Finally, a cooperative, private regional brand and bulk ingredient supplier may pursue different objectives and carry different product mixes. Do not declare one inherently superior from a single margin comparison. Nor should a national market-growth estimate become a forecast that every listed company will gain share at unchanged returns.

13. What belongs on the dairy research sheet?

I would track procurement volume and composition, procurement cost, milk and product volumes, net realisations, clearly defined VAP share, route economics, returns, inventory and cash conversion. Add regional concentration, maintenance and growth spending, funding and incremental plant utilisation.

Build a profit bridge: how much came from volume, price, mix, milk-cost movement, solids inventory and operating efficiency? Then build a cash bridge. Did that profit turn into cash after working capital and the spending needed to maintain food quality and productive capacity?

The strongest dairy thesis is more specific than rising consumption. It explains why this business can repeatedly collect acceptable milk, turn its components into products people want, deliver them reliably, pay suppliers fairly and retain an adequate cash return. The farmer, factory, route and fridge are one connected economic system.
