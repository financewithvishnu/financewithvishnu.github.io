Recycling: the scrap, the spread and the rules

By Finance With Vishnu · Source period: approximately July 2025 · Exact class date unconfirmed

A recycler buys what everyone else throws away and sells it back as raw material. The product is often identical to what a primary producer makes: refined lead is refined lead, whatever its past life. Yet the business could not be more different from mining. The mine's asset is under the ground; the recycler's asset is a collection network, a furnace and a licence.

Three things decide whether this business makes money: what the scrap costs, what the recovered material sells for, and what the rules allow and reward. Price, spread and compliance move together, and each can turn a good year into a bad one. Follow the scrap, the spread and the rules, and the financial statements start to explain themselves.

1. What does a recycler actually earn?

Not the metal price. A lead recycler earns the difference between what it pays for a used battery and what it receives for the refined lead, alloys and other products it recovers, minus the cost of collection, smelting, energy, compliance and finance. The London Metal Exchange price matters to both sides of that trade, so the recycler's real product is the spread between scrap and refined metal, plus whatever extra it earns from value-added forms.

Gravita India's FY2024-25 report shows the shape at scale: consolidated revenue of Rs 3,869 crore, up 22%, adjusted EBITDA of Rs 404 crore at a 10.43% margin, and profit after tax of Rs 312 crore, up 31%. Volumes grew 20% overall, and value-added products reached 46% of revenue.[1] Pondy Oxides and Chemicals, a smaller lead recycler, reported revenue of Rs 2,028 crore, up 33%, and net profit of Rs 65 crore, up 65%.[2] Two different sizes, one shared engine: buy scrap well, recover efficiently, sell into the metal market, and keep the spread.

2. Where does the scrap come from?

From a network, not a mine. Used batteries, bottles and tyres sit scattered across millions of homes, workshops and dealerships, and somebody has to gather them, grade them and move them to a plant. Gravita describes 33 of its own yards and over 1,900 touch points across Asia, Africa and the Americas, collecting more than 2,87,000 tonnes of scrap in FY2024-25.[1] Ganesha Ecosphere, a PET recycler, collects about 450 tonnes of PET waste daily through its national network.[3] The collection web is slow and expensive to build, and once built it is the real barrier to entry.

Watch where the network points. Gravita reported that domestically sourced scrap rose 60% in FY2024-25 and now covers 43% of its inputs, a shift it links to tighter regulations, lower logistics cost and a better working capital cycle.[1] Imported scrap exposes the recycler to foreign prices, currency, freight and other countries' export rules; domestic scrap trades those risks for collection effort.

3. Why is the margin a spread, not a price?

Because both sides of the trade float. When refined metal rises, scrap sellers raise their prices too. When metal falls, scrap follows with a lag. The recycler's margin lives in the gap, and that gap widens and narrows with scrap availability, competition for scrap and demand from rival buyers.

Gravita's report gives a rare, dated look at this in secondary aluminium: the scrap-to-semi-finished-product spread, described as a key market health indicator, narrowed from about Rs 33,000 to 34,000 per tonne in February 2025 to Rs 32,000 to 33,000 by March 2025, as scrap prices rose. In the December 2024 quarter the spread fell as low as Rs 25,000 to 28,000 per tonne, and the report notes that many secondary producers cut output and stopped booking import contracts because margins turned unviable.[1] That is the spread mechanism in action: when it compresses, volume is not worth having.

4. How do recyclers tame commodity risk?

By refusing to take a view on the metal price. Gravita describes a back-to-back hedging mechanism that locks in margins and protects against commodity price volatility.[1] Pondy Oxides, whose refined lead is an LME-registered brand, likewise describes hedging instruments that helped mitigate volatility.[2] The idea is simple: when scrap is bought, the corresponding metal sale is locked in, so the recycler earns the spread it contracted for rather than betting on where the LME goes next.

The price path in Pondy Oxides' report shows why this discipline matters: LME lead peaked near $2,220 a tonne in May 2024, then drifted down through late 2024 and hovered around $1,987 to $1,999 a tonne by December and January, with a recurring seasonal pattern tied to battery demand.[2] A recycler holding unhedged inventory through that slide loses money on metal, not on operations. Hedging converts a trading business into a processing business, which is what the financial statements can then be read as.

5. What decides the yield from a tonne of scrap?

Recovery is where the process earns its keep. A tonne of used batteries is not a tonne of lead: it is lead grids and paste, plastic cases, acid and separators. How much saleable metal and usable plastic comes out depends on the technology, the scrap quality and the discipline of the operation. Pondy Oxides' report contrasts the organised sector's higher efficiency, better recovery rates and lower emissions with informal smelters that bypass compliance costs.[2] The informal operator looks cheaper until yield, quality and the law are priced in.

Yield also improves when the recycler sells better forms. Gravita's value-added share of 46% of revenue, and its emphasis on increasing refined-lead content, point the same way: the closer the output sits to the customer's specification, the more of the spread stays with the recycler.[1] Tinna Rubber's consumer products, moulded goods, mats and tiles grew 55% to Rs 34 crore in FY2024-25 for the same reason: downstream forms carry more value than raw crumb rubber.[4]

6. Why has compliance become a business advantage?

Because the rules are redirecting the scrap itself. Pondy Oxides' report lays out the stack: the Battery Waste Management Rules, 2022, with their 2024 amendment, set collection and recycling compliance targets for battery types ranging from 55% to 90% over three years ending 2026-27; Extended Producer Responsibility requires producers to buy EPR credits from approved recyclers, with penalties per tonne for shortfalls; and a GST reverse charge now applies to metal scrap transactions. The report projects the organised sector's share of India's lead recycling rising from 35% in 2023 to 80% by 2029.[2] Treat that projection as the company's cited estimate, but the direction is structural: scrap that once leaked into informal channels must now flow through registered ones.

7. What is an EPR credit worth?

Real money, booked as revenue. Tinna Rubber accrued EPR credits worth Rs 29.6 crore in FY2024-25, on consolidated revenue of Rs 505 crore.[4] That is close to 6% of revenue arising from the rule book rather than from a physical product. The credit exists because a tyre producer somewhere must show recycling, and Tinna's certified tonnes supply that proof.

The PET side has its own version: recycled-content targets create guaranteed demand for food-grade rPET, and Ganesha Ecosphere's report notes the 30% recycled-content target for PET bottles in FY2025-26 alongside its FSSAI, US FDA and EFSA certifications for bottle-grade chips.[3] When studying a recycler, separate the credit income from the processing margin. Credits can vanish if rules change, if credit prices fall, or if capacity races ahead of obligations; the processing margin is the durable part.

8. Why does working capital decide who survives?

Recycling is a cash-cycle business. Scrap is bought for cash from dealers and collectors who do not wait, while refined output is sold to industrial customers who take credit. The recycler funds the gap: inventory of scrap and finished goods on one side, receivables on the other. Gravita explicitly credits its improving working capital cycle to higher domestic scrap procurement, inventory management and a diversified supplier base.[1] Pondy Oxides raised Rs 175 crore through a qualified institutional placement during FY2024-25, in part to fund its expansion.[2]

The reading habit: inventory days, receivable days and borrowing cost belong on the same line as the spread. A recycler reporting rising spreads alongside ballooning inventory has not improved, it has just parked the risk in the warehouse.

9. Does scale help, and at what point?

Scale helps twice: in buying scrap, where a bigger network gets first look at supply, and in recovery, where larger furnaces and shared compliance costs lower the per-tonne expense. But recycling capacity is easy to announce and slow to fill. Pondy Oxides commissioned the first 36,000 tonnes of a 72,000-tonne licensed facility in Tamil Nadu during FY2024-25, part of a plan taking lead-alloy capacity past 1,20,000 tonnes.[2] Tinna scaled tyre-processing capacity to 1,85,000 tonnes a year and targets 2,50,000 tonnes by FY2026-27.[4]

The number that disciplines capacity is utilisation. Tinna's new Varale plant ran at 55% utilisation in FY2024-25 while its Oman plant ran at 85%.[4] Gravita frames the same discipline financially: a 25% ROIC benchmark, a maximum three-year payback and at least 8 times asset turns for new projects, with brownfield additions completed in six to nine months.[1] When a recycler announces a big plant, the question is not the capacity but the date at which it runs full, and what spread it earns while getting there.

10. Is PET recycling a different business?

Same skeleton, different anatomy. The spread logic holds: Ganesha Ecosphere buys waste bottles and sells recycled polyester fibre, chips and granules. But the customer set changes everything. Bottle-grade rPET sold for food packaging must carry safety certifications, and Ganesha lists FSSAI, US FDA and EFSA approvals for its bottle-grade chips, partnerships with Coca-Cola on circular packaging, and over 1,50,000 tonnes of PET waste converted in FY2024-25, equivalent to more than 8.5 billion bottles.[3] The certification wall replaces the furnace wall: entry requires years of process validation, not just capital.

The economics differ in temperament too. Ganesha reported revenue of Rs 984 crore and profit after tax of Rs 75 crore in FY2024-25, with a return on capital employed of 8.89%, describing pressure from textile overcapacity and volatile scrap bottle prices.[3] Compare that with Tinna's 15% EBITDA margin or Gravita's 10.43%.[4][1] Recycled content mandates promise demand, but the selling side still competes with virgin polyester tied to crude oil. A mandate can create volume without creating pricing power.

11. Is tyre recycling different again?

Yes, because the output has no exchange price. There is no LME for crumb rubber. Tinna converts end-of-life tyres into crumb rubber, modified bitumen inputs, moulded products and mats, so each tonne must find its own buyer and application rather than clearing at a quoted metal price.[4] That makes the business part recycler, part specialty manufacturer: the spread is negotiated product by product, and the product mix, from infrastructure crumb to consumer tiles, decides the margin.

The results show what execution can do in that setting: Tinna's FY2024-25 revenue of Rs 505 crore against guidance of Rs 500 crore, EBITDA of Rs 76 crore at a 15% margin, profit after tax of Rs 48 crore, and a three-year profit CAGR the company puts at 42%.[4] It also shows the inputs to watch: the same report flags raw material costs and ocean freight as headwinds, and notes EPR credits of Rs 29.6 crore inside those numbers.[4] Without a benchmark price, the quality of the order book and the breadth of applications carry the analysis.

12. What can go wrong?

Start with the informal sector. Pondy Oxides' report is blunt: unorganised smelters enjoy lower costs and higher collection volumes precisely because they skip compliance.[2] Every formal recycler competes for scrap against buyers who do not pay for pollution control. The rules are narrowing that advantage, but the pace of enforcement, not the text of the rule, decides the pace of the shift.

Then the market itself. The December 2024 quarter's compressed aluminium spreads show how fast the spread can close.[1] Add scrap-export restrictions abroad, freight swings on imported feed, and currency moves on every traded leg.[1] And finally the rule risk in reverse: EPR credit income, recycled-content demand and compliance-driven scrap flows all rest on regulation staying enforced. A recycler whose profit pool assumes today's credit prices carries a policy exposure that no hedge covers.

13. What should the tracking sheet explain?

My tracking sheet for a recycler would answer, with dates: What does the company recycle, and what share of revenue comes from each material? Where does the scrap come from, and how did the domestic-versus-import mix shift this year? What does the per-tonne spread look like, and which way did scrap and product prices each move? How much of the metal position is hedged? What recovery yields and value-added shares are reported? How much revenue and profit comes from EPR credits, and at what price assumption? What are inventory days, receivable days and the cash conversion cycle, and what does the borrowing cost consume? What capacity was added, at what utilisation is it running, and what return benchmarks does management apply? And for PET or rubber, what certifications, applications and customers anchor the selling side?

None of this requires predicting metal prices, bottle prices or policy. It records whether the business is understood well enough that a squeezed spread, a shifting scrap mix or a rule change would be noticed and read correctly. That record is the real output of the study.
