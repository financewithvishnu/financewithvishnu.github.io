Part VI · Fixed Income and Credit

Chapter 45. Money Markets and Cash Management

Chapter 45 of 85 · about 19 minutes

Every financial system needs a place to park money for a week, a month, a quarter, and it cannot be the stock market. That place is the money market: treasury bills, commercial paper, certificates of deposit and repos, instruments where the question is never "will it grow?" but only "will it be there?" This chapter is about that plumbing, and about the most practical question in personal finance: where your emergency fund should actually live.

45.1What are treasury bills, commercial paper and certificates of deposit?

Three borrowers, three IOUs, all under a year. Treasury bills are the government's short borrowings, sold in 91, 182 and 364-day tenors, issued at a discount and redeemed at face: you buy a 91-day bill at ₹98.30 and receive ₹100 at maturity, the ₹1.70 being your interest, an annualised yield of (1.70 ÷ 98.30) × (365 ÷ 91) ≈ 6.94%. There is no coupon and essentially no default risk; the T-bill yield is the closest thing India has to a risk-free rate at the short end. Commercial paper is the same idea issued by companies: an unsecured, discounted promissory note, 7 days to a year, used by blue-chip corporates and NBFCs to fund working capital cheaper than a bank loan; it must be rated, and its yield sits above T-bills by the credit spread of Chapter 44. Certificates of deposit are bank deposits in tradable form: a CD is a fixed deposit that can change hands, which lets banks raise bulk short money and lets funds hold bank paper with liquidity. Read the three as one instrument with three issuers: sovereign, corporate, bank, in rising order of yield and risk.

45.2What are repo and reverse repo, and what is the central bank corridor?

A repo is a loan wearing a sale's clothes: you sell a security today with an agreement to buy it back tomorrow at a slightly higher price, the difference being the interest. It is collateralised lending, the safest kind, and it is how banks, funds and the RBI move cash around the system overnight. The RBI runs a corridor around this market. At the floor, the standing deposit facility, banks park surplus cash with the RBI at a rate a notch below the policy rate. At the ceiling, the marginal standing facility, they borrow against their G-secs a notch above it. In the middle sits the repo rate, the price at which the RBI lends against collateral, and the number the entire country watches every two months. When the MPC cuts the repo rate by 50 basis points, the whole money market, T-bills, CP, CDs, liquid-fund returns, re-prices within days, and Chapter 4's transmission mechanism then carries the cut, imperfectly, to loan and deposit rates. The corridor is the machine under every short rate you will ever earn.

45.3How do liquid and overnight funds park cash properly?

For an investor, the money market arrives wrapped as two fund categories. Overnight funds lend for exactly one day, every day: near-zero rate risk, near-zero credit risk, returns that track the bottom of the corridor. Liquid funds hold paper maturing within 91 days: a little more yield, a little more sensitivity, and a small but real credit exposure, as investors learned in 2018 when a liquid fund holding IL&FS paper took a visible knock. Both exist to be boring. Their returns are the short rate minus a fee of roughly 0.2-0.4%, their NAVs creep upward in a near-straight line, and redemption lands in a day. Judge them on three things only: the portfolio's credit quality (all sovereign and AAA is a choice, not a default), the expense ratio, and whether you can tolerate the once-in-a-decade credit bruise in exchange for the extra half percent over an overnight fund.

45.4Fixed deposits or money-market funds: which parks cash better?

The honest comparison has four rows. Certainty: the FD wins, a contracted rate and DICGC insurance up to ₹5 lakh per bank per depositor; the liquid fund's return is a path, not a promise. Flexibility: the fund wins, no lock-in and no breakage penalty; break an FD early and the bank docks the rate and charges a penalty. Return: at today's rates they are within a percent of each other, with FDs often a touch higher for locking in and liquid funds a touch lower for instant liquidity. Tax: after the 2023 rule change, both are taxed at your slab, so this is no longer a reason to choose. The practical synthesis most households land on: keep the transaction money in a savings account or sweep-in FD, keep the true emergency reserve in a liquid fund or a short FD ladder, and never let the pursuit of an extra half percent move emergency money into anything that can be locked, gated or marked down on the day you need it.

45.5Worked examples and cases

Case 1: where a six-month emergency fund actually lives

Suppose your household spends ₹1 lakh a month, so the target reserve is ₹6 lakh. A sensible structure: one month, ₹1 lakh, in the savings account or a sweep-in FD for same-hour access; the remaining ₹5 lakh split between a liquid fund and a couple of laddered FDs maturing a month apart. At illustrative rates, savings 3%, liquid fund 6.5%, the blended yield is about 5.9%, roughly ₹35,500 a year against ₹18,000 if it all sat in the savings account. Notice what this structure is not doing: it is not in equity ("the market is down exactly when jobs are lost" is the cruel correlation), not in locked products beyond the horizon, not in credit-risk funds chasing yield with money whose only job is to exist. An emergency fund is insurance that happens to earn interest. Underwrite it like insurance.

Case 2: the sweep-in FD, one setting worth ₹12,000 a year

Many salary accounts let you set a threshold, say ₹50,000, above which surplus is automatically parked in fixed deposits and automatically broken, newest first, when the account needs the money. Compare two identical households holding a ₹3 lakh average surplus. Without the sweep it idles at 3%: ₹9,000 a year. Swept into FDs at 7%: ₹21,000. The difference, ₹12,000 a year, costs one trip to the bank's settings page and nothing else, because liquidity is unchanged: the money comes back the day it is spent. The lesson generalises. Cash management is not investing; it is the removal of friction between your money and the rate it should be earning. Sweep-in FDs, liquid funds and RBI Retail Direct T-bill ladders are three versions of the same move, and the only wrong answer is the default one: a large balance earning the savings rate because nobody set the threshold.
