Commercial vehicles: follow the operator before the order book

By Finance With Vishnu · Source period: approximately mid-January 2026 · Sector study

A truck is not bought for the same reason as a family car. For its owner, it is a working asset. It must carry goods, stay on the road, collect freight income and leave enough cash after fuel, wages, maintenance and financing.

That is where I would begin a commercial-vehicle study. Not with a forecast that the next upcycle must repeat the last one, but with the economics of the person placing the order.

Once those economics improve, the effects can travel through vehicle makers, component suppliers, workshops and lenders. But the timing, risk and profit available to each business can be very different. A shared industry label does not make them interchangeable investments.

Industry data is taken from SIAM's domestic-sales series. The 2021 scrappage-policy announcement is historical context only, not a statement of today's rules. 1. Why are commercial vehicles not one market?

Start with what the vehicle does. A small delivery vehicle, a long-haul truck, a construction tipper, a school bus and a tourist coach face different demand drivers.

Last-mile deliveries depend on local distribution and consumption. Long-haul freight depends on goods movement between locations. Tippers depend on the activity at construction and mining sites. Passenger applications depend on routes, utilisation, institutions and travel demand.

Even familiar labels need care. Light, medium and heavy categories are not always presented with identical boundaries across companies and datasets. Gross vehicle weight includes the vehicle and its permitted load; it is not the same thing as payload. Use the source's definition rather than classifying a truck from its appearance.

Eicher's FY2025 report describes VECV's range across trucks, buses, engines and components. Tata Motors' FY2025 report separates commercial vehicles from its other automotive businesses. Those distinctions help us choose the right demand and financial series. [1, 2]

2. What makes an operator buy another truck?

An operator needs a reason to commit capital. More reliable loads, better freight rates, higher utilisation, lower downtime or a more efficient vehicle can improve the case. Financing terms and the value of the old vehicle matter too.

Think in cash per vehicle, not just revenue. A higher freight rate can be cancelled out by more empty running, higher fuel costs or slower collection. A busy truck is not necessarily a profitable truck.

In a hypothetical month, an operator collects ₹3.0 lakh and pays ₹2.4 lakh for operating costs and debt service, leaving ₹60,000. If collections rise to ₹3.3 lakh with those cash outgoings unchanged, the remainder rises to ₹90,000. A 10% increase in collections produces a 50% increase in the remainder.

This is a simplified cash example, not a profitability estimate for the industry. It excludes taxes, replacement reserves and several possible expenses. Its purpose is to show why small changes in operating conditions can produce large changes in willingness to buy.

Before accepting an optimistic OEM commentary, I would ask whether the improvement has reached the operator's cash account, and whether it is widespread or limited to certain routes and applications.

3. Why can freight growth and truck sales diverge?

Goods movement is not the same as demand for new vehicles. Existing fleets can work more hours, carry different loads, improve return-load utilisation or replace smaller vehicles with larger ones.

Suppose freight demand rises from 100 to 110 units while productive output per vehicle rises from 10 to 11. The same ten vehicles can handle the new workload in this simplified example. Freight grows by 10%, but the arithmetic does not require an extra vehicle.

Conversely, replacement buying can support new-vehicle sales even when the fleet itself barely expands. Units sold, carrying capacity added and the total fleet are different measures.

This is why I would track product mix alongside volume. A shift towards heavier vehicles can increase capacity and revenue without an equal increase in unit sales. Changes in legal operating limits can also affect capacity, but any specific regulatory claim needs its own dated official source.

The important question is not whether the economy is growing. It is how much additional vehicle capacity the relevant customer needs after using the capacity already available.

4. How do replacement demand and pre-buying differ?

Replacement demand arises when an owner changes an existing vehicle. Reasons can include maintenance costs, reliability, fuel efficiency, customer requirements or applicable rules. There is no single replacement age that guarantees an industry upcycle.

Pre-buying is different. Customers bring a planned purchase forward, perhaps ahead of an expected price increase or a confirmed regulatory transition. It can make current sales stronger without creating the same amount of additional long-term demand.

Ask how much growth comes from new capacity, replacement and purchases brought forward. These categories are hard to measure perfectly, but keeping them separate prevents one strong quarter from becoming an assumption of permanent acceleration.

5. How should we handle scrappage and regulation?

Read the actual rule. Separate an announcement, a draft, a final notification, an implementation date and the vehicles or geography covered.

The government's March 2021 scrappage-policy announcement described a programme centred on vehicle fitness and stated proposals and tentative timelines. It is useful historical evidence of the policy's design, not a substitute for checking today's applicable notifications. [3]

I would not turn a rumour about an age-based ban into a national replacement forecast. Local restrictions, fitness requirements and different vehicle categories can produce very different effects. The number of old vehicles is not automatically the number of new vehicles that will be purchased.

For a confirmed safety or emissions transition, check who must comply, when, what the extra equipment costs and whether buyers are responding. Suppliers may gain more content per vehicle, but the OEM and operator may face higher costs.

The same event can help one business and hurt another. Even where pre-buying occurs, demand after the transition needs its own assessment. Do not count the same purchase once before the rule and again afterwards.

6. Why do service and distribution matter?

A working vehicle loses income when it cannot operate. The purchase price is therefore only one part of the decision. Availability of parts, repair capability, warranty support, financing and resale confidence can affect the total ownership experience.

Ashok Leyland's FY2025 report discusses its product portfolio and aftersales support, including an uptime centre. Eicher's FY2025 report describes VECV's service network and connected services. These are disclosed business capabilities, not proof that one brand is always the best choice on every route. [2, 4]

A wide network can be an advantage when it delivers reliable support where the operator needs it. Counting outlets alone is weaker evidence. Workshop quality, parts availability and the customer's actual downtime matter.

For a new entrant, a good vehicle is necessary but may not be enough. It also needs a workable service system and customer trust. For an established supplier, the network still needs investment and execution; it is not an automatic right to high margins.

I would look for repeat buying, customer retention, service economics and evidence of useful support, rather than treating network size as a complete moat analysis.

7. What differs between an OEM and a component supplier?

An OEM integrates the vehicle and sells the finished proposition. A component supplier may sell a forging, axle, spring, braking system or other part. It can have a different customer mix, contract structure and capital requirement.

A supplier with a small commercial-vehicle exposure is not a pure proxy for the domestic truck cycle. Exports, passenger vehicles, industrial applications or another product line may dominate its results.

Jamna Auto's Q4 FY2025 presentation shows suspension products and describes plans for integrated suspension manufacturing. It provides a concrete example of the move from individual components towards a broader system offering. The plans are historical disclosures, not confirmation that every proposed facility is now operational. [5]

More content per vehicle can support growth even when vehicle volumes are flat. But the supplier must win the programme, make the product reliably and earn a return on the additional investment.

For each company I would record domestic versus export exposure, vehicle categories, major customers, new products and the exact reporting boundary. A long list of possible customers is not evidence of current revenue from them.

8. Does the aftermarket remove cyclicality?

The aftermarket serves vehicles already in use. That can make its demand pattern different from sales of new vehicles. Maintenance and replacement parts do not stop simply because OEM production slows.

However, the aftermarket still depends on how much vehicles operate, how owners maintain them and where they buy parts. A weak operator can defer spending. Competition, distribution costs and stock availability also affect the supplier.

Jamna Auto's presentation separates market and product-development initiatives. Its example is useful because expanding the aftermarket is a commercial task, not merely redirecting a factory's output. [5]

A supplier needs the right range, channel relationships and inventory. Selling many small orders through distributors can require a different operating system from delivering large batches directly to an OEM.

I would not automatically assign a higher value to every rupee called aftermarket revenue. Compare margins, working capital, channel concentration, returns and cash collection. Diversification helps only if it improves the economics after those costs.

9. Why can profit move faster than sales?

Manufacturing has costs that do not change one-for-one with short-term volume. When output rises, those costs can be spread across more units. The reverse can happen when output falls.

Take an original teaching example. A business sells 100 units at ₹10 each. Variable cost is ₹7 per unit and fixed operating cost is ₹200. Operating profit is ₹100. At 120 units with the same unit economics and fixed cost, profit becomes ₹160.

Actual margins also depend on discounts, raw materials, product mix, new-product costs and currency. Better profit is not always evidence of a permanent cost advantage. It may reflect favourable utilisation near the stronger part of the cycle.

That is why I would stress-test earnings at lower volumes before calling a cyclical business cheap on its latest price-to-earnings ratio.

10. Which monthly data is worth tracking?

Use consistent series. Production, domestic sales, exports and registrations answer different questions. A company's dispatches are not necessarily the same as deliveries to the final operator.

SIAM publishes separate production, domestic-sales and export trends. Its historical domestic CV series shows 9,62,468 units in FY2022-23, 9,68,770 in FY2023-24 and 9,58,679 in FY2024-25. These are industry totals, not an identical cycle for every truck and bus subsegment. [6]

The figures are a useful antidote to a story in which all commercial-vehicle demand rises smoothly every year. They do not, by themselves, identify today's turning point.

Track month-on-month and year-on-year changes with seasonality and the comparison base in mind. A high growth percentage after an unusually weak month can be misleading. A single month can also be affected by holidays, inventory adjustments or tender deliveries.

Compare a rolling period, company mix, channel inventory and operator conditions. When comparing market share, use the same geography, weight category, period and sales definition on both sides of the fraction.

11. Why do buses and electric vehicles need another lens?

A bus sale can come from a private operator, school, institution or public-transport programme. Delivery schedules and purchasing processes differ from those of a small freight operator.

Some contracts may include operation or service obligations as well as vehicle supply. Determine who owns the asset, who pays, when revenue is recognised and what performance obligations remain.

An electric vehicle also changes the cost structure. Energy costs, charging access, route distance, utilisation, financing, maintenance and battery-related obligations all matter. The relevant comparison is the ownership and operating economics for that use case, not just the sticker price.

The electric-vehicle study explains that wider chain. Here the point is to avoid treating an electric-bus order as identical to an immediately paid conventional truck sale.

A large order announcement is only the beginning of the cash-flow analysis. Check execution milestones, acceptance conditions, payment security, investment requirements and the cost of providing the promised service.

12. Why are lenders a different business?

Financing can enable an operator to buy a vehicle, but a lender does not earn the same way as the manufacturer. It earns a spread while carrying funding, credit, operating and capital costs.

Faster loan growth is not enough. An investor must study borrower quality, collections, delinquencies, provisions, funding conditions and the capital required to support growth.

New-vehicle and used-vehicle lending can involve different ticket sizes, borrower profiles and collateral risks. A strong year for vehicle sales does not guarantee a better loan book.

The manufacturer's financial statements can also include financing businesses, joint ventures and subsidiaries. Eicher's FY2025 report, for example, explains that its share of VECV profit is included as a single line rather than VECV revenue being consolidated into the parent's revenue. [2]

Read the accounting perimeter before comparing sales, margins or valuation multiples. Do not add every related business together as though shareholders own the same proportion of each cash flow.

13. What would make the research convincing?

I would want several pieces of evidence to agree: better operator economics, demand across the relevant segment, sensible channel inventory and company cash generation that supports reported profit.

I would become less confident if dispatches rise while operator conditions weaken, inventory grows faster than demand or a thesis depends on an unconfirmed regulatory event.

A good business can remain cyclical. A strong cycle can also make an ordinary business look unusually profitable. The task is to separate the two before paying for earnings that may not last.

Commercial vehicles begin with a simple question: can the customer earn enough from the asset? Follow that answer through the value chain, then test how much cash the supplier keeps when the cycle turns.
