Aerospace suppliers: from qualified capability to cash

By Finance With Vishnu · Source period: approximately November 2025 · Sector study

A large aircraft order can make the aerospace opportunity look simple. Airlines need planes, manufacturers have backlogs and suppliers must make more parts. But between an aircraft order and a component supplier's cash collection sits a long chain of engineering, qualification, scheduling and production.

That chain is where the business becomes interesting. A small part can demand difficult manufacturing and strict process control. A supplier can spend years building a capability before meaningful revenue appears. Yet the same long development period can consume capital without guaranteeing a profitable production programme.

I would study aerospace suppliers by asking what they are approved to make, which programme creates demand, what the customer has actually committed to buy and how much cash remains after the ramp-up.

Study window: the commercial-aerospace and precision-engineering classes from approximately November 2025. Exact class days are unconfirmed. Company illustrations use FY2024-25 reports, Airbus' February 2024 contract announcement and FY2025 Airbus/Safran results published in February 2026, after the classes. This is an explanatory study, not a current production-rate, order-book or valuation update. Defence programmes and speculative corporate transactions are outside its scope.

1. Where does a supplier sit in the aircraft?

Separate the aircraft manufacturer from the maker of an engine or major system, the maker of a subassembly and the maker of an individual component. Then distinguish all of those from the provider of manufacturing or maintenance tools.

The labels tier one, tier two and tier three describe relationships in a supply chain. They are not permanent quality grades. A company can supply one customer directly and another through an intermediary. Its position can differ by product or programme.

An engine component is not an entire engine. A door assembly is not an aircraft. A tool used to repair a component may never become a flying part. These distinctions determine the addressable market, approvals, purchasing pattern and business risks.

Before using a prestigious customer name, establish the exact product, contracting entity and relationship. A product that ultimately appears on an Airbus aircraft need not be sold directly to Airbus.

2. Why does qualification take more than buying a machine?

A machine can create a physical capability. It does not by itself establish a repeatable process, a qualified part or a production relationship with a customer.

Material control, tooling, programming, inspection, documentation, traceability and reliable delivery all matter. Different parts and processes carry different requirements. Avoid assuming that every company faces the same approval route or the same fixed number of years before production.

The IAQG describes its 9100-series scheme as aerospace quality-management-system certification, supported by accredited audits and the OASIS information system. That is evidence about a quality system. It should not be confused with a purchase order for a particular part or approval of an entire aircraft. [1]

Azad Engineering's FY2025 report describes qualified parts, specialised manufacturing processes and a business spanning aerospace, energy and oil and gas. Sansera's FY2025 report describes a multi-year expansion of its precision-engineering capabilities. These are useful examples of capability building, not a universal promise that qualification leads to high returns. [2, 3]

I would look for evidence of what has been qualified and where it is used, rather than treating a certificate displayed in an investor presentation as the whole investment case.

3. Is an aircraft backlog a supplier's order book?

No. The airframer's backlog records orders at the aircraft level. A supplier's revenue depends on its programme exposure, content, customer sourcing decisions, releases, delivery schedule and contract terms.

Airbus reported 8,754 commercial aircraft in its year-end 2025 backlog and 793 commercial deliveries during 2025. It also discussed supply constraints and programme-specific production plans. The figures show why demand visibility and near-term execution are separate questions. They do not establish a delivery timetable for an individual Indian supplier. [4]

Do not simply divide the backlog by the latest delivery rate and call the result guaranteed years of revenue. Order changes, programme mix, production changes and supplier allocation can alter the path.

At supplier level, separate a framework agreement, an estimated lifetime programme value, a scheduled release and a firm purchase order. Ask which assumptions sit behind the reported order book and whether the number is gross of future price adjustments or changes in quantities.

A long-term agreement can be valuable. It is still a contract whose duration, volumes, pricing and termination terms need to be understood.

4. What does content per aircraft tell us?

Content per aircraft is the value a supplier expects to provide for each relevant aircraft, or shipset. A shipset is the set required for one aircraft under the particular product definition. It is not necessarily one physical part.

A simple revenue model multiplies relevant aircraft production by supplier participation and content per aircraft. But every term needs grounding. A supplier may be one of several sources, its ramp may lag aircraft production and prices may change over time.

Suppose a hypothetical programme makes 100 aircraft, a supplier provides half the required shipsets and its revenue is ₹2 lakh per shipset. The resulting programme revenue is ₹1 crore. If the aircraft rate increases by 20% with the same share and content, it becomes ₹1.2 crore.

That is arithmetic, not a model of any named company. It illustrates why an industry growth estimate alone is insufficient. The supplier's actual participation can matter as much as the aircraft rate.

Track content won, participation, delivery readiness and price separately. Announced potential value is not the same as annual revenue.

5. What does a real contract announcement establish?

It can establish a specific relationship and scope. It may not disclose the contract's margin, annual revenue, minimum purchase obligation or the supplier's required investment.

Airbus' February 2024 announcement awarded Dynamatic Technologies manufacturing and assembly work for A220-family doors. It described eight doors per aircraft, detailed-parts opportunities and additional capacity alongside an existing source. It also distinguished this from the A320 door contract awarded to Tata Advanced Systems. [5]

This is a useful case because it corrects several easy mistakes. A220 and A320 are different programmes. An award adding another source is not proof of global sole-source status. A contract announcement does not prove that a later production ramp has already been achieved.

Moving from a component to an assembly can increase the work and value handled by a supplier. It can also increase coordination, inspection, inventory and delivery obligations.

I would follow the award with evidence of qualification progress, production delivery, customer acceptance and cash. Do not stop at the press release or fill undisclosed economics with an attractive guess.

6. Why can the ramp-up be financially painful?

New programmes can require facilities, machines, people, materials and development work before the supplier earns meaningful revenue. Early output may have lower productivity and more rework than mature production.

Capacity available on paper is not the same as accepted output. The practical constraint could be a specialised process, trained labour, inspection capacity or the availability of suitable material.

A supplier can make progress at the first two stages while still consuming cash. This is not automatically a failure. It is a reason to compare spending, milestones and the funding plan.

Ask how much of the investment is reusable across customers and how much is tied to one programme. Also ask whether the customer contributes to tooling or development costs, and what happens if the launch is delayed.

7. How do yield and inspection affect economics?

The selling price of an accepted part is only one side of the calculation. Material, machine time, labour, inspection and any unsuccessful output sit on the other side.

In an original simplified example, a batch costs ₹80,000 before any additional rework costs. If 80 accepted units result, the cost per accepted unit is ₹1,000. If the same batch cost produces 90 accepted units, it falls to about ₹889.

Actual manufacturing is more complicated, but the direction is important. Better yield can release capacity and improve cost. Poor quality can consume expensive material and production hours without generating accepted sales.

There is no case for relaxing a requirement to improve this arithmetic. The business must achieve efficiency within the specified quality and process controls.

I would track reliable output, on-time delivery, rework and customer acceptance where disclosed. Installed machine count alone does not explain profitability.

8. Is maintenance the same business as maintenance tooling?

Maintenance, repair and overhaul, or MRO, covers work on aircraft, engines and components. The provider carrying out that work is different from the manufacturer selling tools used by the provider.

Unimech's FY2025 report describes aero and MRO tooling for aero-engine and airframe applications, alongside precision components and assemblies. It also describes high-mix, low-volume manufacturing. That supports a tooling-supplier classification, not the assumption that every rupee of its revenue is a recurring maintenance-service payment. [6]

A workshop may need a tool to carry out a task, but it does not necessarily buy a new tool every time it performs that task. Tool demand can depend on new facilities, new programmes, replacement, additional capacity and customer specifications.

Safran's FY2025 results distinguish original-equipment activity, spare parts and services, including rate-per-flight-hour contracts. That is a reminder that even within the aftermarket, the commercial model can differ. [7]

Ask who buys, what they buy, when they reorder and what obligations follow. The attractive economics of an engine-service contract should not be copied onto a tooling manufacturer's valuation without evidence.

9. Why can aftermarket demand differ from new aircraft demand?

New aircraft production requires parts for new units. The aftermarket supports the installed fleet. Flight activity, fleet composition, maintenance needs and retirement decisions can affect demand differently.

If new-aircraft deliveries are delayed, an operator may keep an existing aircraft in service for longer. That can support some maintenance activity while a new-production supplier waits for its ramp. It does not benefit every aftermarket company equally.

Safran's February 2026 results linked its 2025 civil-engine spare-parts and services activity to factors including shop visits, work content and the expansion of LEAP aftermarket activity. These are historical company explanations, not a guarantee that every maintenance category grows at the same rate. [7]

Analyse the relevant fleet and product. A consumable, a repairable component, a long-lived tool and a service agreement have different repeat-demand patterns.

Also check who owns the customer relationship and the intellectual property. A supplier manufacturing a part to another company's design does not automatically receive the economics of the design owner.

10. What does an aerospace label hide inside a group?

A listed engineering company can also serve cars, industrial turbines, energy projects or other markets. Its aerospace capability may be important while still contributing a minority of current revenue.

Azad's FY2025 report separates aerospace and defence from its larger energy and oil-and-gas activities. Sansera's FY2025 report describes automotive products as well as expansion into aerospace, defence and semiconductor-related manufacturing. These examples show why group revenue should not be relabelled entirely as commercial aerospace. [2, 3]

A combined aerospace, defence and semiconductor category is not a separately disclosed commercial-aerospace business. Do not allocate its revenue or margin precisely unless the company provides the evidence.

Group profit can also be affected by losses in another division, financing, tax and corporate costs. A sum-of-the-parts exercise needs ownership, debt and shared-cost treatment, not just a higher multiple for the most exciting segment.

I would keep the reporting boundary visible in every comparison. A plant, subsidiary, joint venture and listed parent can have different economics and different claims on the cash.

11. Where can profit and cash diverge?

A supplier can recognise profit while cash is tied up in material, work in progress, completed goods or receivables. It may also be investing heavily in future production.

In a hypothetical year, operating cash before new equipment spending is ₹20 crore and capex is ₹30 crore. Cash after that spending is negative ₹10 crore. Future returns may justify it, but funding is still required.

Inventory needs interpretation rather than an automatic verdict. Long lead-time materials or a planned ramp can explain a build. So can delayed acceptance, excessive buying or weaker demand. Compare inventory with production milestones, customer releases and ageing disclosures.

Read definitions of adjusted returns carefully. Azad's FY2025 report presents an adjusted return-on-capital measure that excludes items including work in progress on capital projects and capex advances from the relevant capital base. That measure can answer a different question from returns on all the money shareholders have committed. [2]

I would calculate both operating performance and the return on total invested capital, explaining any exclusions. Capital waiting to earn revenue is still capital at risk.

12. Do high entry barriers guarantee pricing power?

No. Qualification can make switching difficult, but customers can still negotiate prices, seek productivity improvements, add sources or redesign a product. A supplier may face a small number of powerful buyers.

Being hard to replace is useful only if the commercial terms allow the supplier to earn a return after investment, quality and delivery obligations.

Concentration also links the supplier to programme-specific problems. A delay, production interruption or reduced build rate at one major customer can affect a highly capable manufacturer.

Currency, tariffs, imported material and specialised subcontracting can create further risk. The location of final delivery does not always reveal every geographic exposure in the chain.

I would stress-test slower production, lower yield, later collection and more capex. A thesis that works only at the customer's most optimistic ramp is too fragile. The industry can be strategically important while a particular investment remains expensive or poorly funded.

13. What would I keep in the research file?

Keep capability, management ambition, contracted work and realised results in separate columns. This lets us appreciate a difficult engineering achievement without treating every ambition as future profit.

I would gain confidence when a supplier converts qualification into repeat accepted deliveries, funds its investment sensibly and collects cash. I would lose confidence when the backlog narrative expands but cash conversion and disclosure do not improve.

Aerospace rewards patience in building capability. Investing in it also requires patience with the evidence, especially the evidence that turns a technically impressive part into a financially sound business.
