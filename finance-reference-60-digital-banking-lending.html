Chapter 60. Digital Banking and Lending Fintech

Chapter 60 of 85 · about 19 minutes

The bank in your pocket is not always a bank. A generation of apps now lends, holds deposits and sells financial products with a slickness the old institutions never managed, and the single most important skill in this corner of finance is telling where the app ends and the regulated institution begins. This chapter draws that line for digital banking and lending, and prices the products that live along it.

60.1What are neobanks and payments banks, and what can they not do?

Two creatures dominate the label "digital bank" in India, and neither is a full bank. A payments bank holds an actual, narrow licence: it can take deposits, capped at ₹2 lakh per customer, and run payments, but it cannot lend, so its money is made on floats, fees and cross-selling, which is why the model has struggled wherever payments are free. A neobank holds no banking licence at all: it is a technology and experience layer sitting on top of a partner bank that actually holds your deposit, issues the card and answers to the RBI. The practical questions follow directly. Whose balance sheet holds your money? The partner bank's, so deposit insurance applies to the bank, not the app. What happens if the app fails? Your money should survive at the partner, but your access, history and convenience may not. And who profits from you? Usually the interchange, the float and the cross-sell, so expect the app to keep suggesting products, because products are the business model.

60.2What is buy now, pay later: the product, the economics and the risks?

BNPL is the oldest product in lending, merchant credit, rebuilt for the checkout button: split ₹10,000 into three or four instalments, pay nothing extra if you pay on time. The economics run on two engines. The merchant pays 2-5% for the conversion lift, so the "free" credit is priced into the shelf, the way card rewards are. And a minority of users miss instalments, where late fees and interest, often 24-36% annualised, turn the free product into one of the most expensive forms of consumer debt per rupee borrowed. The structural risks deserve naming. Invisibility: each plan is small, so the plans stack, and a borrower can hold six plans across four apps before anyone, including the borrower, notices the total. Credit history: missed BNPL dues increasingly reach the bureaus, so a forgotten ₹800 instalment can dent a home-loan application years later. And friction removal is the product: the entire design exists to make spending feel like not spending, which is the one feature no budgeting rule survives untouched. Used once, on a planned purchase, paid on time, BNPL is free money management. Used as a lifestyle, it is a payday loan with better design.

60.3How do P2P lending platforms work, and what are the default risks?

Peer-to-peer platforms match lenders with borrowers directly, under an RBI framework that registers them as NBFC-P2Ps, caps a lender's aggregate exposure at ₹50 lakh and any single lender-borrower pair at ₹50,000, and bars the platform from guaranteeing returns. The promise: bank-beating yields, often quoted at 10-14%, by lending to borrowers banks underserve. The reality is Chapter 44's asymmetry in miniature: the interest is a stream, the default is a cliff, and your outcome depends entirely on diversification across hundreds of small loans and the platform's actual underwriting quality, which you cannot observe from the app. The arithmetic to run before lending: take the quoted gross yield, subtract expected defaults at the platform's own reported vintage rates, subtract the platform's fee and the tax at your slab, and ask whether the remainder, typically mid-to-high single digits with wide dispersion, compensates for illiquid, unsecured, unrated consumer credit. For most savers, a small, capped, experimental allocation is the honest maximum; P2P is a credit fund with none of a fund's diversification machinery.

60.4What are digital gold, digital FDs and embedded finance?

A cluster of products share one trait: they are old instruments wearing an app's clothes, and the wrapper's regulatory status varies more than the marketing suggests. Digital gold, a gram at a time in an app, sits in a regulatory gap: it is not SEBI-registered like a gold ETF, not interest-bearing like an SGB, and your gold is a private arrangement with the seller and its vaulting partner, with a 3% GST and a buy-sell spread as the quiet costs, convenient, but the weakest of the gold wrappers this website covers. Digital FDs are simply fixed deposits opened in-app, often from small finance banks paying a premium rate; the DICGC's ₹5 lakh insurance applies per bank, so the product is real and the discipline is the usual one: the extra 1% is the market's price of the bank's risk. Embedded finance is the umbrella trend: credit, insurance and investment sold inside apps whose main business is something else, the ride-hailing wallet, the shopping app's loan, the food app's credit line, efficient distribution that also means every commercial relationship you have wants to become your lender.

60.5What are account aggregators and open banking?

Underneath the apps runs a genuinely important piece of plumbing. The account aggregator framework lets you, and only you, direct your financial data, bank statements, deposits, securities, insurance, from the institution that holds it to one that needs it, through a consent artefact that specifies what, whom, and for how long, revocable at will. The AA itself cannot read or store your data; it is a courier with a sealed envelope and a consent receipt. The immediate use is lending: a small business can share verified cash flows in minutes and get a loan priced on its actual turnover rather than its collateral, the beginning of an answer to India's informal-credit problem. The wider idea is open banking: your data as an asset you carry between institutions, breaking the lock-in that kept banks lazy. The discipline it demands of users is the same as UPI's: consent screens are the new fine print, and "share all data for a year" clicked in a hurry is a disclosure you made, however frictionless it felt.

60.6Worked examples and cases

Case 1: reading a BNPL offer's true cost

The offer: ₹10,000, three monthly instalments of ₹3,333, "zero interest, zero fees". Read it in layers. If you pay on time, the cost to you is genuinely zero; the merchant's 2-5% fee to the provider is the hidden payer, already inside the price. Now the miss: one forgotten instalment attracts a late fee, say ₹500, plus interest around 2.5% a month on the overdue amount; two months of that on the remaining ₹6,667 is roughly ₹833 on a ₹10,000 purchase, over 8% for a two-month slip, an annualised rate no credit card would advertise. Then the bureau: the miss is reported, and it sits in your file for years. Finally the behavioural layer, the true product: the checkout asks "or 3 easy payments of ₹3,333?" precisely because reframing the price as a payment raises spending, the merchant's whole reason for paying that fee. The verdict is not avoidance but arithmetic: set the auto-debit, use it for planned purchases only, and treat any plan you cannot auto-pay as a loan you have not priced.

Case 2: the RBI's digital lending rules, written in ashes

Between 2019 and 2021, hundreds of instant-loan apps, many fronts for unregulated offshore operators, exploited India's lending gaps: approval in minutes against contact-list and photo access, seven-day tenors at annualised rates in the hundreds of percent, and recovery by humiliation, collectors messaging a borrower's entire contact list. Multiple suicides were linked to the harassment. The RBI's response, codified in the 2022 digital lending guidelines, rebuilt the perimeter: disbursals and repayments must run directly between the borrower and the regulated lender's account, never through the app's pool; every loan needs a standardised key fact statement showing the all-in cost, including the annual percentage rate; lending service providers must be disclosed, with the regulated entity answerable for their conduct; and data collection is confined to need, with consent. The episode's lessons outlive the apps. In lending, the borrower's protection is the lender's licence, so the first question of any loan offer is "which regulated entity is actually lending?" Speed and shame are a business model, not an underwriting standard. And regulation in fintech tends to arrive written in the ashes of the last abuse, so the absence of a rule is not evidence of safety.
