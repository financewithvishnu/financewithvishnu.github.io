Chapter 40. Choosing Funds and Building SIPs

Chapter 40 of 85 · about 20 minutes

There are over a thousand mutual fund schemes in India, and the industry would like you to believe choosing among them is a sophisticated art. It is not. It is a short list of checks that delete most of the shelf, followed by the one behaviour, buying every month without stopping, that matters more than the entire selection. This chapter gives you the checks and the behaviour, in that order of importance reversed: selection first, then the part that actually decides your wealth.

40.1How do you read past performance without being fooled?

The league table is the industry's most dangerous document. Its traps: recency, the top fund of the last year is usually the most extreme bet that happened to win, and next year's table will not feature it; survivorship, the funds that died or were merged away are absent from every long-term average, flattering the category; benchmark games, a mid-cap fund compared against the Nifty 50 in a mid-cap year looks like genius; and era dependence, a manager's decade may be one style's decade (Chapter 33's drought logic). The robust reading: rolling returns over many periods rather than one start date, performance against the right benchmark and the category median, downside behaviour in the bad years (2008, 2011, 2018, 2020), and consistency, a fund that is never first and never last for ten years is worth more than three years at the top. You are not buying the past; you are buying a process whose past merely testifies.

40.2How do you judge the fund manager, process and portfolio overlap?

Three checks beyond the numbers. The manager: tenure on this fund, style history, and what happened to their previous funds after they left, because a star's track record often does not survive the star's resignation letter. The process: can the fund house state, in one paragraph, how it picks stocks, and does the actual portfolio match the claim? A "quality growth" fund holding momentum darlings is a style drifter, and drift is how yesterday's reason for buying becomes tomorrow's surprise. Overlap: owning three large-cap funds with 60% identical portfolios is not diversification; it is paying three fees for one portfolio. Compare the top holdings before adding any fund; your portfolio should hold meaningfully different things or meaningfully fewer funds.

40.3How does a SIP work, and why does it suit human behaviour?

The SIP buys a fixed rupee amount every month, so it automatically buys more units when prices fall and fewer when they rise, rupee-cost averaging, a gentle mathematical tailwind. But the SIP's real magic is not mathematical; it is behavioural. It converts the hardest decision in investing, "is now the time?", into no decision at all; it arrives before you can spend the money; and it keeps you buying through the crashes, which is when the average is genuinely pulled down and the future returns are genuinely made. The data on Indian SIPs bears the behavioural reading out: the investors who stopped SIPs in the 2020 crash locked in the worst prices, while those who continued were made whole within months. A SIP is not a product. It is a pre-commitment device, and its return is measured in the disasters it prevents you from causing yourself.

40.4What are systematic withdrawal plans and systematic transfer plans?

The mirror machines for the other phases of life. A systematic withdrawal plan (SWP) pays you a fixed amount monthly from a fund, the retirement engine: it beats the dividend option for most because you control the amount and the timing, and each withdrawal is partly return of capital, which changes the tax character favourably in equity funds. A systematic transfer plan (STP) moves money gradually from a debt fund into an equity fund, the answer to the lump-sum problem: a large amount arriving at once, a bonus, a property sale, belongs partly in the market immediately (lump sums usually win, because markets usually rise) and partly phased in over six to twelve months for the investor who cannot stomach investing everything the week before a crash. The STP is honest psychology: it buys you peace at a small expected cost, and peace that keeps you invested is the highest-returning luxury in finance.

40.5How do you build a fund portfolio with core and satellite?

The architecture that ends most fund-selection debates. The core, 70-90% of the equity allocation, is one to three cheap, broad, boring funds: a Nifty 50 or Nifty 500 index fund, perhaps a flexi-cap with a decade-long process, holding the market's compounding with minimal decisions. The satellites, 10-30%, are the expressions of conviction: a mid or small-cap fund, a sector bet, a factor fund, an international fund, each sized so its total failure scars but does not cripple, and each held to the Chapter 39 review standard. The design's virtues are all behavioural: the core is too boring to abandon, the satellites are small enough to forgive, and the whole structure answers the eternal "should I sell?" with "which sleeve, and what does its review say?". Complexity beyond this is decoration, usually purchased at the fee counter.

40.6Worked examples and cases

Case 1: a three-fund portfolio for a 30-year goal

A 32-year-old investing for a goal thirty years out, illustrative: 60% in a Nifty 500 or flexi-cap core, 25% in a mid-and-small-cap satellite, 15% in an international (US index) satellite, rebalanced once a year back to the weights. Total cost under 0.6%, total maintenance one hour a year. Over three decades this portfolio absorbs everything: crashes, which the SIP converts into cheap units; fads, which the core ignores and the satellites can join in sized doses; and its owner’s own emotions, which the annual one-hour review replaces with a rule. The expected outcome is not brilliance; it is the market's full compounding, minus very little, captured by the one method that has never required being clever.

Case 2: what the missed-best-days studies actually show

The famous finding, in its honest form (illustrative figures of the true shape): ₹100 stayed invested through a volatile decade grows to about ₹275. Remove just the ten best days, out of roughly 2,500, and the same money grows to about ₹172. Remove the thirty best days and you are left with ₹135, barely ahead of inflation.

Two readings, and both are true. The bullish reading: the best days cluster around the worst days, half of them arrive within weeks of the crashes, so market timing must dodge the falls and catch the rebounds, a double trick nobody performs reliably. The sceptic's reading: missing the worst days helps just as much, which is true and useless, because the worst days are equally unpredictable. The operative conclusion stands: the market's return is delivered in unpredictable bursts, and the only way to be present for them is to be present. The SIP investor is present by construction.
