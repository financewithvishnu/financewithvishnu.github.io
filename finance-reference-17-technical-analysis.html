Chapter 17. Technical Analysis and Market Behaviour

Chapter 17 of 85 · about 21 minutes

Somewhere right now a trader is buying a stock because two lines crossed on a chart, and a professor is proving the lines carry no information. They are both partly right, and the space between them is where this chapter lives. You will learn what technical analysis claims, which claims survive evidence, and the honest summary forty years of watching Indian markets produces: charts describe behaviour, and behaviour has habits; but a habit is not a law, and a line is not a reason.

17.1What does technical analysis assume about price and volume?

Technical analysis rests on three claims. Prices discount everything: all known information is already in the price. Prices move in trends: motion persists more than a coin-flip would allow. And history repeats: the patterns of crowd fear and greed recur because crowds do. The first claim is roughly true for liquid large-caps and laughably false for thin small-caps where the float is controlled. The second has real statistical support, momentum is one of the most documented effects in all of finance. The third is true in spirit and treacherous in practice, because a pattern you can see after the fact and a pattern you can trade before it are different animals. Volume is the chart's cross-examination: a price move on heavy volume is a crowd's conviction; the same move on thin volume is a rumour with legs.

17.2What are trends, support and resistance?

A trend is a series of higher highs and higher lows (up) or the reverse (down), nothing more mystical. Support is a price zone where declines have repeatedly found buyers; resistance where rallies have repeatedly met sellers. The first-principles reason these exist at all: memory. Investors anchored to past prices act at them: the buyer who missed ₹800 buys the return to ₹800; the holder trapped at ₹1,000 sells the return to ₹1,000 to "get out even". Zones where many such decisions cluster do temporarily attract or repel price. They break exactly when the information changes, which is why support and resistance are descriptions of where battles happened, not predictions of who wins the next one.

17.3How do moving averages and momentum indicators work?

A moving average smooths price over N days to show the trend without the noise; a shorter average crossing above a longer one is the classic trend-change signal.

The honest arithmetic of that signal: in a trending market it keeps you on the right side of the big move, and in a sideways market it whipsaws you in and out for small losses, paying for its wins with its churn. RSI, the relative strength index, measures the speed of recent gains versus losses on a 0-100 scale; above 70 reads "overbought", below 30 "oversold", and in a strong trend it can sit overbought for weeks while the price doubles. MACD compares two exponential averages to catch momentum shifts earlier, at the price of more false alarms. Every indicator is a transformation of price and volume; none contains information that was not already there. What they offer is discipline, a rule instead of a mood, and that is genuinely worth something to the humans who lack one.

17.4Do chart patterns actually work?

Head and shoulders, triangles, flags, cup with handle: the pattern literature is a century of confident geometry. The academic verdict is unkind to most of it. When researchers test named patterns mechanically, the famous ones carry little reliable predictive power beyond what momentum and trend already explain, and human pattern-spotting adds a further problem: we are pattern-finding machines that find them in noise, which is why two technicians can look at one chart and see opposite futures. The defensible residue: trend and momentum effects are real across decades and countries, including India; volume confirms or denies; and very short-term price behaviour in liquid contracts has exploitable structure for those with speed, cost advantages and discipline, which describes almost no retail traders. Read charts as a record of what the crowd has done and where its memory lives, not as tea leaves.

17.5How should traders size positions and place stop-losses?

The part of technical trading that unambiguously works is not prediction, it is survival arithmetic. The rules that keep traders in the game: risk a small fixed fraction of capital per trade, one or two percent, so ten consecutive losses, which will happen, cost a fifth of the account rather than all of it; place stops where the trade idea is actually wrong, not where the loss feels comfortable; and size the position from the stop distance, not from enthusiasm: wider stop, smaller position, same rupees at risk. The mathematics is unforgiving and worth memorising: a 50% loss needs a 100% gain to recover; a 90% loss needs 900%. Investors who never trade still benefit from this section, because its logic, cap the damage of being wrong before you count the winnings of being right, is portfolio design in miniature.

17.6Worked examples and cases

Case 1: a moving-average system on Nifty history

Take the simplest mechanical rule: hold the Nifty when its 50-day average sits above its 200-day, sit in cash otherwise. Run across long Indian history, the system's shape is consistent with what such studies find everywhere: it captures most of the big bull markets, sidesteps the deepest crashes, 2008 and the 2020 Covid collapse included, and gives back a steady drip of small losses in range-bound years. Net of realistic costs and taxes, it roughly matches buy-and-hold returns with meaningfully smaller drawdowns, which for many investors is a better trade than it sounds. The lesson is not that the cross is magic; it is that a mediocre signal plus unbreakable discipline beats a good signal abandoned at the first losing streak. The system is the discipline, automated.

Case 2: where technical analysis helps, and where it fails

Helps: timing entries and exits in positions you chose fundamentally; reading crowd extremes at market turning points; enforcing exit discipline on trades; sizing risk. Fails: predicting news; valuing businesses; thin small-caps, where the chart is drawn by whoever controls the float and you are the audience; and anywhere the user believes a pattern removes uncertainty rather than repricing it. The dividing line is information. Where genuine information moves prices, liquid large-caps and indices, charts describe a real crowd. Where the float is controlled, the chart is marketing. Technical analysis is a lens on behaviour; pointed at a manipulated price, it shows you a beautiful picture of the trap you are about to walk into.
