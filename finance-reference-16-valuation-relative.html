Chapter 16. Valuation II: Relative and Asset-Based Methods

Chapter 16 of 85 · about 22 minutes

Nobody prices a flat by discounting its future rents; they ask what the flat next door sold for, and adjust. Relative valuation is the market's favourite shortcut for the same reason: fast, intuitive, and wrong in ways that are entirely predictable. This chapter teaches the shortcut properly: which multiple fits which business, what each one silently assumes, and how to know when "cheap versus peers" means a bargain and when it means a warning the market has already read.

16.1What is the P/E ratio, and which variant should you use?

The price-to-earnings ratio asks one question: how many rupees do you pay for one rupee of annual profit? Trailing P/E uses the last twelve months: factual, but backward-looking, and distorted by one-off quarters. Forward P/E uses next year's forecast: relevant, but built on an analyst's hope. Cyclically adjusted P/E, averaging earnings across a decade, fixes the specific sin of valuing a cyclical on peak earnings, the Chapter 11 trap where the lowest P/E marks the top. Every P/E also inherits the accounting: a P/E of 12 on manufactured earnings is expensive at any number. And a P/E compares equity value to equity profit, ignoring debt completely, which is exactly the hole the next section's multiple patches.

16.2When should you use EV/EBITDA, price-to-sales or price-to-book?

  • EV/EBITDA: enterprise value (market cap + debt - cash) over operating earnings before interest, tax, depreciation. Capital-structure-neutral, so it compares a leveraged company with an unleveraged one honestly. Its blind spot: depreciation is a real economic cost in heavy industry, so EBITDA flatters capital-hungry businesses. Best for comparing industrial peers.
  • P/S, price to sales: the multiple of last resort, for companies with no earnings yet: young tech, turnaround stories. It values the hope, not the business, and a P/S of 10 says "grow fast and fat or fall". Use it only when the path from revenue to profit is visible and mapped.
  • P/B, price to book: price over net asset value per share. Meaningful where the balance sheet is the business: banks, NBFCs, insurers, where assets are financial and marked near reality. Meaningless where value lives in intangibles the balance sheet never booked: brands, networks, code. A consumer franchise at 12x book can be cheap; a lender at 12x book is a bubble symptom.

The discipline: the multiple must match where the value lives, earnings, assets, or revenue-on-the-way-to-earnings. Choosing the multiple to fit the story you want is the most common valuation error in Indian research, sell-side and self-side alike.

16.3What is the PEG ratio, and how does it adjust valuation for growth?

The PEG ratio, P/E divided by the earnings growth rate, promises to make expensive stocks comparable: a 40 P/E growing 40% has a PEG of 1, as does a 12 P/E growing 12%. It is a useful thumb rule and a dangerous formula. Growth compounds for a few years, not forever, so dividing by this year's growth rate prices a temporary state as a permanent one; and PEG treats all growth as equal, whether earned at 25% ROCE or bought with dilution at 8%. Use PEG as a second screen, never a verdict: below 1 invites a look, above 2 invites scepticism, and the look and the scepticism are both done with Chapters 9-11, not with more ratios.

16.4What is a company worth from its assets: liquidation, replacement and sum-of-the-parts value?

Sometimes the right question is not "what will it earn?" but "what is it made of?". Liquidation value, assets sold piecemeal minus liabilities, prices the floor: relevant for distressed companies and for the cigar-butt style of deep value. Replacement cost asks what a competitor would spend to rebuild the capacity: when market value sits far below replacement cost, new supply stops arriving, which is how commodity cycles bottom. Sum-of-the-parts values each business separately and adds: the standard tool for India's conglomerates, where one listed company may hold a bank, a cement plant and three listed subsidiaries. SOTP almost always produces a number above the market price, the famous holding-company discount, and the analyst's real work is deciding how much of that discount is bad capital allocation, how much is tax leakage, and how much is simply permanent.

16.5How do you value banks, NBFCs and insurers differently?

Financial firms break the standard toolkit: debt is their raw material, not their capital structure, so EV/EBITDA and debt-to-equity say nothing. The working model is the justified P/B: a lender earning an ROE above its cost of equity deserves a price above book; earning below it, below book. The logic is pure first principles: book value is the capital; ROE is what the capital earns; the multiple is the market's price for that spread, sustained and growing. The full sector treatment is Chapter 19; the valuation identity belongs here because it is the cleanest example in all of finance of a multiple with an actual formula behind it rather than a habit.

16.6Worked examples and cases

Case 1: an Indian bank by P/B and ROE

The justified P/B formula, from the dividend discount model: P/B = (ROE - g) / (r - g), where g is sustainable growth and r the cost of equity. Take a quality private bank, composite but typical: ROE 17%, growth 10%, cost of equity 13%. Justified P/B = (0.17 - 0.10) / (0.13 - 0.10) = 2.3x. Now a recovering PSU lender: ROE 10%, growth 7%, cost of equity 14% for its weaker book and governance history. Justified P/B = (0.10 - 0.07) / (0.14 - 0.07) = 0.4x.

The market's habitual prices, around 2-2.5x for the first and 0.5-0.9x for the second in recent years, are this formula wearing clothes. And the formula tells you what would change the price: not hope, but a sustained two-point change in ROE, which on a leveraged balance sheet is an enormous operational fact. When someone calls a PSU bank "cheap at 0.7x book", they are forecasting ROE; make them say so.

Case 2: a sum-of-the-parts on an Indian conglomerate

Composite, numbers illustrative: a holding company whose listed subsidiaries, at market prices, are worth ₹80,000 crore for its stakes, whose unlisted businesses earn ₹2,000 crore and might deserve ₹24,000 crore, minus ₹10,000 crore of holding-level debt: SOTP says ₹94,000 crore against a market cap of ₹56,000 crore, a 40% discount. Is it cheap? The decomposition: perhaps 10 points are structural (tax on any actual sale or spin-off), 10 are capital allocation history (cash has gone into the weakest businesses), and 20 are the market's standing scepticism that the discount ever closes. The catalyst question decides the investment: a demerger, a listing, a buyback at the holdco level turns "cheap" into "closing"; without one, a discount can outlive your patience, as decades of Indian holding companies have proven in both directions.
