Chapter 14. Fundamental Analysis: Top-Down and Bottom-Up

Chapter 14 of 85 · about 22 minutes

Every stock tip you will ever receive skips the same four questions: How does this business make money? Is it good at it? Will it stay good? And what am I paying for that goodness? Fundamental analysis is simply the discipline of answering those questions in order, with documents instead of opinions. This chapter assembles Chapters 8 to 13 into one working method, the method every serious investor you admire actually uses, whether they call it top-down, bottom-up, or just doing the work.

14.1What is top-down analysis: economy, industry, company?

Top-down analysis funnels from the whole economy to the industry to the individual company: judge the macro tailwind first, then the industry’s structure, then the specific business inside it.

Economy
growth, rates, inflation, currency
Industry
structure, cycle position, regulation
Company
unit economics, moat, management
Price
what you pay vs what it is worth

Top-down: each layer filters the next. Bottom-up walks the same path in reverse, starting at the company.

Top-down starts with the tide. India's growth, interest rates, inflation and currency set the weather for every business beneath them: rate cycles move banks and real estate, the rupee moves exporters and importers in opposite directions, government capital expenditure moves entire capital-goods chains. The middle layer is industry structure, Chapter 11's five forces plus the industry's position in its own cycle. Only then do you select companies. The strength of top-down is that it aligns you with forces larger than any management team; the weakness is that the macro view is the most crowded, most forecast-dependent, most confidently wrong layer of all. Use it to choose ponds, not to predict rain.

14.2What is bottom-up stock picking?

Bottom-up inverts the funnel: find companies so good that the macro becomes a detail. The creed has two clauses and the order matters. Quality first: a business with honest accounts, a real moat, rational capital allocation and a long runway can be held through almost any weather, because its value compounds internally whether or not the market cooperates. Price second: quality is necessary and not sufficient, because a wonderful business at a silly price is a silly investment; Chapters 15 and 16 exist to keep this clause honest. The practical difference between the two styles: top-down asks "what should I own this year?", bottom-up asks "what can I own for ten?". Indian retail experience argues for spending most of your energy on the second question.

14.3What is the difference between growth and value investing, and which has won?

The oldest argument in markets. Value buys statistically cheap stocks, low P/E, low P/B, high yield, trusting pessimism to mean-revert; the academic evidence across a century of US data and decades of Indian data gives cheap stocks a long-run edge, with long, painful stretches where it does not show up. Growth buys rapid earnings expansion and accepts high multiples, trusting the expansion to outrun the price; it dominates in falling-rate, liquidity-rich regimes, as Indian investors learned in 2017 and 2020-21, and gives it back when the regime turns, as they learned in the small-cap corrections that followed each episode. The honest synthesis most professionals land on: growth and value are joined at the hip, because growth is a component of value, not its opposite. The real axis is price versus worth. Buy worth at a discount; let other people argue about the label.

14.4How do you forecast a company’s earnings honestly?

A forecast is not a number, it is a list of assumptions wearing a number's clothes. Build it from the business's actual drivers, and keep each driver checkable. For most companies the decomposition is short: volume (how many units, growing with the industry plus share gains), price (realisation per unit, growing with pricing power and mix), margin (the spread between them, moving with costs and operating leverage), and capital needs (what growth consumes in working capital and capex). Honesty enters through three habits. Forecast ranges, not points: a base case, a good case, a bad case, each with its own logic. Anchor every driver to something observable: capacity, industry volume data, management's own past accuracy. And keep the score: write the forecast down, revisit it against results, and let your calibration improve in public with yourself. An investor with a written, falsifiable, frequently wrong forecast improves; one with a vibe merely rotates.

14.5What does a research checklist from screener to conviction look like?

Screen the universe for quality and price, read three to five annual reports, run the forensic checks, value the business, and finish with a one-page thesis that names what would prove it wrong.

#StepToolOutput
1Screen the universeScreener filters: ROCE, debt, CFO/profit, growth20-40 candidates
2Read the businessAnnual report: business description, segment dataOne-paragraph explanation of how it makes money
3Run the health checkChapter 9's ten ratios, five-year trendsQuality verdict
4Run the forensic checkChapter 10's eight flagsClean pass or a written question
5Judge the moat and managementChapter 11: moat test, capital allocation recordDurability verdict
6Write the earnings driversVolume, price, margin, capital needsThree honest scenarios
7Value itChapters 15-16A range, not a point
8Write the one-page thesisPen and paperWhy, what kills it, what it is worth

Notice how late valuation arrives. Conviction is built from the business outward, and price is the last gate, not the first filter, because a cheap screen that skips steps 2 to 5 is how investors collect Peer B from Chapter 9's table. The one-page thesis at the end is not ceremony: if you cannot write why you own it, what would prove you wrong, and what it is worth, on one page, in ink, you do not own a thesis, you own a ticker.

14.6Worked example: the one-page thesis, built from public documents

Case: a listed Indian pipes maker, assembled in one evening

Take a mid-sized listed plastic-pipes manufacturer, composite of several real companies, numbers illustrative. The screen caught it: ROCE above 20% for five years, negligible debt, CFO tracking profit. The business read: it sells pipes through thousands of dealers, brand-led, adding capacity and adjacent products; the unit economics are resin price plus a brand premium. The moat verdict: brand plus distribution scale, confirmed by stable margins across a resin cycle. The forensic pass: clean auditor, no pledges, related-party note thin. The drivers: industry volume growing with housing and agriculture, share gains from unorganised players post-GST, margins bounded by resin, capex funded internally. The three scenarios: base case 12-15% earnings growth, good case 18% with adjacencies working, bad case a resin spike and a weak monsoon compressing a year to flat. The valuation: the price asks for the good case, so the thesis ends, unusually and instructively, in watchlist, with a written trigger price at which the base case would be enough. The exercise's real product is not the verdict; it is that every future news item about this company now lands on a prepared mind.
