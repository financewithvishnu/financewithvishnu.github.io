Chapter 78. Retirement, Estate and Legacy Planning

Chapter 78 of 85 · about 20 minutes

Retirement is not an age but a number: the corpus at which work becomes optional. This chapter builds that number, stress-tests it against the sequence of returns, and then does the paperwork nobody enjoys, nominations, wills, trusts, so the wealth outlives the plan and reaches the right hands.

78.1How much money do you need to retire: the 25x rule?

The number comes from three inputs. First, annual spending in retirement, estimated from today's spending with the components that vanish removed, EMIs, commutes, children's costs, and the ones that grow added, healthcare, help, travel. Second, inflation to the retirement date: at 6% a rupee of spending doubles every twelve years, so the 35-year-old's ₹60,000 a month is ₹2.6 lakh a month at sixty, same life, bigger number. Third, the withdrawal rate: the 25x rule inverts the 4% guideline, meaning the target corpus is twenty-five times annual spending at retirement. The number is rude the first time anyone computes it, routinely eight to ten crore for a middle-class urban household retiring at sixty, and it is also clarifying: it converts a fog of anxiety into a savings-rate problem with a known solution. Recompute it every few years; the inputs move, and so does the number.

78.2What are EPF, PPF, NPS and their 401k and IRA cousins?

Every tax system builds the same machine with different labels: a wrapper that defers or exempts tax in exchange for locking money until retirement. India's trio: EPF, the salaried default, employer-matched, currently tax-favoured within limits, decent debt-like return, mediocre flexibility. PPF, the sovereign fifteen-year classic, EEE, the best risk-free compounding vehicle a resident can buy, capped at ₹1.5 lakh a year, a floor not a plan. NPS, the market-linked pension with the extra ₹50,000 deduction, real equity exposure, and the mandatory annuity at exit that caps its charm. The American cousins, 401k and IRA, share the architecture, pre-tax in, taxed out, with the Roth variants reversing the order. The principles transfer across jurisdictions: capture every employer match first, it is the only guaranteed 100% return; fill the tax-advantaged space before taxable investing; and treat each wrapper by its asset, equity wrappers can hold equity, not by its label.

78.3What is a safe withdrawal rate, and what is sequence-of-returns risk?

Accumulation forgives timing; decumulation does not. Sequence-of-returns risk is the reason: two retirees with identical average returns and identical withdrawal rates end differently if one meets the crash in year one, withdrawals from a falling portfolio amputate the base that recovery compounds on. The 4% rule was born as an answer: across US history, 4% of the starting corpus, inflation-adjusted annually, survived every thirty-year window. For a forty-year Indian retirement, with different market history and higher inflation volatility, prudence argues for 3.25 to 3.5%, and for the guardrails: spend less after bad years, more after good ones. The defences are structural, the cash bucket, two to three years of spending in liquid assets so no equity is sold into a crash, and the glide path, equity stepped down into retirement and, in the newer evidence, stepped gently back up after it. The retirement that fails rarely fails in the averages; it fails in the first five years, which deserve their own plan.

78.4What do wills, nominations and trusts do, and why the paperwork matters?

India's estate law contains a trap that surprises every family it catches: a nominee is a custodian, not an heir. The nominee collects the asset, bank account, MF folio, insurance proceeds, and holds it for the legal heirs, who are decided by the will or, absent one, by succession law, which may distribute the estate differently from every intention the deceased ever expressed. The fixes are unglamorous and cheap. Nominations on everything, and kept current through marriages, births and deaths. A will, written, signed, witnessed, registered optionally, reviewed on every life event; the family that needs it will be grieving, and the document is a kindness written in advance. Trusts for the cases wills handle poorly: minor children, spendthrift heirs, special-needs dependents, staggered inheritance, and the letter of instruction, the informal map of accounts, policies, lockers and passwords that saves the family the archeology. Estate planning is not for the rich; it is for anyone whose death should not also be an administrative emergency.

78.5What do withdrawal rates look like in one picture?

Decumulation is the phase where the earlier chapters must all work at once: the asset allocation of Chapter 75 funds the bucket strategy, the behaviour of Chapter 77 resists the crash-year panic that sequence risk weaponises, and the withdrawal rate sets the pace the corpus can actually sustain.

78.6Worked examples and cases

Case 1: the 35-year-old's plan, stress-tested

Spends ₹60,000 a month at thirty-five, wants retirement at sixty. Inflation at 6% makes that ₹2.58 lakh a month at sixty, ₹31 lakh a year, and 25x prices the corpus at ₹7.75 crore. The SIP that builds it: twenty-five years at an assumed 11% nominal requires about ₹49,000 a month, before step-ups; start at ₹35,000 and escalate 8% annually with increments and the same goal is reachable with a gentler beginning. Now stress it. Inflation at 7% instead of 6% moves the corpus to ₹10.3 crore, a third higher, so the plan must hold an inflation margin, which in practice means more equity for longer. Returns at 9% instead of 11% push the flat SIP past ₹70,000, the step-up version absorbs this better, which is the quiet argument for step-ups over flat commitments. Retirement at fifty-five instead of sixty removes five compounding years and adds five withdrawal years, roughly a 60% larger problem; early retirement is bought with savings rate, not with cleverness. The exercise's product is not the number but the sensitivity map: which assumption hurts most, and which lever, savings rate, is the one entirely in hand.

Case 2: the family that found no nominations

A salaried man dies at forty-nine, suddenly, leaving a spouse, two school-age children, ₹85 lakh across two banks, three MF folios, EPF, a flat, and term insurance. He was organised by temperament, spreadsheets everywhere, and had done none of the estate paperwork: no will, and the nominations frozen in time, his mother, deceased six years, on the bank accounts; his brother, estranged, on the oldest folio. The term insurance paid cleanly, nomination and insurer both current, proof that the machinery works when fed. The rest took twenty-nine months: legal-heir certificates, succession paperwork, a court process for the flat, and a family negotiation with the estranged brother that money did not improve. Total friction cost, fees, travel, lost interest, lapsed opportunities, is estimated near ₹6 lakh, but the true cost was the bereaved spending their worst year doing archeology. The repairs are a weekend's work: nominations refreshed on every account, a registered will naming guardians and executors, a letter of instruction with the account map, and a calendar reminder to review all of it every three years. The documents cost almost nothing; their absence costs the family everything except money, and some of that too.
