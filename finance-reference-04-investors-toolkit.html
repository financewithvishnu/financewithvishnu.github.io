Chapter 4. The Investor's Toolkit: Accounts and Market Plumbing

Chapter 4 of 85 · about 15 minutes

You tap Buy on your phone at 9:20 in the morning and 100 shares appear in your account. It feels like an app trick. It is actually one of the most reliable machines ever built: five institutions, some handling lakhs of crores a day, handing your order to each other in sequence so that a trade with a total stranger settles without you ever knowing their name. This chapter walks the machine. When you finish, you will know exactly what you own, where it actually sits, and what happens to it if your broker disappears tomorrow.

4.1Demat, trading and bank accounts: who holds what

Investing in India runs on three linked accounts, and the confusion between them is worth killing early:

  • Demat account: holds your securities in electronic form, shares, bonds, ETFs, with a depository (NSDL or CDSL). Think of it as the godown where your certificates would once have sat.
  • Trading account: the order window, usually inside your broker's app, where you place buys and sells. It holds nothing.
  • Bank account: holds the money. Funds move from here when you buy and return here when you sell.

One transaction touches all three: money leaves the bank account, the order goes through the trading account, the shares land in the demat account. Your broker is the courier between them, never the owner of any of it.

4.2Brokers, depositories, exchanges and clearing corporations

Four institutions stand between you and a stranger on the other side of every trade:

  • The broker (Zerodha, Groww, ICICI Direct and the like): your licensed doorway to the exchange. It routes orders, collects margins and answers to SEBI.
  • The exchange (NSE, BSE): the auction room, an electronic order book where every buy and sell bid meets openly and the best price wins.
  • The clearing corporation (NSE Clearing, ICCL): after the match, it inserts itself between the two parties and guarantees both sides, becoming the buyer to every seller and the seller to every buyer. This is why you never worry about who sold to you.
  • The depository (NSDL, CDSL): the registry that actually records who owns which securities, like a land records office for shares.

4.3How a trade actually settles: T+1 and the journey of one order

You tap Buy
100 shares on your broker's app, 9:20 am
Broker routes it
order reaches the NSE order book
Exchange matches it
your buy meets someone's sell, trade done
Clearing corporation steps in
becomes buyer to the seller and seller to you
Settlement on T+1
shares reach your demat, money reaches the seller, next working day

The 24-hour journey of one ordinary Indian trade.

India moved most of its market to T+1 settlement, shares and money exchanged one working day after the trade, in January 2023, one of the fastest settlement cycles in the world, and it has been piloting same-day T+0 settlement on a growing list of stocks. The day between trade and settlement is not idle: it is when the clearing corporation collects money and shares from thousands of brokers and nets lakhs of obligations into a small number of movements. That netting is why the whole system does not need to move lakhs of crores to settle lakhs of crores.

4.4Custodians, registrars and transfer agents

Behind the retail machine sit the institutions' institutions. Custodians hold the securities of large investors, FIIs, mutual funds, insurance companies, and handle their settlements and corporate actions. Registrars and transfer agents (RTAs) such as CAMS and KFin maintain the investor records for mutual funds and companies: your folio, your nomination, your address. When your dividend arrives without asking, an RTA did the paperwork.

4.5The global mirror: DTC, DTCC and how the US settles

Every serious market runs the same machine with local labels. The United States settles through the DTCC group: its depository, DTC, holds the securities and its clearing arm nets the trades, and the US moved to T+1 in May 2024, after India. The lesson generalises: wherever you invest, ask the same four questions: who matches the trade, who guarantees it, who records ownership, and how many days until it is final.

4.6Worked examples and cases

Case 1: tracing one NSE buy order end to end

9:20 am, Monday: you buy 100 shares of a company at ₹1,000. Your broker checks you have ₹1,00,000 plus charges and routes the order. The NSE order book shows a seller at ₹1,000; matched. By evening the broker's contract note lands: price, brokerage, STT, exchange charges, GST, stamp duty, the real all-in cost. Tuesday, the clearing corporation delivers 100 shares to your demat account and your ₹1,00,000, minus charges, to the seller's broker. Total institutions touched: five. Total strangers trusted by name: zero.

Case 2: what happens if your broker fails

Your shares do not sit with the broker; they sit at the depository in your demat account, and your uninvested money mostly sits in the broker's client bank accounts, segregated from its own. So a broker's failure is an inconvenience, not a wipeout: the depository records still name you. If a broker is found to have misused client securities or funds, the exchange's investor protection fund compensates genuine claims up to a notified limit, and SEBI's SCORES platform is where complaints are filed and tracked.

Contrast with the bank from Chapter 1: deposits there are the bank's IOU, protected by DICGC only up to ₹5 lakh. Demat holdings are not an IOU at all; they are property in your name. Same app screen, completely different legal footing, which is why the plumbing was worth a chapter.
