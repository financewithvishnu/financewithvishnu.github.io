Aluminium and copper: the metal is not the business model

By Finance With Vishnu · Source period: March 2024

Higher metal demand is an industry statement. It does not tell us which company earns the money. An aluminium smelter, a rolling mill and a recycler can all serve the same end market while depending on different prices, costs and amounts of capital.

The distinction is equally important in copper. Owning an ore body is different from processing someone else's concentrate. Making a conductor or cable is another business again. A rising copper price can help one stage while putting pressure on another.

I would begin by drawing the physical chain and then drawing the money chain beside it. Where does the company buy, where does it sell, what price can it pass through, and what must it fund in between?

1. Where in the chain does the company operate?

In primary aluminium, the simplified chain runs from bauxite to alumina, then to smelted aluminium and downstream products. Recycling supplies a different route back into metal and product manufacture. Do not confuse tonnes of ore, alumina, liquid metal and finished sheet.

Hindalco's FY2025 manufactured-capital chapter describes an integrated aluminium chain and a separate custom copper-smelting business. Integration can place several economic engines inside one listed company. It does not make them identical. [1]

Before comparing margins, decide whether the accounts describe a mine, smelter, converter or consolidated group. Otherwise a low percentage margin in a metal-pass-through business can be mistaken for weak value creation, or a high upstream margin for permanent quality.

2. Why is electricity central to primary aluminium?

Smelting turns alumina into aluminium using electrolysis. Power availability, price and operating reliability therefore belong near the start of the cost analysis, alongside alumina, carbon inputs and other operating costs.

The International Aluminium Institute's smelting-energy series measures electricity for electrolysis and defines what is inside that boundary. It excludes activities such as casting and carbon plants. That is not the same as a mine-to-finished-product energy measure or a complete emissions footprint. [2]

This is a useful discipline whenever a company claims a cost or environmental advantage. Are we comparing the same process, product, geography and period? Does the number include purchased power, captive generation, upstream inputs and downstream processing?

Captive coal or bauxite can change supply security and the cost curve. Captive does not mean free. Mining costs, permissions, royalties, transport, rehabilitation, plant investment and sustaining capital still matter. A captive resource only helps shareholders if its full economics are attractive.

I would test an energy-cost increase and an operating interruption separately. One changes the unit cost; the other can also reduce saleable output and fixed-cost absorption.

3. Is an integrated producer one simple aluminium bet?

No. Separate bauxite and alumina, primary metal, downstream products and any overseas subsidiary. Then add copper or other businesses where relevant. Each segment needs its own volume, price, cost, capital and cash bridge.

Hindalco's report lists refinery, smelting, rolling and other capacities separately. Those tonnes are not additive measures of finished output. Material can pass through several stages, so adding every installed-capacity number can count the same physical stream more than once. [1]

A consolidated revenue share is also not an earnings share. A large metal-pass-through business can dominate sales without contributing the same proportion of profit or invested capital.

For research, I would reconcile each segment's earnings to the consolidated number, including eliminations and corporate costs. If a chart does not reconcile, leave the gap visible instead of forcing percentages into an attractive pie.

Integration deserves a return-on-capital test. If an upstream investment lifts operating profit but requires a much larger capital base, the improvement in margin alone cannot establish that value has been created.

4. What does a conversion model really protect?

Novelis's FY2025 filing describes a significant amount of business under a conversion model. Nearly all its flat-rolled products use a base aluminium price, a local market premium and a conversion premium. The filing defines the conversion premium as conversion costs plus a margin, not as pure operating profit. [3]

That structure can pass through changes in metal prices. But pass-through is not an all-purpose shield. Contract resets, metal-price timing, product mix, scrap sourcing, energy, labour, logistics and plant performance can still affect results.

The example deliberately leaves tax, depreciation, financing and other items outside the calculation. Its purpose is to show why more revenue from a higher metal price is not automatically better conversion profitability.

The opposite is also possible: lower metal prices can reduce reported sales while shipment volumes and conversion earnings improve. Read the volume-and-spread bridge before judging the headline growth rate.

5. Does recycling guarantee a wider margin?

Recycling and primary production have different energy requirements. The International Aluminium Institute's facts material explains the energy advantage of recycling. That physical advantage does not establish what a particular recycler earns after buying scrap, sorting it, processing it and selling qualified output. [4]

Scrap is a purchased input with competing buyers. Its availability, alloy, contamination, collection cost and recoverable yield matter. A greater recycled-content percentage is not a complete profit forecast.

Novelis's FY2025 filing discusses the risk that competition for recycled material raises scrap prices. Its FY2025 results also identify higher scrap costs among the reasons for weaker adjusted EBITDA. These are company-reported historical examples of why recycling benefits need a spread calculation. [3, 5]

I would ask which scrap types a plant can actually use and at what yield. Can technology expand the input basket without losing the product specification? Does a customer's recycled-content requirement increase willingness to pay, or does it mainly increase competition for the same feedstock?

Recyclable is also not the same as collected and recycled. Material properties, collection systems and commercial economics are different parts of the story.

6. Why can profit improve while cash weakens?

Even when metal is passed through, it must be funded while it sits in inventory or receivables. A higher metal price can increase the cash tied up in the same physical volume.

Metal-price lag can also affect reported results when purchase and sale pricing do not line up in time. Derivatives and accounting treatment add another reconciliation. Do not combine operating conversion earnings, timing effects and unrealised gains into one supposedly repeatable margin.

Novelis's FY2025 results explain net-income changes using metal-price lag and derivatives, while separately discussing adjusted EBITDA and operating cash flow. They report capital spending and free cash flow as further, different measures. [5]

The question is not whether an adjustment is automatically good or bad. It is whether it is consistently defined, reconciled and useful for estimating cash through a cycle. Expansion capital can make free cash flow negative even when the existing plants remain profitable.

7. How is a copper miner different from a smelter?

A miner extracts ore and produces a saleable material, often concentrate after beneficiation. A custom smelter processes purchased concentrate into metal. A downstream converter makes another product from metal. They do not all earn the copper price in the same way.

Hindustan Copper's company profile describes a present focus on copper-ore mining and concentrate sales. Hindalco's manufactured-capital chapter describes its custom-smelting and downstream copper operations. Those descriptions identify different business models; they are not a relative stock recommendation. [6, 1]

For a mine, ore tonnes are not contained copper tonnes. A useful first calculation is ore processed multiplied by copper grade and recovery. The resulting metal measure still needs a bridge to payable metal, sales terms, charges and actual cash.

For example, 1 million tonnes of ore at 1% copper grade contains 10,000 tonnes of copper before recovery. At an assumed 90% recovery, 9,000 tonnes is recovered. Those are original assumptions, not figures for an actual deposit.

Grade, dilution, recovery, stripping or underground development, logistics and sustaining capital can offset a favourable metal price. A large resource statement is not a near-term sales forecast.

8. Can tight copper supply hurt a copper processor?

Yes. Scarcity of concentrate can improve the bargaining position of miners while reducing the treatment and refining charges available to custom smelters. A broad copper-demand story does not tell us how the processing spread moves.

Treatment and refining charges, usually shortened to TC/RC, are part of the commercial terms for turning concentrate into refined metal. The full economics can also include recovery terms, by-products, sulphuric acid, energy, logistics and downstream products. The exact contract matters.

Hindalco's FY2025 fourth-quarter presentation transcript attributes weaker copper EBITDA in that quarter to lower TC/RC. It is a dated company explanation, not evidence of today's contract rates. [7]

I would therefore stress-test concentrate availability and processing terms separately from the copper benchmark. A higher metal price and a weaker smelting spread can coexist.

Port access, scale and by-product handling can be useful operating factors. None proves a permanently high return without checking feedstock contracts, plant costs, environmental obligations and capital employed.

9. Does the demand theme reach the shareholder?

Electrification, equipment, buildings and transport can create uses for aluminium and copper. But an end-use trend must pass through several gates before it produces per-share value: product demand, customer orders, commercial terms, operating execution, cash collection and funding.

A cable manufacturer is not a copper mine. Higher input prices can lift its sales without proportionate volume growth. A project supplier can face customer concentration, guarantees, milestone payments and slow collections. A producer adding capacity can face depreciation and financing costs before the ramp is complete.

Compare businesses using measures suited to their stage: mining cost and recoverable output, processing spread, conversion profitability, return on capital and cumulative cash. There is no single sector margin that all participants should converge toward.

Also separate customer qualification from orders. The capability to supply an attractive application may be necessary without guaranteeing utilisation or pricing power.

The research question should be specific enough to be disproved. What observed volume, spread or cash result would show that the demand theme is not translating into this company's economics?

10. What does a mineral option need before it becomes a business?

Rare-earth elements are a specific group: the lanthanides plus scandium and yttrium. The US Geological Survey's reference distinguishes that group. It is not a synonym for copper, lithium, every battery input or all critical minerals. [8]

A deposit announcement begins a chain of questions rather than ending one. The mineral mix, recoverability, separation process, environmental requirements, funding, product specification and buyers all matter.

GMDC's FY2025 annual report discusses rare-earth development and processing plans. Those plans are useful evidence of intended work, not proof of a fully commercial separated-product business or a particular profit margin. [9]

I would keep this optionality separate from the established operating business until the evidence supports bringing it into a forecast. A promising resource can remain economically uncertain for a long time.

11. Why check the existing business before valuing optionality?

For a resource company developing new minerals, the existing mines still have depletion, replacement, operating-cost and capital needs. An exciting future product does not replenish today's cash engine by itself.

For a lignite producer, research should include the life and permissions of each operating mine, the readiness of replacements, saleable output, realised prices, customer demand and development spending. Company-wide reserve totals cannot tell us when a specific mine stops producing or how quickly another replaces it.

Do not treat a planned regulated return as a guaranteed shareholder return either. Approved capital, operating availability, recovery rules, financing and implementation can sit between a tariff framework and distributable cash.

The same discipline applies outside mining. Maintenance spending and replacement capacity are different from growth spending. If the cash engine requires large reinvestment merely to stay level, the money available for a new theme is smaller than EBITDA suggests.

I would model the existing business on its own, then show the new option separately with its evidence gaps and funding needs. That keeps an uncertain future from hiding deterioration in the present.

12. Does a subsidiary listing automatically unlock value?

No. A listing, demerger and sale are different transactions. A proposed transaction is not a completed one, and a quoted subsidiary valuation is not automatically cash received by the parent.

Start with who sells shares and who receives the proceeds. New shares can fund the subsidiary while diluting existing ownership. A sale of existing shares can generate parent proceeds while reducing its retained stake. Mixed transactions need both calculations.

A sum-of-parts model should then account for retained ownership, segment debt and cash, corporate costs, other claims, tax or fees where applicable and the chosen treatment of any holding-company discount. Avoid counting subsidiary value and sale proceeds twice.

This article does not assert a current Novelis transaction status or a current valuation. The general lesson is that a subsidiary event must be translated into an explicit ownership-and-cash bridge before calling it value creation.

Capex funding deserves the same per-share discipline. A larger earnings pool can still produce modest growth per share after dilution, financing costs and a slower-than-planned ramp.

13. What belongs on the final research sheet?

For each material business, record its stage in the value chain, saleable unit, input formula, selling-price formula, customer mix, actual volumes and realised operating spread. Keep capacity, production and shipments in separate columns.

Then add inventory and receivable funding, sustaining capital, expansion spending, debt, contingent obligations and diluted shares. Reconcile segment results to the consolidated accounts and explain material adjustments.

The downside case should combine plausible weak conditions rather than changing one variable at a time: lower volumes, narrower conversion or processing spreads, higher power costs, expensive scrap, slow collection and a delayed project ramp.

The best starting question is not which metal has the strongest story. It is which part of the chain the company owns, what it retains after paying the other participants, and how much cash remains after keeping that business competitive.

Once that is clear, a demand theme becomes something we can test. Until then, the metal name is only a label.
