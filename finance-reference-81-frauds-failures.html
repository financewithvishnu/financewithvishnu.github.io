Chapter 81. Frauds and Failures: Forensic Case Studies

Chapter 81 of 85 · about 22 minutes

Accounting chapters taught you to read statements; this chapter shows what the statements looked like when they were lying. Five Indian failures and five global ones, and then the part that pays: the red flags that were visible in public documents before each fall, because almost every fraud announced itself, quietly, in numbers anyone could have read.

81.1What was the Harshad Mehta scam of 1992?

The scam that created modern Indian market regulation. The mechanism: banks were required to hold government securities, and the interbank market for them ran on bank receipts, paper promises Mehta learned to counterfeit and time-shift, diverting perhaps ₹4,000 crore of the banking system's money into his own accounts, where it became buy orders. His buying pushed the Sensex up more than four-fold in eighteen months, the rally itself was the fraud's exhaust, and when the paper trail surfaced, the market halved and the banking system's weak plumbing stood exposed. The lessons that still pay: a price rise funded by diverted money cannot outlive the diversion; settlement and custody systems are market infrastructure, not back-office trivia, the scam directly produced NSE, demat and screen-based trading; and when a single operator's buying is the story, the story is the risk. Every Indian investor today trades inside the house this fire built.

81.2What happened at Satyam, India’s Enron?

January 2009: the chairman of India's fourth-largest IT company confessed in a letter that over ₹7,000 crore of the balance sheet was fictitious, ₹5,040 crore of cash that did not exist, accrued interest on that imaginary cash, understated liabilities, overstated debtors. The forensic wonder is how visible it was in principle: a company reporting industry-leading margins while parking thousands of crores in low-yield deposits, profits that never became cash, a promoter family whose stake had dwindled to low single digits while it borrowed against every other asset, and an aborted attempt to buy a related-party construction business with the "cash". Each flag alone explains away; together they described the fraud before the confession. The aftermath teaches too: auditors, board and regulators all failed simultaneously, so the reader of accounts is the last line of defence; and the government-brokered sale to Tech Mahindra showed that even a frauded company can have real operations worth saving, price and value survive even when reported numbers do not.

81.3What were the NSEL, IL&FS, DHFL and Yes Bank credit failures?

A decade of Indian failures that were not equity frauds but credit rot, and they rhyme. NSEL (2013): a spot exchange running disguised forward financing, ₹5,600 crore unpaid, the instruments it claimed to back, warehoused commodities, barely existed. IL&FS (2018): an infrastructure financier with ₹91,000 crore of debt layered through hundreds of subsidiaries, funding thirty-year roads with ninety-day paper; when refinancing stopped, the holding structure itself became the scandal, nobody, including the board, could see the whole. DHFL (2019): a housing lender whose funds allegedly walked to promoter-linked entities while its fixed deposits were sold to retirees as safe. Yes Bank (2020): growth-at-any-cost lending to the weakest corporate names, and the ₹8,415 crore AT1 bond write-down that taught Indian investors the fine print of "perpetual" capital. The shared signature: borrowing short to lend long, complexity that defeats inspection, and credit ratings that trailed reality by years. For readers: treat NBFC and bank investments as credit analysis first, and remember that a fixed deposit with a high rate is a risk statement.

81.4What do Enron, WorldCom, Wirecard, Madoff and FTX have in common?

Five more ways to lie. Enron (2001): mark-to-model accounting and off-balance-sheet entities, revenue booked from projections, $63 billion bankruptcy; lesson, when the business cannot be explained, that is the finding. WorldCom (2002): the simplest fraud in the canon, $11 billion of ordinary expenses capitalised as assets, profits manufactured by a journal entry; lesson, compare capex and cash flow with peers, the mismatch was one ratio wide. Wirecard (2020): €1.9 billion of "trustee accounts" in Asia that did not exist, journalists attacked for years before regulators looked; lesson, third-party "cash" needs third-party proof. Madoff (2008): no trades at all, a Ponzi paying old investors with new money behind an impossible returns curve, small, smooth, never down; lesson, returns too consistent are the anomaly, and audited-by-nobody is not a detail. FTX (2022): customer deposits lent to the founder's own trading firm against a self-issued token; lesson, custody and segregation are the entire game in new asset classes, and charisma is not a control system.

81.5What are the common signatures of accounting fraud?

Lay the ten cases side by side and the same five flags keep appearing, which is why the rest of the book's accounting chapters were the training and this is the match.

One: profits without cash, operating cash flow persistently below reported profit, Satyam, Enron, WorldCom all sat here. Two: promoter behaviour, pledging, shrinking stakes, related-party ventures, the owners telling you with their own money what they think. Three: auditor and governance signals, small auditors for large books, resignations, qualified opinions, CFO churn. Four: related-party fog, subsidiaries and transactions structured to defeat consolidation. Five: performance too good for the industry, margins or growth no competitor can match, Madoff's smooth line included. No single flag convicts; two together demand investigation; three together are the investigation's answer. The discipline costs a few hours a year per holding and has saved more money than any stock-picking skill ever taught.

81.6Worked example: the flags, before and after

Case: what was checkable before Satyam, and the checklist it left

Reconstruct what a diligent reader could verify in 2008, before the confession. Cash versus profit: cumulative reported profits far exceeded cumulative operating cash flow, checkable in the cash-flow statement, flag one. Yield on cash: thousands of crores in deposits earning implausibly low interest, checkable from the interest-income line, flag two, and the one that specifically predicted fake cash. Promoter behaviour: the family's stake down to low single digits and pledged elsewhere, checkable in shareholding disclosures, flag three. Related parties: the December 2008 attempt to buy Maytas, the promoter's construction firm, with company cash, announced publicly, voted down by the market's own reaction, flag four in daylight. Governance: a Big-Four auditor signed everything, showing the limits of that defence. The transferable checklist: for every large holding, annually, reconcile profits to cash over five years; check interest earned against cash balances; track promoter pledging and stake trend; read the related-party schedule; note auditor changes. Five checks, one spreadsheet, and the investor who ran it owned none of Satyam, NSEL, DHFL or Yes Bank at the point of failure, not through brilliance, but through arithmetic.
