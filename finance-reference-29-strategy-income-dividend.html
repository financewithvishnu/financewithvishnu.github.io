Chapter 29. Stock Strategies: Income and Dividend Strategies

Chapter 29 of 85 · about 19 minutes

There comes a point, retirement, or simply the desire for a portfolio that pays you, when the question changes from "how much can this grow?" to "how much can this send home, reliably, without eating itself?". Income investing is that question answered with a system. Done well it is the calmest strategy in this book; done as a hunt for the highest yield on the screen, it is one of the most dangerous. The difference is the entire chapter.

29.1Dividend yield and dividend growth investing

Two strategies share the shelf. High yield buys stocks paying large dividends relative to price, 4-6% in India's usual candidates, utilities, PSUs, mature cyclicals at the right point of the cycle. It produces income now, and its risk is that the high yield is often a falling price divided by an about-to-be-cut dividend, the yield trap of section 29.4. Dividend growth buys companies paying modest yields, 1-3%, but raising the dividend every year as earnings compound. The second strategy's magic is slow and total: yield on your original cost climbs into double digits over fifteen years, and the income is inflation-linked by construction, because it is backed by pricing power. The first strategy feeds you today; the second feeds your seventies. Most sensible income portfolios hold both, in layers.

29.2Building an income ladder

The robust design is a ladder across instruments, each layer chosen for what it alone provides:

LayerInstrumentYield (indicative)Role
BaseDebt funds / FDs / RBI floating bonds6-7.5%Stability, the part that never surprises
MiddleREIT and InvIT distributions6-8%Contracted cash flows, some growth
TopDividend-growing equities1.5-3% + growthThe inflation fighter: income that rises

The logic is Chapter 3's risk grammar applied to income. The base layer guarantees the floor: expenses covered by cash flows that do not depend on markets. The middle layer adds yield from contracted, regulated cash flows, office rents, road tolls, with mild growth and the rate-sensitivity that comes with them. The top layer is the engine: a smaller current yield from companies whose dividends grow with earnings, so the income stream fights inflation over decades. Laddered this way, a bad year in any layer is an inconvenience, not a crisis, and the retiree is never a forced seller of equity in a crash, the single behaviour that destroys income plans.

29.3The dividend vs buyback choice, and its Indian tax angle

A company returning cash can pay a dividend or buy back shares; economically they are near-identical, a rupee out is a rupee out, but Indian tax law has made them different for the investor. Dividends are taxed at your slab rate, painful at 30%+ for high earners; buybacks are now taxed as a deemed dividend in the recipient's hands under the post-2024 rules, narrowing the old arbitrage; and simply not selling, letting the company retain and compound, defers all tax until you choose. The practical hierarchy for a high-bracket Indian investor: prefer companies that reinvest well (no tax event at all), then buybacks, then dividends. For someone in a low bracket, or a retiree needing the cash flow, dividends remain perfectly rational. The tax tail should never wag the investment dog: a poor business paying a fat dividend is still a poor business.

29.4Yield traps and unsustainable payouts

The screen's most dangerous output is its top row. A 9% yield is rarely a gift; it is usually a price that has fallen 40% (the market pricing a cut) divided by a dividend that has not been cut yet. The trap checklist: payout ratio near or above 100% means the dividend is borrowed from the future; earnings or cash flow falling while the payout holds means the cut is scheduled, not speculative; a cyclical at peak earnings paying "6%" is paying you out of a temporary windfall (Chapter 25's lesson); and a promoter-controlled company with high pledging paying a fat dividend may be paying the promoter's personal creditors, not you. The sustainable dividend is boring by construction: covered twice over by free cash flow, grown or held through a full cycle, and paid by a business whose Chapter 9 ratios pass. Yield is what you receive; coverage is what you can count on.

29.5Worked example: a retirement income portfolio

Case: ₹1.5 crore, needing ₹7.5 lakh a year, laddered

Composite design, indicative numbers. Base layer, ₹70 lakh in a mix of FDs, RBI floating-rate bonds and short-duration debt funds at a blended 6.75%: about ₹4.7 lakh a year, covering two-thirds of the need with near-zero market risk. Middle layer, ₹30 lakh in listed REITs and InvITs yielding 7%: about ₹2.1 lakh, completing the annual requirement with contracted cash flows. Top layer, ₹50 lakh in a basket of ten dividend-growing companies, current yield 2% (₹1 lakh), growing 10-12% a year: today it is the smallest contributor; in year ten it is the largest, and it is the layer that keeps the whole plan ahead of inflation. The retiree's rules: draw from the base in bad equity years, never sell the top layer into a crash, rebalance the layers annually, and review every dividend for coverage, not just arrival. The portfolio's real product is not the ₹7.5 lakh; it is the absence of forced decisions.
