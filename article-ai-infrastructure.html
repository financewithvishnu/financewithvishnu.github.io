AI infrastructure: who earns when the bottleneck moves?

By Finance With Vishnu · Class date: 8 February 2026 · Sector study

An AI service may appear as a small box on a screen, but behind it sits a physical system. Chips need memory. Machines need connections. Buildings need power and cooling. A weakness in one part can limit the output of the whole system.

That creates opportunities for suppliers well beyond the company selling the final AI application. It also creates a trap. We can draw a long list of necessary products and mistake that list for a portfolio of good businesses.

I would ask a narrower question: what is scarce, why is it scarce, and can this company earn attractive cash returns after customers and competitors respond? A temporary shortage and a durable competitive advantage are not the same thing.

Study window: the AI-infrastructure segment of the class dated 8 February 2026, excluding its broader moat workshop and unrelated company cases. Technical and business illustrations use primary product pages and FY2025 reports, including Besi and Vertiv reports published after the class. This is an explanatory study, not a reconstruction of information available on the class date or a current capacity, shortage or valuation update.

1. What does the AI-infrastructure chain include?

Start with the useful work: training or running a model. The software needs computing resources. Those resources sit within systems that store and move data, communicate with other systems and keep operating within physical limits.

NVIDIA's FY2025 annual material describes a platform spanning computing, networking and software. The IEA's April 2025 Energy and AI report explains the connection between data centres and electricity demand. Together, they give us a better starting map than treating AI as either a chip story or a software story alone. [1, 2]

The related wafer-fabrication equipment study covers how the chip factory is built. The software study covers who keeps productivity gains. Here I want to focus on the connections between physical constraints and business returns.

2. Why can the fastest processor still wait?

Computing speed is only useful when the system can supply data and coordinate the work. A processor can have impressive theoretical performance while another constraint limits the application's actual throughput.

Think of a hypothetical three-stage service. The compute stage can handle 100 units of work per hour, the data-feeding stage 80 and the connection stage 70. If every completed unit must pass through all three, the simple system cannot sustainably deliver more than 70 units per hour.

Increasing compute capacity from 100 to 120 changes the headline specification but not that 70-unit limit. Improving connections to 90 moves the constraint to the 80-unit data stage. This is an original teaching model, not a benchmark of any AI cluster.

For an investor, the lesson is to study system-level value. A component that removes an expensive constraint can matter more than its share of the bill of materials. That does not mean the supplier can keep all of the value it creates.

3. Why are memory capacity and bandwidth different?

Capacity describes how much information can be held. Bandwidth describes how quickly information can move. A system can need more of one without needing the same proportional increase in the other.

Micron's HBM3E product page discusses high-bandwidth memory in relation to data movement, computing utilisation, model training and inference. It describes stacked memory products designed for these uses. The useful point is the function, not a claim that one memory generation or supplier will permanently win. [3]

HBM is not a synonym for all memory or all storage. Nor should we assume that demand for one advanced product guarantees the same pricing or economics across an entire memory company.

I would separate the technical qualification from the commercial result. Does the product meet a customer's needs? Can the supplier manufacture it with acceptable yields? What capacity must it commit? How much of the selling price survives manufacturing costs, depreciation and the next technology transition?

A customer announcement can establish a design relationship. It does not automatically disclose the contract margin, the total lifetime volume or whether the relationship will carry over to the next platform.

4. What does advanced packaging add?

Packaging is not merely wrapping a finished chip in a protective shell. In advanced systems, the way devices are assembled and interconnected can affect how they work together.

Besi's 2025 annual report describes assembly equipment used for semiconductor packages and discusses technologies including hybrid bonding and thermo-compression bonding. It distinguishes wafer processing from assembly and also explains that newer device architectures can blur the old boundaries between those stages. [4]

The investment idea is not simply "put chips closer together and compute increases." The actual outcome depends on architecture, interconnects, thermal behaviour, production accuracy, throughput and yield. The manufacturing process has to work reliably at scale.

A packaging-equipment company sells a different product from the foundry or assembly provider that operates the equipment. The tool supplier's customer capex is not its own manufacturing output. We need to know which layer the company occupies before estimating its addressable market.

For a new process, I would track development, qualification, production orders and repeat use separately. An installed development tool can be important evidence without proving that a high-volume production programme has arrived.

5. Why do networking and optics matter?

Machines working together need to exchange data. Networking connects the computing resources, while optical components help carry information using light in relevant parts of the network.

Coherent's FY2025 annual report describes transceivers and other optical and optoelectronic components, modules and systems. Its datacom discussion connects products with AI and machine-learning applications. This establishes a genuine product route into the infrastructure chain, rather than a loose association with AI. [5]

Still, "optics" covers many products. Lasers, components, transceivers and complete networking systems are not interchangeable businesses. A company may be vertically integrated in some steps and dependent on outside suppliers in others.

Be careful with absolute statements that one platform uses only optical links or that copper disappears everywhere. Interconnect choices depend on the architecture and application. A transition can create new opportunities while also changing where the supplier earns its margin.

My questions would be product-specific: what does the company supply, at which network layer, to which customer set, and under what qualification and pricing conditions? Increasing data traffic is not enough to answer them.

6. Why are electricity and cooling separate constraints?

A data centre needs electrical supply to the site and infrastructure that distributes and supports power within it. Equipment also produces heat that must be managed. These needs are related, but solving one does not automatically solve the other.

The IEA's Energy and AI report models electricity demand and supply under different assumptions. A projection is not a guaranteed construction schedule or a purchase order for a particular equipment maker. Local grid conditions and project timing can differ substantially from the global picture. [2]

Vertiv's 2025 annual report describes power management, thermal management, air- and liquid-cooled products, integrated systems and lifecycle services. Its product scope illustrates the difference between owning electricity generation and supplying the infrastructure that supports digital equipment. [6]

Liquid cooling is not a single product or an automatic replacement cycle for every facility. A supplier must match the design, install and support it, and manage reliability and service obligations. A wider equipment opportunity can bring more engineering responsibility as well as more revenue.

For the economics, ask when the customer's site will be ready, when products can be delivered and accepted, and what happens if another project dependency slips.

7. Is a shortage the same as a moat?

A shortage means demand exceeds available supply under particular conditions. A moat is an advantage that helps a company protect attractive economics over time. A business can have one without the other.

When a component is scarce, customers may pay more, sign longer commitments or help fund expansion. Those responses can improve current economics. They can also encourage new capacity, alternative designs and additional suppliers.

A stronger advantage might come from accumulated process knowledge, reliable yields, integration with the customer, intellectual property or a service network. We should identify the exact mechanism and look for evidence that it survives the next product cycle.

The bottleneck can move. Better supply of one component may shift the limiting factor to another. A company that earned high returns during an exceptional shortage may face normal competition after capacity catches up.

I would therefore put "what ends the shortage?" next to "what creates it?" in the research notes. A thesis that has no answer to the first question is incomplete.

8. How can we overcount the opportunity?

The same investment can appear in several companies' stories. A cloud operator buys systems. A system vendor buys chips and networking parts. A chip supplier buys manufacturing and packaging capacity. A foundry buys tools.

Each transaction can be real revenue for someone, but adding every layer's sales does not tell us the final customer's total spending or the profit available to shareholders. It mixes different levels of the chain.

Consider a hypothetical ₹100 system purchase. If the system vendor pays ₹60 to component suppliers, that does not make final demand ₹160. It means ₹60 of the system vendor's costs becomes revenue further upstream. Both sets of businesses still have their own expenses and capital needs.

The same caution applies to orders described as AI-related. Some orders may serve multiple workloads. Some companies report a data-centre category but no separate AI revenue. Do not create a precise AI share of sales from a broad management comment.

For an Indian supplier, proximity to a global trend is only the start. Verify its exact component, capability, qualification, customer order and revenue contribution. Do not infer a major customer relationship from an industry diagram or a plausible product match.

9. What does a backlog really tell us?

A backlog can support visibility, but its definition matters. Does it contain firm customer orders, framework agreements or expected releases? How far into the future does it extend? Can orders be delayed, changed or cancelled?

Vertiv's 2025 report cautions that backlog estimates are not necessarily indicative of future net sales and identifies risks to realising expected sales. That is a useful reminder to read the definition and risk section together, rather than treating the headline as guaranteed revenue. [6]

Also separate orders from shipments and revenue. A system may need customer acceptance. A component can be shipped ahead of the final installation. A new platform transition can change the timing or composition of demand.

I would reconcile new orders, execution and the closing backlog where possible. Then I would compare reported revenue with inventory, receivables, customer advances and cash collection. Strong demand should eventually leave a trace in more than presentation language.

Customer concentration deserves attention too. A supplier can be important to a large customer and still have limited bargaining power when that customer redesigns a platform or negotiates the next contract.

10. Can faster growth reduce near-term cash?

Yes. A company may need inventory, equipment, development spending and supplier commitments before it collects from customers. Growth can be profitable while consuming cash.

NVIDIA's annual material describes reliance on manufacturing, assembly, packaging and testing partners. That shows why even a business without the same factory footprint as an integrated manufacturer still has supply-chain dependencies and commitments. A lighter owned-asset model does not mean no capital or execution risk. [1]

Use a hypothetical supplier with ₹100 of revenue and ₹20 of operating cash before a capacity investment. If it spends ₹30 on new equipment, cash after that investment is negative ₹10. The investment may be sensible, but the business needs funding. A good explanation should connect the spending to future output, customer demand and returns.

Depreciation, inventory provisions and research spending can also affect reported earnings differently from current cash payments. Compare the accounting and cash-flow statements rather than choosing whichever measure makes growth look best.

I would distinguish maintenance investment, capacity expansion and acquisitions. They solve different problems and should not be hidden inside one optimistic free-cash-flow adjustment.

11. What if efficiency improves faster than expected?

More efficient hardware and software can reduce the resources needed for a unit of useful work. That can make AI services cheaper, improve performance or reduce the cost of deployment.

It does not give a single answer for total infrastructure demand. Lower cost can encourage more use. It can also reduce the equipment needed for a fixed workload. The net result depends on how much demand expands, how customers price services and where the efficiency gain appears.

For a simple hypothetical example, suppose resource use per task falls by 25%. If the number of tasks rises by 20%, total resource use becomes 0.75 ×1.20 =0.90 of the starting level. If task volume doubles, it becomes 1.50. Both outcomes are compatible with a more efficient task.

The right response is scenario analysis, not certainty that efficiency either kills the infrastructure story or guarantees unlimited demand.

12. What could break the investment thesis?

Demand can disappoint if customers struggle to earn an acceptable return from AI services. Spending can shift between training and inference, between architectures or between regions. More of the budget can go to one layer while another slows.

Supply can also respond. New capacity, improved yields or alternative technology can reduce scarcity. A supplier may need another large investment just as the previous generation becomes less valuable.

Export rules, power availability, permitting, customer concentration and manufacturing dependencies add other risks. A single global narrative can hide very different local and product-level conditions.

Finally, expectations can be too high even when the business grows. A stock price that assumes smooth execution and years of exceptional margins leaves little room for normal delays or competition. The industry's importance does not remove valuation risk.

I would become more confident after seeing repeat production orders, sensible capacity additions, credible collections and returns that survive a product transition. I would become less confident when only the market-size estimate keeps rising.

13. How would I build the research file?

Choose one layer first. Learn what the product does, what the customer values and what can replace it. Then connect the technical role to the company's commercial disclosures.

Keep company claims, verified historical facts and your own scenarios in separate columns. A customer's expansion plan is useful context. It is not a substitute for the supplier's order.

AI infrastructure is a system of connected businesses. That is what makes it interesting, and what makes a simple basket of familiar names inadequate. The work is to follow the bottleneck without assuming that today's scarcity will become tomorrow's permanent profit.
