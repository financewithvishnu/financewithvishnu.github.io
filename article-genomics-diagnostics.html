Genomics and molecular diagnostics: follow the test, the evidence and the payer

By Finance With Vishnu · Source period: March 2021 · Company data through: September 2026

Reading biological information has become much easier than it once was. Turning that information into a useful test, a clinician's decision and collected revenue is a different task. A sequencing instrument can work well while the laboratory using it struggles to fill capacity. A test can generate interest while its payment pathway remains uncertain.

I would start this industry study with one question: what exactly is being sold? Instruments, reagents, analysis software, a laboratory service and an evidence-backed clinical test belong to the same ecosystem, but they do not earn money in the same way.

1. What are genomics and molecular diagnostics?

Genomics studies genomes and their biological meaning. DNA sequencing determines the order of bases in a DNA sample. That is a measurement process, not the same thing as diagnosing a disease, editing a gene or developing a treatment. The business must be classified by what it actually delivers.

Molecular diagnostics uses molecular information to answer a defined clinical question. A test might help establish a diagnosis, estimate risk or guide treatment. The National Human Genome Research Institute distinguishes diagnostic, predictive, carrier, pharmacogenomic and research testing, among other uses. These are not interchangeable markets. [1]

Nor is every test a whole-genome sequence. Illumina's FY2025 filing describes sequencing applications ranging from targeted regions to whole genomes, alongside arrays and other workflows. Veracyte's filing describes molecular tests and approaches involving gene-expression information. Begin with the biological question and assay, rather than assuming that more data always means a better product. [2] [3]

2. What happens between a sample and a report?

A simplified workflow includes obtaining an appropriate sample, preparing it, running the assay, processing the data, interpreting the result and delivering a report. Quality checks belong across the workflow, not only at the end. The report then needs to reach a clinician or other intended user in time to be useful.

The bottleneck can sit outside the instrument. Sample transport, insufficient material, repeat runs, interpretation capacity and billing can all affect the economics. Veracyte's FY2025 filing identifies laboratory turnaround time, failure rates and deviations from controls as operating measures. This connects laboratory execution to the business rather than treating scientific measurement as the only task. [3]

A research service may be paid to produce data under a project contract. A clinical testing business must also support a defined use in patient care and its applicable requirements. Understanding which workflow is being sold is necessary before comparing revenue per sample, gross margins or growth rates.

3. Is the cost of sequencing the cost of a clinical test?

No. A quoted sequencing cost needs a precise boundary. Does it include sample preparation, equipment, labour, data analysis, interpretation, repeats and reporting? What coverage, quality and utilisation assumptions were used? A low number without those conditions can mislead.

NHGRI's sequencing-cost series explicitly distinguishes included production costs from excluded activities. Its exclusions include downstream analysis such as identifying variants and interpreting results, along with several other non-production activities. The per-genome series is an estimate for a human-sized genome with specified assumptions, not a universal bill for a complete clinical service. [4]

Comparing the cost of a large historical research programme with the marginal price of one sequencing run is also not a like-for-like productivity calculation. The direction of technical progress can be important without requiring an exaggerated ratio.

For a company model, build the entire cost per usable report. Falling sequencing cost may expand demand, reduce customers' spending or intensify competition. Which effect dominates depends on volume, pricing and who owns the rest of the workflow.

4. How do instrument and consumable businesses work?

An instrument sale places a platform in a laboratory. Continued use can create demand for reagents, flow cells, sample preparation and services. That makes the active installed base and its usage important, not merely the number of machines shipped.

Illumina's FY2025 Form 10-K reports that consumables represented 74% of consolidated revenue, instruments 11%, and services and other 15%. This is a dated company-specific mix illustrating the model, not the standard revenue split of the genomics industry. The same filing describes intense competition across platforms and alternative approaches to biological questions. [2]

A later dated check: Illumina's Q3 2026 results, for the quarter ended September 2026, reported revenue of $1.13 billion, up 4.2% year on year, with management raising full-year guidance on clinical demand and NovaSeq X placements. One quarter is a data point, not a run-rate guarantee. [9]

A useful model is active instruments multiplied by activity per instrument and consumable revenue per unit of activity. Adjust for platform and application mix. More throughput does not automatically mean proportionately more revenue if price per unit falls. Laboratories may also postpone upgrades, consolidate work or shift spending.

Recurring consumables are attractive only if customers keep using the system and the supplier remains competitive. Installed is not the same as active, and technical compatibility is not a guarantee of permanent pricing power.

5. How does a testing company differ from a tools supplier?

A central laboratory earns from tests it performs. A kit supplier may sell to hospitals and other laboratories that perform tests locally. The customer, operating cost, distribution requirement and collection risk differ, even when the underlying clinical question is similar.

Veracyte's FY2025 filing provides a concrete example: it describes centralised US laboratory testing and an international IVD model involving distribution to laboratories and hospitals. Its reimbursement section separately explains direct payer billing for testing and billing laboratory customers for kits. Treat this as the company's reported structure for that period, not a claim about every product or country today. [3]

A later dated check: Veracyte's Q3 2026 results, for the quarter ended September 2026, reported revenue of $151.8 million, up 15% year on year, with Decipher Prostate revenue up 12% and Afirma up 19%, alongside a new $100 million share repurchase programme. Quarterly growth is not proof of durable reimbursement. [10]

For a central lab, examine processed volume, reportable volume, realised revenue per test and cost per report. For a kit business, examine customer adoption, kit usage, inventory, distribution and the customer's own reimbursement conditions. Do not compare the two as though each rupee of revenue carries the same costs.

6. What does the evidence need to prove?

A test needs more than a compelling technology demonstration. The archived CDC ACCE framework separates analytic validity, clinical validity, clinical utility, and ethical, legal and social implications. It is a useful structure for questions, not a current product approval rule. [5]

In plain business terms: can the assay measure reliably; does its result relate to the intended clinical condition; and does using it provide useful support for care in the intended setting? Evidence for one question does not automatically settle the others.

A study in one population, disease stage or sample type may not support every proposed use. Check the study design, comparison, intended population and limitations. Ask whether results were reproduced and whether later evidence changes the interpretation. A company presentation about potential use is not the same as evidence accepted by clinicians and payers.

Veracyte explicitly connects evidence generation, guideline inclusion, clinical utility requirements and reimbursement in its filing. That is a description of its commercial process, not independent proof that every test improves every patient's outcome. [3]

7. Why is the payer part of the product?

The person ordering a test, the patient receiving it and the organisation paying may be different. A test's list price therefore tells us little about the revenue the company can reliably collect. Covered indications, negotiated rates, documentation and claim handling matter.

CMS National Coverage Determination 90.2 illustrates the specificity. It addresses NGS diagnostic testing for somatic and germline cancer, with conditions involving the patient, test, laboratory and treating physician. It also describes circumstances for decisions by Medicare Administrative Contractors. It is not blanket coverage for all sequencing or a rule applying to India or every US insurer. [6]

Veracyte likewise discusses payer contracts, covered indications, billing rules, denials, audits and possible repayments. A business model should therefore separate ordered tests, performed tests, recognised revenue and cash received. They are connected, but they do not necessarily move together in a quarter. [3]

8. What can a simple laboratory model show?

Assume a hypothetical lab receives 1,000 samples. It produces a report from 90%, or 900 samples. Suppose the average eventual net collection per report is 100 monetary units, giving 90,000 in collections. Processing cost is 40 per received sample, or 40,000, and fixed operating cost is 30,000.

The remaining operating surplus is 20,000 before depreciation, research, financing, tax and capital expenditure. If average collection falls to 80 per report, collections become 72,000 and the surplus falls to 2,000 with the same costs. Nothing changed about sample volume or the instrument's technical performance.

Now add the time required to collect. A profitable-looking test can still consume working capital while claims remain unresolved. Growth funded by rising receivables deserves a different assessment from growth that brings cash with it.

9. Does more genomic information always remove uncertainty?

No. NHGRI explains that a positive genetic result does not always mean someone will develop a disease. Interpretation can be difficult and can have implications for relatives. This is one reason specialised clinical interpretation matters; the raw data are not a diagnosis by themselves. [1]

A variant of uncertain significance is a finding whose relationship to a health condition is unclear. NHGRI notes that more population information, functional work or family evidence may be needed. Its explanation also links unequal representation in genomic information with differences in interpretability across populations. [7]

For business diligence, ask whether evidence represents the population the company wants to serve. Do not turn a larger dataset into an automatic claim of better clinical decisions. The quality of labels, relevance of the cohort, validation and permitted use all matter. A database's commercial promise must remain separate from claims about an individual patient's health.

10. Which regulatory shortcuts should an analyst avoid?

Keep research-use, investigational and clinical products distinct. FDA's guidance specifically addresses when IVD products are properly labelled for research use only or investigational use only. Illumina's filing also identifies certain interpretation products as research-use-only and not intended for diagnostic procedures. Do not silently upgrade a research capability into an authorised clinical test. [8] [2]

A company's laboratory credentials, a product authorisation and payer coverage answer different questions. One does not replace the others. The relevant requirements depend on the product, setting and jurisdiction, and can change. This article is not an exhaustive legal map of laboratory-developed tests or IVDs.

Apply the same discipline to adjacent science. Reading genomic information is different from editing cells, manufacturing a gene therapy or developing printed tissues. Those activities have separate evidence, delivery and regulatory problems. A successful diagnostic workflow is not proof that a related treatment programme will succeed.

11. Where might a durable advantage come from?

Possible advantages include a useful workflow, dependable performance, relevant evidence, customer integration, interpretation capability, an efficient laboratory network and payer access. The emphasis differs by business model. A tools company and a clinical-test company should not receive the same moat checklist.

Installed platforms and trained customers can support repeat usage, but competition can change the economics. Test evidence can support adoption, but later research can challenge it. A commercial channel can accelerate a new product, but does not remove the need to establish its use and payment pathway.

For a data-based thesis, inspect lawful access, consent scope, permitted secondary use and security responsibilities rather than assuming every stored sample can be monetised. Treat those as diligence questions to answer, not rights the analyst can infer from a database size. Unclear rights weaken the business case even before assigning a value to the data.

12. How should the financial model follow the science?

Separate instrument placements, consumable activity, service revenue and clinical tests where reporting allows. For laboratories, bridge volume growth to net realised price, reportable yield, labour and reagent costs. Then follow receivables, inventory, investment and research spending to cash.

Watch concentration in a platform, payer, test or customer group. Growth from one successful assay does not prove a repeatable product-development engine. A large pipeline can create future opportunities while also requiring years of evidence spending and commercial investment.

Compare prior milestones with actual delivery. Were launches delayed, evidence requirements larger, collections slower or margins lower than assumed? Do not justify every miss with the size of the eventual market. Market size, accessible population, test adoption and the company's share are different inputs.

13. What belongs on the first research page?

  • Name the assay, intended use, customer and payer.
  • Identify whether revenue comes from tools, consumables, software, testing or kits.
  • Define sequencing cost separately from full cost per usable report.
  • Check analytic and clinical evidence without extending it to unstudied uses.
  • Read the actual product-use and jurisdiction boundaries.
  • Separate performed volume, recognised revenue and collected cash.
  • Stress utilisation, pricing, reportable yield and reimbursement.
  • Review population relevance, data rights, concentration and investment needs.

Cheaper biological data can open valuable markets. The investable question is who converts those data into useful work that customers pay for, and at what return on the capital and evidence required. Follow the test, the evidence and the payer before following the story.
